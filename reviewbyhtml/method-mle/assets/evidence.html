This leads us to introduce an extension of the Weibull distribution to the extreme-value distribution family, i.e. the generalized extreme-value distribution (GEV).

The adoption of the GEV has other benefits. One is that the maximum likelihood parameter estimation in the GEV is easier than in the W3P, which will be discussed in Section 5. References [28] and [5] are relevant to the idea of the parameterization of the GEV model from the W3P model; however, they do not show that using the GEV is effective in estimating the Weibull parameters.

## 7.1. MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD PARAMETER ESTIMATION IN THE GEV MODEL

The main idea of the proposed algorithm lies on a assumption that $\widehat{\sigma}(k)$ and $\widehat{\mu}(k)$ do not vary as much as k does; $\widehat{\sigma}(k)$ and $\widehat{\mu}(k)$ denote the MLE for the GEV distribution when k is fixed. Moreover, when $k \to 0$ , which corresponds to the Gumbel distribution, we can easily estimate the MLE of parameters in the Gumbel distribution by using the Newton-Raphson method. Since the MLE of parameters when using the Newton-Raphson method are very sensitive in general with respect to the initial guesses, the following method is one of the recommended methods for a stable and successful searching scheme of the MLE.

Step 1: Obtain the sample's mean and standard deviation as follows

$$
m = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} x _ {i}\tag{11}
$$

$$
s ^ {2} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - m) ^ {2}\tag{12}
$$

where n denotes the number of samples. Set initial guesses, $\sigma_{0}^{(0)}$ and $\mu_{0}^{(0)}$ , for the MLE of $\widehat{\mu}(G2P)$ and $\widehat{\sigma}(G2P)$ as

$$
\sigma_ {0} ^ {(0)} = \sqrt {\frac {6}{\pi}} s = 0. 7 7 9 6 9 7 s\tag{13}
$$

$$
\mu_ {0} ^ {(0)} = m + 0. 5 7 7 2 1 6 \sigma\tag{14}
$$

Obtain the MLE of G2P using $\sigma_0^{(0)}$ and $\mu_0^{(0)}$ . Set $\sigma_0 = \widehat{\sigma} (\mathrm{G2P})$ , $\mu_0 = \widehat{\mu} (\mathrm{G2P})$ , and $k_{0} = 0$ .

Step 2: Define a small number $\Delta k > 0$ , say $\Delta k = 0.01$ . Set $k_{1} = \Delta k$ , and $k_{-1} = -\Delta k$ . Obtain the MLE of the 2-parameter GEV distribution under the constraint of $k = k_{1}$ , by using $\sigma_0$ and $\mu_0$ as initial guesses. Set these MLE as $\sigma_1$ and $\mu_1$ . Obtain $\sigma_{-1}$ and $\mu_{-1}$ corresponding to $k_{-1}$ in a very similar manner.

Step 3: By using the three points, $(\sigma_{-1}, k_{-1})$ , $(\sigma_0, k_0)$ , and $(\sigma_1, k_1)$ , obtain an extrapolated point $(\sigma_2^{(0)}, k_2)$ by solving a quadratic equation, where $k_2 = k_1 + \Delta k$ (Figure 7). Obtain $(\mu_2^{(0)}, k_2)$ in a similar manner. By using $(\sigma_2^{(0)}, \mu_2^{(0)})$ as initial guesses, obtain the MLE, $\sigma_2$ and $\mu_2$ of the 2-parameter GEV distribution under the constraint of $k = k_2$ . Assuming that $(\sigma_{\nu-2}, \mu_{\nu-2})$ , $(\sigma_{\nu-1}, \mu_{\nu-1})$ and $(\sigma_{\nu}, \mu_{\nu})$ have already been obtained by the previous steps, then $\sigma_{\nu+1}$ and $\mu_{\nu+1}$ for $k_{\nu+1} = k_{\nu} + \Delta k$ are obtained by the Newton-Raphson method using $\sigma_{\nu+1}^{(0)}$ and $\mu_{\nu+1}^{(0)}$ as the initial values (Figure 7). $\sigma_{\nu+1}^{(0)}$ is an extrapolated value from $(\sigma_{\nu-2}, k_{\nu-2})$ , $(\sigma_{\nu-1}, k_{\nu-1})$ and $(\sigma_{\nu}, k_{\nu})$ ; $\mu_{\nu+1}^{(0)}$ is similarly obtained. If the constraint, $\sigma_{\nu+1}^{(j)} > 0$ or $1 + k_{\nu+1}^{(j)} \{(x_i - \mu_{\nu+1}^{(j)}) / \sigma_{\nu+1}^{(j)})\} > 0$ , is violated in the Newton-Raphson iteration, we use an alternative extrapolated point which is closer to the point $(\sigma_{\nu}, \mu_{\nu}, k_{\nu})$ ; $j$ denotes the Newton-Raphson iteration count for the point $(k_{\nu+1}, \sigma_{\nu+1}, \mu_{\nu+1})$ . If $\Delta k$ is small, then convergence would be confirmed. This continues until $k = 1 - \Delta k$ . Special treatment is needed at the corner, $k = 1$ , as Rockette et al. have pointed out [26].

Step 4: A similar process to Step 3 is taken in the opposite direction from $k = k_{-2}$ to $k = k^*$ , where usually $k^* = -1$ .

Step 5: Search for a maximum point of $\log L(\sigma(k_{\nu}), \mu(k_{\nu}), k_{\nu})$ from all the knots which we are investigating. If $k_{\nu} \neq 1$ and $k_{\nu} \neq k^{*}$ , then do the 3-parameter Newton-Raphson iteration using the initial values $\sigma_{\nu}, \mu_{\nu}$ and $k_{\nu}$ (if $\nu = 0$ , use the next point $(\sigma_1, \mu_1, k_1)$ ). The converged variables may be the (local) MLE. We must check if this point is not a saddle point by using the determinants of the submatrices formed by the observed information matrix (Appendix 2); the log-likelihood at this point should also be checked if it is larger than the log-likelihood at the corner points, $k = 1$ and $k = k^{*}$ . If $\log L(\sigma(k = 1), \mu(k = 1), 1)$ is still maximum, the MLE of the Weibull parameters are $\widehat{\gamma} = x_{\min}$ , $\widehat{\beta} = 1$ and $\widehat{\eta} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - x_{\min})$ under the constraint that $k \leqslant 1$ ; $x_{\min}$ is the minimum observation in the experiment. If $\log L(\sigma(k^*)$ , $\mu(k^*)$ , $k^*)$ is maximum, $k^*$ should be set much smaller.
