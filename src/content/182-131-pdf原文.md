# Corrected Maximum Likelihood in Non-Regular Problems

By R. C. H. CHENG† and T. C. ILES

UWIST, Wales

[Received May 1986. Revised October 1986]

## SUMMARY

Maximum likelihood (ML) estimation can break down for distributions like the three-parameter Weibull or gamma where one of the parameters is a limit of the range of the distribution. It is suggested that use of the product of densities for the likelihood function is a misapplication of the ML method in such non-regular cases, and that this is the reason for its failure. It is shown that ML works quite satisfactorily if the likelihood is corrected in an appropriate way.

Keywords: MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD; NON-REGULAR ESTIMATION; WEIBULL DISTRIBUTION; GAMMA DISTRIBUTION; SPACINGS

## 1. INTRODUCTION

It is well known that the method of maximum likelihood (ML) does not always give satisfactory estimates of parameters for distributions in which one or more of the parameters corresponds to a limit on the range of the distribution. Typical examples are the three-parameter Weibull, gamma and lognormal distributions where, in each case, the density is positive only to the right of an unknown shifted origin (or threshold), $\alpha$ . The usually suggested ML procedure is to maximize the likelihood, with this defined as the product of the densities evaluated at the observations, but treated as a function of the parameters. The problem is that the likelihood can then be made to tend to infinity as $\alpha$ tends to the smallest observation, $x_{1}$ say. The principle of ML then requires the choice $\hat{\alpha}=x_{1}$ ; but this leads to inconsistent ML estimates of the other parameters. Our main contention is that the use of the product of densities for the likelihood in such problems is a misapplication of the ML method, and that this is the reason for its failure. It is shown that ML works satisfactorily if the likelihood is corrected in an appropriate way.

Various methods have previously been advanced to get round the problem of the unbounded likelihood. Three suggestions of interest are as follows.

(A) Group the observations, thereby reducing the problem to that of estimation in a multinomial distribution. This method has been discussed by Barnard (1967) and also by Giesbrecht and Kempthorne (1976).

(B) Maximize a certain product of spacings (MPS) rather than a product of densities. This method has been suggested by Cheng and Amin (1983), and independently by Ranneby (1984).

(C) Use (in certain situations) $x_{1}$ as the estimate of $\alpha$ and then use ML to estimate the other parameters from the remaining observations. This method was suggested by Harter and Moore (1966) and has been rigorously studied by Smith (1985).

All three methods successfully overcome the problem. A comparison of methods A and B with our proposed correction to the likelihood gives an indication of why they should be successful. Method C is shown to be a special case of the corrected method.

The correction also throws light on the interpretation by Titterington (1985) of the MPS method as grouped data. Titterington mentions an alternative choice of “cell” groupings to that used in the MPS method, but conjectures that this alternative can lead to an unnatural estimate of the threshold parameter. The corrected likelihood interpretation suggests how this inadmissible estimate can be removed so that the alternative choice of grouping becomes viable.

## 2. THE CORRECTED LIKELIHOOD

Let $x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{n}$ be a random sample of a random variable X with p.d.f. $f(x, \theta)$ and c.d.f. $F(x, \theta)$ , where $\theta$ is a vector of unknown parameters. With no loss of generality the sample is assumed to be ordered with $x_{1}$ the smallest observation.

We take the viewpoint that the likelihood is the product of probability increments which, for a continuous distribution, can be taken in the form

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{lik} & = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \operatorname * {P r} \left\{x _ {i} \leqslant X \leqslant x _ {i} + h _ {i} | \theta \right\} \\ & = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \int_ {x _ {i}} ^ {x _ {i} + h _ {i}} f (x, \theta) d x \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where the $h_{i}$ are positive quantities which are assumed to be small in a manner to be made precise. The method of ML then requires $\theta$ to be obtained by maximizing (1). Underpinning the method is the idea that the quantity being maximized is associated with a probability.

For problems where usual regularity conditions apply, two simplifications can be made to (1). First, the probability increments are replaced by the first order approximations

$$
\int_ {x _ {i}} ^ {x _ {i} + h _ {i}} f (x, \theta) d x \simeq f (x _ {i}, \theta) h _ {i} (i = 1, 2, \dots , n).\tag{2}
$$

Secondly, since the $h_i$ are independent of $\theta$ , they can be made to tend to zero so that (2) becomes increasingly more accurate and (1) can then be replaced by

$$
L = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (x _ {i}, \theta),\tag{3}
$$

and this is customarily taken as the definition of the likelihood.

Our contention is that for problems where the usual regularity conditions do not apply, then either or both steps may not be valid. Our claim is that ML can always be used with (1) provided the $h_{i}$ are chosen appropriately.

Related suggested modifications have been mentioned by Matthews and Farewell (1985) for a change-point model and by Smith (1986) for a nonlinear time series model, though the precise properties of these adjustments is not discussed. It is likely that the approach of this paper could be used in these cases, though we have not as yet looked into this possibility.

We shall indicate more general results later, but we concentrate initially on the example of the three-parameter Weibull distribution. The behaviour of the estimates in this distribution typifies the various situations that can arise more generally.

The Weibull density can be written as:

$$
f _ {W} (x, \theta) = \beta \gamma^ {- \beta} (x - \alpha) ^ {\beta - 1} \exp [ - \{(x - \alpha) / \gamma \} ^ {\beta} ], x > \alpha .\tag{4}
$$

The log-likelihood corresponding to the product of densities (3) reduces to

$$
l _ {W} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} [ \log \beta - \beta \log \gamma + (\beta - 1) \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) - \{(x _ {i} - \alpha) / \gamma \} ^ {\beta} ].\tag{5}
$$

It is known, see Cheng and Amin (1983) or Smith (1985), that the distribution of the ML estimate, $\hat{\theta} = (\hat{\alpha}, \hat{\beta}, \hat{\gamma})'$ , depends on the shape parameter $\beta$ . Its properties can be summarized as follows.

Result 1

(i) If $\beta > 2$ then there is in probability a solution $\hat{\theta}$ of $\partial l_W / \partial \theta = 0$ that is asymptotically normal

with

$$
\sqrt {n} (\hat {\theta} - \theta) \stackrel {L} {\rightarrow} N [ 0, - n E (\partial^ {2} l _ {W} / \partial \theta^ {2}) ^ {- 1} ].\tag{6}
$$

(ii) If $1 < \beta < 2$ then there is, in probability, a solution where

$$
\hat {\alpha} - \alpha = O _ {p} (n ^ {- 1 / \beta})\tag{7}
$$

and where $(\hat{\beta},\hat{\gamma})^{\prime} = \hat{\phi}$ , say, is asymptotically normal with

$$
\sqrt {n} (\hat {\phi} - \phi) \stackrel {{L}} {{\rightarrow}} N [ 0, - n E (\partial^ {2} l _ {W} / \partial \phi^ {2}) ^ {- 1} ].\tag{8}
$$

(iii) If $\beta < 1$ , there is no consistent solution of $\partial l_{w} / \partial \theta = 0$ .

The result is as stated in Cheng and Amin (1983). Smith (1985) gives rather fuller details (covering, for instance, the special cases $\beta = 1$ and $\beta = 2$ ), but the above is sufficient for our purpose.

The difficulty with the case $\beta < 1$ arises in the following way. For any $\beta < 1$ , $l_{W}$ as given in (5) is an increasing function of $\alpha$ . Thus $l_{W}$ is maximized at $\alpha = x_{1}$ . When $\alpha \to x_{1}$ , however, the probability increment with $x_{1}$ is

$$
\int_ {x _ {1}} ^ {x _ {1} + h _ {1}} f _ {W} (x, \theta) d x = 1 - \exp \{- (h _ {1} / \gamma) ^ {\beta} \} = (h _ {1} / \gamma) ^ {\beta} \{1 + o (h _ {1}) \}.\tag{9}
$$

In contrast, $f_{W}(x_{1},\theta)$ in the approximation (2) tends to $\infty$ as $\alpha \to x_1$ , so that (2) is not a valid representation of (9) in this situation.

A second point arises. If the integral $\int_{x_1}^{x_1 + h_1} f_W$ is retained in (1), in place of (2), this does not then allow the dependence on $h_1$ to be factored out. This means that $h_1$ cannot tend to zero in a meaningful way.

These difficulties can be got round by using a corrected form of likelihood as follows.

Result 2

Consider the log-likelihood $\lambda_W(h)$ defined by

$$
\lambda_ {W} (h) = \log \int_ {x _ {1}} ^ {x _ {1} + h} f _ {W} (x, \theta) d x + \sum_ {i = 2} ^ {n} \log f _ {W} (x _ {i}, \theta),\tag{10}
$$

so that $l_{W}$ and $\lambda_W(h)$ differ only in the first term, and let $\widetilde{\theta}(h) = (\widetilde{\alpha}(h), \widetilde{\beta}(h), \widetilde{\gamma}(h))^{\prime}$ maximize (10) subject to $\alpha \leqslant x_1$ . Then $\widetilde{\theta}(h)$ can be found such that the following hold.

(i) If $\beta > 1$ , $\widetilde{\theta}(h)$ has, in probability, the same asymptotic distribution as $\hat{\theta}$ , for any $h > 0$ . Moreover

$$
\lim _ {h \to 0} \tilde {\theta} (h) = \hat {\theta}, \text {   in   probability.   }\tag{11}
$$

(ii) If $\beta < 1$ then, $\tilde{\alpha}(h) = x_1$ , and it is superefficient in the sense that

$$
| \tilde {\alpha} (h) - \alpha | = O _ {p} (n ^ {- 1 / \beta}).\tag{12}
$$

Moreover, provided

$$
h \geqslant O _ {p} (n ^ {- 1 / \beta}),\tag{13}
$$

then $\tilde{\phi}(h) = (\tilde{\beta}(h), \tilde{\gamma}(h))^{\prime}$ has the same asymptotic distribution as $\hat{\phi}$ when $\alpha$ is assumed known. Finally,

$$
\lim _ {h \to \infty} \tilde {\theta} (h) = \hat {\theta} _ {1} \equiv (x _ {1}, \hat {\phi} _ {1}),\tag{14}
$$

where $\hat{\phi}_1$ is the $ML$ estimate of $(\beta, \gamma)'$ obtained by setting $\alpha = x_1$ and maximizing the likelihood using the sample $x_2, x_3, \ldots, x_n$ (where $x_1$ has been omitted).

An outline of the proof is given in the Appendix. The method is the same as that used by Cheng and Amin (1982) to obtain Result 1. A similar result has been given by Cheng and Amin (1981) for the case of the inverse Gaussian distribution.

The result allows our suggested method to be easily implemented in various ways in practice. Asymptotically, the only real restriction is that h be “sufficiently large” if $\beta < 1$ . However, in small samples, it is necessary to ensure that, even if $\beta > 1$ , estimation does not break down because sampling error points to a value of $\beta < 1$ . One possibility is to try a range of h values. If $\widetilde{\beta}(h)$ remains greater than 1, then the estimate corresponding to $h \to 0$ is taken. If $\widetilde{\beta}(h)$ does not remain greater than 1, the estimate corresponding to $h \to \infty$ is taken. This latter case has an interesting interpretation. It is equivalent to maximizing the usual likelihood, but with the sample taken to be $x_{2}, \ldots, x_{n}$ (where $x_{1}$ is censored), using $x_{1}$ as a bound to restrict $\alpha$ to $\alpha \leqslant x_{1}$ . This technique of censored estimation has been suggested by Harter and Moore (1966) and has been studied rigorously by Smith (1985).

A simpler possibility is to use a fixed value of h always. Result 2 shows that the choice $h = \infty$ yields, irrespective of the true value of $\beta$ , asymptotically efficient estimates of $\beta$ and $\gamma$ and an estimate of $\alpha$ that is consistent to the order of n given in the result. However for small sample size some information is lost in not using $x_{1}$ explicitly. A better procedure would perhaps be to use $h = x_{2} - x_{1}$ (which satisfies inequality (13)), or, in the case of a repeated smallest observation, the first non-zero difference between consecutive ordered observations.

The authors have tried all the above methods on a variety of numerical examples and have encountered no problems in their application even for the “difficult” case where $\beta < 1$ . It is hoped to describe the more practical details of implementation elsewhere.

## 3. DISCUSSION

The viewpoint taken in the previous section can be extended to interpret the approaches of Barnard (1967) and of Giesbrecht and Kempthorne (1976) and Cheng and Amin (1983) mentioned in the Introduction.

Barnard (1967) and Giesbrecht and Kempthorne (1976) suggest that all observations are in practice discrete and that a continuous model is, therefore, only an approximation. If the observations are treated as grouped, the model becomes a multinomial one with cell probabilities dependent on the unknown parameters, and these can then be successfully estimated by ML. This argument does not, however, appear to identify the real issue. Whether it is possible in practice or not, it is still legitimate to suppose that the observations are truly continuously distributed and that it is then the discretization which is the approximation. Our viewpoint is that the success of the grouping approach lies in the integration step used to obtain the cell probabilities. This avoids the rectangle approximation of equation (2) and yields proper probabilities so that the multinomial likelihood is a “proper” one always.

The Cheng and Amin MPS method can be viewed in a similar way. The quantity

$$
H = (n + 1) ^ {- 1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n + 1} \log D _ {i} (\theta),\tag{15}
$$

where

$$
D _ {i} (\theta) = \int_ {x _ {i - 1}} ^ {x _ {i}} f (x, \theta) d x \text { with } x _ {0} = \alpha , x _ {n + 1} = \infty ,\tag{16}
$$

is maximized rather than the likelihood. Again probabilities are evaluated as proper integrals, and no approximation of the type (2) is introduced.

Titterington (1985) mentions an alternative method of defining H using

$$
Z _ {0} = \alpha , \quad Z _ {i} = (x _ {i} + x _ {i + 1}) / 2, \quad (i = 1, \dots , n - 1), Z _ {n} = \infty\tag{17}
$$

as cell boundaries instead of the $x_{i}$ . The problem here, as Titterington points out, is that this does not automatically restrict $\alpha$ to being less than $x_{1}$ . However, the issue of inconsistency no more arises in this case than for MPS, because again probabilities are evaluated as proper integrals. Thus maximizing the modified H subject to $\alpha \leqslant x_{1}$ should be a viable alternative, though we have yet to investigate details.

The discussion of the previous section focussed on the three-parameter Weibull, but is easily extended to other cases. The gamma distribution with pdf

$$
f _ {G} (x, \theta) = \left\{\gamma^ {\beta} \Gamma (\beta) \right\} ^ {- 1} (x - \alpha) ^ {\beta - 1} \exp \{- (x - \alpha) / \gamma \}, x > \alpha ,\tag{18}
$$

has parameters $\alpha$ , $\beta$ , $\gamma$ with the same geometric interpretation as those in the Weibull and Results 1 and 2 are unchanged for the gamma case except for the change of density function. The four-parameter beta distribution, where one or both end points are unknown, can be handled in like manner if integral terms are used to replace the densities corresponding to the smallest or/and largest observations.

Distributions like the three parameter lognormal, where the “degree of contact” of the density function with the x-axis is much higher, do not in practice cause the same difficulties. The ML estimates are consistent and have the expected asymptotic properties in this case. However, the likelihood can become unbounded as the shifted origin $\alpha \rightarrow x_{1}$ , and it can be that a satisfactory ML estimate is not obtainable for a particular sample. Use of the corrected likelihood might then be worthwhile to guarantee that satisfactory estimates are always obtained.
