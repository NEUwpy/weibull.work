# Nearly unbiased estimators for the three-parameter Weibull distribution with greater efficiency than the iterative likelihood method

Denis Cousineau*

Département de Psychologie, Université de Montréal, Montréal, Québec, Canada

The maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) method is the most commonly used method to estimate the parameters of the three-parameter Weibull distribution. However, it returns biased estimates. In this paper, we show how to calculate weights which cancel the biases contained in the MLE equations. The exact weights can be computed when the population parameters are known and the expected weights when they are not. Two of the three weights' expected values are dependent only on the sample size, whereas the third also depends on the population shape parameters. Monte Carlo simulations demonstrate the practicability of the weighted MLE method. When compared with the iterative MLE technique, the bias is reduced by a factor of 7 (irrespective of the sample size) and the variability of the parameter estimates is also reduced by a factor of 7 for very small sample sizes, but this gain disappears for large sample sizes.

# I. Introduction

The Weibull distribution is often used in experimental psychology to either describe response time data (e.g. Burbeck & Luce, 1982; Palmer, 1998) or test a model (e.g. Cousineau & Shiffrin, 2004; Cousineau, Goodman, & Shiffrin, 2002). In some applications, the Weibull distribution is just a convenient tool used to highlight the properties of interests in the data (e.g. Rouder, Lu, Speckman, Sun, & Jiang, 2005). In other applications, however, the Weibull distribution is a direct consequence of a cognitive model (e.g. Cousineau, 2004; Logan, 1988; Van Zandt & Ratcliff, 1995; Tuerlinckx, 2004).

The most commonly used technique to estimate the parameters of a data set assuming the Weibull distribution is the maximum likelihood estimation technique (henceforth called the iterative MLE). The problem with this technique is that it returns biased estimators. The exact amount of bias is unknown and depends on the sample size. As a consequence, it is not possible to compare the parameter values obtained from samples of different sizes. As an example, for a very small sample (eight observations), the shape and scale parameters can be underestimated by more than  $40\%$  and  $30\%$ , respectively (these figures were approximated using Monte Carlo simulations described later). Overall, if the three parameters are seen as a vector, the length of the estimated vector is wrong by over  $50\%$ . For medium-sized samples  $(n = 32)$ , the vector is still wrong by over  $10\%$ . Averaging the parameters across multiple participants (assuming they have identical reponse-time distributions) can be used to reduce the variability of the estimates, but this cannot eliminate the biases. Because experiments in psychology rarely have the same sample sizes, this is a major obstacle for cross-experiment comparisons, an obstacle that we wish to eliminate in the present paper.

Electrical engineers, who extensively use the Weibull distribution for voltage breakage, have developed many heuristics which aim to remove the bias of MLE estimates. However, these heuristics were developed for the two-parameter (no shift) Weibull distribution and cannot be generalized to the three-parameter Weibull distribution (see Cacciari & Montanari, 1994, for a review of some of these heuristics). Likewise, Hirose (1999) proposed to create a set of polynomials which, given the obtained parameters, would return the unbiased parameters. These polynomials are based on the biases found using Monte Carlo simulations for a large number of parameter values. However, this technique requires a tremendous amount of computation and must be recalibrated for each implementation of the MLE program. Further, we found the gains to be modest (Cousineau, in press).

In the following, we extend the MLE technique by incorporating three weights into the solutions to the maximum likelihood equations. Exact expressions for these weights are given, but being based on the (unknown) population parameters, we instead examine their expected values. In doing so, we will need to move from an iterative MLE technique to a two-step method in which two of the parameters are estimated iteratively and the last one is estimated algebraically. Finally, Monte Carlo simulations will examine the performance of the iterative versus the two-step MLEs, and within the two-step methods, the impact of the weights on the bias of the parameters.

# 2. The iterative and weighted MLE equations

In psychology, the iterative MLE technique is definitely the most commonly used technique (Cousineau & Larochelle, 1997; Dolan, 2000; Heathcote, 1996; Heathcote, Brown, & Cousineau, 2004; Rouder, Sun, Speckman, Lu, & Zhou, 2003; see Cousineau, Brown, & Heathcote, 2004, for a review and Van Zandt, 2000, for alternatives).

The best-fitting shape, scale, and shift parameters are found by maximizing the likelihood function (or, more commonly, the log of the likelihood function), performing a search in the parameter space (see Myung, 2003, for a tutorial). Let the true parameters be denoted by  $\gamma$ ,  $\beta$ , and  $\alpha$  for the shape, the scale, and the shift parameters, respectively. Figure 1 illustrates some Weibull distributions varying on their shape.

The domains of the parameters are given by  $\gamma \in \Gamma = \mathbb{R}^{+}$ ,  $\beta \in B = \mathbb{R}^{+}$ , and  $\alpha \in A = \{\alpha < \min(X), \alpha \in \mathbb{R}\}$  where  $X$  denotes the sample and  $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ excludes zero. The iterative MLEs are obtained by performing a search over the three parameters simultaneously:

$$
\{\hat {\gamma}, \hat {\beta}, \hat {\alpha} \} _ {\mathrm {M L E}} = \left\{\underset {\gamma \in \Gamma , \beta \in B, \alpha \in A} {\arg \max } \log (\ell (\gamma , \beta , \alpha | X)) \right\} \tag {1}
$$

where  $\hat{\gamma},\hat{\beta}$ , and  $\hat{\alpha}$  are the estimated parameters, and the likelihood function  $\ell$  is given by

$$
\ell (\gamma , \beta , \alpha | X) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (x _ {i} | \gamma , \beta , \alpha)
$$

in which  $n$  is the sample size and  $f$  is the assumed probability density function (pdf) of  $x$ , given by the Weibull distribution:

$$
f (x | \gamma , \beta , \alpha) = e ^ {- ((x - \alpha) / \beta) ^ {\gamma}} (x - \alpha) ^ {\gamma - 1} \beta^ {- \gamma} \gamma .
$$

Its cumulative density function (cdf) is given by

$$
F (x | \gamma , \beta , \alpha) = 1 - e ^ {- ((x - \alpha) / \beta) ^ {\gamma}}.
$$

The iterative MLE technique is used by most of the computer programs available (RTSYS, PASTIS, DISFIT, and QMPE). It is very general and can be adapted to any distribution whose pdf is known.

Instead of relying on brute computational power, we can seek an analytical solution to (1) by looking for a maximum using the derivatives with respect to each of the parameters. The result, given in equation (2), is composed of a system of equations for  $\hat{\gamma}$  and  $\hat{\alpha}$  and a second equation for  $\hat{\beta}$  given  $\hat{\gamma}$  and  $\hat{\alpha}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{l} \{\hat {\gamma}, \hat {\alpha} \} _ {\mathrm {M L E}} = \left\{\arg \min  _ {\gamma \in \Gamma , \alpha \in A} \left(\frac {1}{\gamma} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}\right) ^ {2}, \right. \\ \underset {\gamma \in \Gamma , \alpha \in A} {\arg \min } \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \alpha} \times \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma - 1}} - \frac {\gamma}{\gamma - 1}\right) ^ {2} \}, \tag {2} \\ \end{array}
$$

$$
[ \hat {\beta} | \hat {\gamma}, \hat {\alpha} ] _ {\mathrm {M L E}} = \sqrt [ \gamma ]{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \hat {\alpha}) ^ {\hat {\gamma}}}.
$$

We call this solution a 'two-step MLE' solution. In the above notation, the curly braces denote that a search is required whereas square brackets indicate an algebraic solution.<sup>2</sup> Appendix A indicates how this solution is derived.

The equations in (2) are the well-known MLE solutions for the Weibull distributions (see Rockette, Antle, & Klimko, 1974). What is less well known is that they can also be obtained from simple algebraic manipulations. Jacquelin (1993) showed how to derive the solution in the case of the two-parameter (no shift) Weibull distribution; in Appendix B, we derive the solution for the three-parameter Weibull distribution. We call this solution the weighted MLE solution as it is identical to the standard MLE solution except for the presence of three weights,  $W_{1}$ ,  $W_{2}$ , and  $W_{3}$ .

Overall, the weighted MLEs are given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \{\hat {\gamma}, \hat {\alpha} \} _ {W} = \left\{\arg \min  _ {\gamma \in \Gamma , \alpha \in A} \left(\frac {W _ {2}}{\gamma} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}\right) ^ {2}, \right. \\ \underset {\gamma \in \Gamma , \alpha \in A} {\arg \min } \left(\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \alpha} \times \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma - 1}} - W _ {3}\right) ^ {2} \}, \tag {3} \\ \end{array}
$$

$$
[ \hat {\beta} | \hat {\gamma}, \hat {\alpha} ] _ {W} = \sqrt [ \gamma ]{\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \hat {\alpha}) ^ {\hat {\gamma}}}.
$$


Nearly unbiased Weibull parameters

in which the quantities  $W_{1}$ ,  $W_{2}$ , and  $W_{3}$  are defined as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} W _ {1} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right), \\ W _ {2} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right) \log \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right)\right)}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right)} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right)\right), \\ W _ {3} = W _ {1} \frac {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {- 1 / \gamma}}{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {(\gamma - 1) / \gamma}}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Except for the introduction of  $W_{1}$  and  $W_{2}$  and the replacement of  $\gamma / (\gamma - 1)$  by  $W_{3}$ , the weighted MLE equations are identical to the standard MLE equations. The terms  $W_{i}$  can be seen as weights and the corresponding MLE weights are 1, 1, and  $\gamma / (\gamma - 1)$ . However, they cannot be pulled out of the equations, so that it is not possible to unbiased the estimates after the search has been completed.

When the true weights  $W_{1}$ ,  $W_{2}$ , and  $W_{3}$  are used, the estimated parameters are precisely the true population parameters whatever the sample. Hence, the estimated parameters  $\hat{\alpha} = \alpha$ ,  $\hat{\beta} = \beta$ , and  $\hat{\gamma} = \gamma$ . In addition,  $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\alpha}) = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}) = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\gamma}) = 0$ . The difficulty is that computing the true weights  $W_{1}$ ,  $W_{2}$ , and  $W_{3}$  requires  $F(X_{i})$ , which in-turn requires the true parameters. Of course, they are unknown in practical applications.

One way around this difficulty would be to estimate the  $W_{s}$  using the observed cumulative frequencies  $\hat{F}(x_{i})$ . However, there is no agreed method to estimate them. The simple method  $\hat{F}(x_{(i)}) = i/n$  would return 1 for the last datum and indeterminate for  $W_{1}$  (in which  $x_{(i)}$  is the  $i$ th datum sorted in ascending order). Fothergill (1990) and Haccoun and Cousineau (2007) discuss some other estimates.

A more fruitful approach is to realize that the weights are random variables. Hence, instead of using the exact but unknown weights, we can infer their most probable values. In the following section, we examine the arithmetic mean, the median, and the geometric mean of  $W_{1}$ ,  $W_{2}$ , and  $W_{3}$ .

To prove the limit of  $W_{1}$  and some of the following propositions, it is convenient to deduce the sampling distribution of  $W_{1}$ . Assume a population  $X$  following a Weibull distribution with true parameters  $\gamma$ ,  $\beta$ , and  $\alpha$ . The quantity  $\log (1 / (1 - F(X_i)))$  equals  $((x_i - \alpha) / \beta)^{\gamma}$ . Defining  $z_{i}$  as  $((x_{i} - \alpha) / \beta)^{\gamma}$ , we find that the  $z_{i}$  are distributed following an (unshifted) exponential distribution with mean parameter 1 (Rose & Smith, 2001). The quantity  $(1 / n)\sum_{i = 1}^{n}((x_i - \alpha) / \beta)^{\gamma}$  equals  $\sum_{i = 1}^{n}(z_{i} / n)$  which is a convolution of  $n$  exponentially distributed variates with mean parameter  $1 / n$ . This is known to result in a gamma distribution with parameters  $\{n, 1 / n\}$  (Luce, 1986; Townsend & Ashby, 1983). The expected value of  $W_{1}$  is therefore  $n\times (1 / n) = 1$  whatever the sample size.

Regarding  $W_{2}$  and  $W_{3}$ , we were unable to derive their sampling distributions or to demonstrate the propositions.

Proposition 1, if correct, shows that for large  $n$ , the weighted MLEs (equation (3)) are equivalent to the standard MLEs (2). Among other things, asymptotically this means that whenever standard MLEs are efficient and normally distributed, so are the weighted MLEs. As shown in Smith (1985), this is the case when  $\gamma \geq 2$ .

Proposition 2. The sampling distribution of  $W_{1}$  and  $W_{2}$  depends only on the sample size, whereas the sampling distribution of  $W_{3}$  depends on the sample size and the shape parameter of the population.

Regarding  $W_{1}$ , we have already shown that the sampling distribution is gamma with parameters  $\{n, 1/n\}$ . Regarding  $W_{2}$ , note that the quantity  $\log(1/(1 - F(X_{i}))$  equals  $((x_{i} - \alpha)/\beta)^{\gamma}$  which follows an exponential distribution with rate parameter 1. Hence, the three sums in  $W_{2}$  are random variates which only depend on  $n$ . For  $W_{3}$ , we do not have a formal demonstration. However, we remark that the numerator follows an extreme value type II (Fréchet) distribution with scale parameter  $1/n$  and shape parameter  $\gamma$ , whereas the denominator follows an extreme value type II distribution with scale parameter  $1/n$  and shape parameter  $\gamma / (\gamma - 1)$  (Galambos, 1978; Gumbel, 1958).

Proposition 3. Regarding  $W_{1}$ , its mean value is 1 (irrespective of sample size); its median value is the median of a gamma distribution with parameter  $\{n, 1/n\}$ ; and its geometric average value is  $\exp(\psi(n)) / n$  where  $\psi(n)$  is the digamma function given by  $\Gamma'(n) / \Gamma(n)$ . Regarding  $W_{2}$ , its mean value is  $1 - (1/n)$ .

The propositions regarding  $W_{1}$  are all derived from the fact that its sampling distribution is a gamma distribution with parameter  $\{n, 1/n\}$ . The geometric average of  $W_{1}$  was obtained by finding the expected value of the transformed variable  $y_{i} = \log (z_{i})$ , since  $G(z_{i}) = e^{\log (G(z_{i}))} = e^{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log (z_{i})\right)/n} = e^{E[\log (z_{i})]}$  where  $G(z_{i}) = \sqrt[n]{\prod_{i=1}^{n} z_{i}}$  denotes the geometric mean (Rose & Smith, 2000).

The proposition regarding the mean of  $W_{2}$  is not demonstrated but Monte Carlo simulations suggested this result.

# 5. Point estimates and median bias

To compare the techniques, we need to define a measure of exactitude showing how well the true parameters are recovered from random samples. Possible definitions of exactitude are the mean bias  $(b_{\mathrm{Mn}} = E[\hat{\theta} ] - \theta_T)$ , where  $\theta$  is one parameter and  $E$  is the mean), the median bias  $(b_{\mathrm{Md}} = \mathrm{Md}[\hat{\theta} ] - \theta_T)$  and the modal bias  $(b_{\mathrm{Mo}} = \mathrm{Mode}[\hat{\theta} ] - \theta_T)$ .

The mean bias is not a good choice because if the distribution of the estimates is skewed (as is certainly the case for small samples), the true parameter value is more likely to be near the mode than the mean of the distribution. The modal bias is not a convenient measure as it is difficult to estimate the mode of a sample. Hence, the median bias is the preferred measure of exactitude (Cacciari & Montanari, 1994; Jacquelin, 1997). In addition, the following property holds for any continuous increasing (decreasing) transformation  $U$ :  $\mathrm{Md}(U(x)) = U(\mathrm{Md}(x))$ . In the following, we will use the median bias to evaluate the estimation techniques.

# 6. Bias of the scale parameter assuming  $\gamma$  and  $\alpha$  are known

Regarding the parameter  $\beta$  given by equation (3), if we assume that  $\gamma$  and  $\alpha$  are known, we can write

$$
\operatorname {M d} (\hat {\beta}) = \operatorname {M d} \left(\sqrt [ \gamma ]{\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}\right) = \sqrt [ \gamma ]{\frac {1}{n \operatorname {M d} (W _ {1})} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}. \tag {5}
$$

Hence, the median bias of  $\beta$  when the median of the weight  $W_{1}$  is used  $(J_{1})$  should be zero.

This was confirmed by simulations. Across simulations, we varied the sample size  $n$  (8, 16, 32) and the population shape  $\gamma$  (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5). The scale and shift parameters, being scaling parameters (see Rouder et al., 2005, footnote 3, for a definition), were held constant at  $\{\beta = 100, \alpha = 300\}$ . The shape parameters were chosen such that there are two for which the regular MLE should be both efficient and normally distributed  $(\gamma \geq 2)$ , two for which the regular MLE should work but where the distributions of the estimates are not normal  $(1 \leq \gamma < 2)$ , and the last case  $(\gamma < 1)$  corresponds to a situation for which there should be no consistent estimators (Smith, 1985).

For each simulation, a random sample of size  $n$  was generated and the best-fitting scale parameter  $\hat{\beta}$  was estimated using equation (3) with three different weights: the expected value of  $W_{1}$  ( $E_{1}$ , which is always 1); its median value ( $J_{1}$ ) and its geometric mean value ( $G_{1}$ ). For each combination of  $n \times \gamma$ ,  $2^{10}$  such simulations were run and the median estimated  $\hat{\beta}$  was computed.


The results are reported in Table 5. As can be seen, when the median weight is used, the median bias is very close to zero, as expected from equation (5). More importantly, the median bias seems independent of the sample size and of the true population parameter. Hence, the weight  $J_{1}$  returns a truly unbiased estimate of  $\beta$  (given the assumption that  $\gamma$  and  $\alpha$  are known; we come back to this issue later). Using the mean weight  $E_{1}$  produces an underestimated median estimate and using  $G_{1}$ , an overestimated median estimate. In the last two cases, the estimates are better when  $n$  is large and when  $\gamma$  is large. This last result is understandable because as  $\gamma$  increases, the Weibull distribution becomes more symmetrical and the mean, median, and geometric mean weights tend towards identical values.

# 7. Bias of the shape and shift parameters

Isolating the second weight from equation (3), we can write

$$

$$

such that, using the geometric mean (for which  $G(x_{i}y_{i}) = G(x_{i})G(y_{i})$  holds for two independent random variables), we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} G _ {2} = G \left(W _ {2}\right) = G \left(\gamma \left(- \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) + \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma} \log \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right)}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma}}\right)\right) \\ = G (\gamma) \times G \left(- \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) + \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha)}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

Sadly, it is not possible to isolate  $G(\gamma)$  in the other equation defining  $\hat{\gamma}$  and  $\hat{\alpha}$ . Hence, it is not known whether  $G_{2}$  and  $G_{3}$  would result in small biases or not. For that reason, we

# 8. Simultaneous estimates of the three parameters

The above results (unbiased estimates of  $\beta$  using  $J_{1}$ , nearly unbiased estimates of  $\gamma$  and  $\alpha$  using  $G_{2}$  and  $G_{3}$ ) were established independently and, regarding  $\beta$ , assuming the exact values of  $\gamma$  and  $\alpha$ . In practical applications, the three parameters will be estimated and the estimated  $\hat{\beta}$  will depend on the estimated  $\hat{\gamma}$  and  $\hat{\alpha}$  as well. In addition, even though  $\{\gamma, \alpha\}_{G_1, G_2}$  are less biased than  $\{\gamma, \alpha\}_{J_1 J_2}$ , it does not mean that the  $\hat{\beta}$  obtained from the former will be less biased than the  $\beta$  obtained from the latter. Because this question is difficult to answer by examining the equations, we ran another series of simulations.

We first examine the usual iterative MLE solutions (equation (1)), which is a one-step method since all three parameters are estimated simultaneously. We also examined the two-step method of equation (2). This is the same as equation (3) except that the asymptotic weights  $1, 1$ , and  $\gamma / (\gamma - 1)$  are used. Finally, we tested three sets of weights  $\{J_1, J_2, J_3\}$ ,  $\{G_1, G_2, G_3\}$ , and a mixture of weights  $\{J_1, G_2, G_3\}$ . This last mixture of weights was tested because  $G_2$  and  $G_3$  were the best weights for estimating  $\gamma$  and  $\alpha$ , whereas  $J_1$  was the best weight for estimating  $\beta$ . The procedure used was identical to the one previously used.

Some of the results are presented in Table 7. The median bias is shown for each parameter individually. We also report a three-dimensional bias which is the median bias in the three-dimensional parameter space, measured as the median length of the vector separating the true parameters from the estimated parameters relative to the parameter vector length - in symbols,

$$
b _ {\mathrm {Md}} ^ {\mathrm {3D}} = \frac {\mathrm {Md} \left(\| \hat {\theta} - \theta \|\right)}{\| \theta \|} \times 100 \%,
$$

where  $\theta$  is the true parameter vector and  $\hat{\theta}$  is the estimated parameter vector.

The iterative MLE evidently does a very poor job for very small samples  $(n = 8)$ , with biases on  $\gamma$  and  $\beta$  exceeding  $25\%$ . To see how bad this is, note that with a medium-sized sample  $(n = 32)$ , the bias is worse than for the two-step MLE technique with a small sample  $(n = 16)$  and also twice as bad as the  $J_{1}$ ,  $J_{2}$ , and  $J_{3}$  weights with a very small sample  $(n = 8)$ .

The three set of weights outperformed the two-step MLE. The set  $\{J_1, J_2, J_3\}$  (reported in Table 7) was the best one, followed by the mixture  $\{J_1, G_2, G_3\}$ . The set  $\{G_1, G_2, G_3\}$  was last with bias approximately  $50\%$  larger than those of  $\{J_1, J_2, J_3\}$ . Still, the biases obtained by this last set were half those obtained by the two-step MLE technique.

Examining the three-dimensional biases for  $n = 16$  and  $n = 32$ , we see that on average the set of weights  $\{J_1, J_2, J_3\}$  produced estimates that are about seven times less biased than the iterative MLE estimates (average three-dimensional bias of 0.187 for the iterative MLE vs. 0.025 for the weighted MLE) and 3.5 times less biased than the two-step MLE technique (average three-dimensional bias of 0.085).

In parallel to bias, we also checked the efficiency of the techniques (efficiency measures the variability of the estimates). This measure is important since the general error of estimation is a function of both the systematic bias and the variability of the estimate such that a biased estimate with high efficiency (low variability) can sometimes be preferable to an unbiased estimate with low efficiency.

# 9. General discussion

The present paper has shown how to modify the regular MLE equations to obtain nearly unbiased estimates. The exact values of the weights required are not known when the true parameters are unknown, but their most probable values can be used.

The weighted MLE technique (with median weights) works best for the three-parameter Weibull distribution and for the two-parameter Weibull distribution as well (as discussed in Appendix C).

The difference between the weights proposed here and the MLE weights becomes vanishingly small as  $n$  increases. Hence, there must be an  $n$  after which the choice of technique no longer makes a difference. Simulations ran in Cousineau (in press) suggest that the critical  $n$  is near 80 (this is true only if  $\gamma > 1$ ; in the opposite case, the MLE solutions are always inconsistent whatever the sample size). Conversely, a temptation would be to use this technique for very small sample sizes (e.g.  $n < 16$ ). However, we recommend cautiousness as human participants introduce other forms of variability beyond sampling variability.

As mentioned earlier, the third weight used by the standard MLE technique is undefined when  $\gamma = 1$  and inconsistent when  $\gamma < 1$ . Using  $J_{3}$ , the median of the weight  $W_{3}$ , avoids the problem as it is defined for any  $\gamma$ . The maximum product of spacing technique solved this inconsistency problem using a slightly different approach: instead of changing the weights, Cheng and Amin (1983) changed the probability measure. Indeed, they replaced  $f(x_{(i)})$  by  $\int_{x_{(i-1)}}^{x_{(i)}} f(x) dx = F(x_{(i)}) - F(x_{(i-1)})$ , with  $F(x_{(0)}) = 0$ ,  $F(x_{(n)}) = 1$  and  $x_{(i)}$  being sorted in ascending order. With this new measure, infinities that occurred when the smallest datum was equal to  $\alpha$  are avoided. Nevertheless, this technique still returns biased estimates (Cousineau, in press; Cousineau, Brown, & Heathcote, 2004). The same also applies to related techniques, the quantile maximum product (QMPE, Heathcote, Brown, & Cousineau, 2004) and the order statistic product of spacing (Speckman & Rouder, 2004).

In the context of a different distribution, Warm (1989) developed a weighted maximum likelihood estimator. The general approach used by Warm is similar to that taken here but differs on two details. First, in the context of the model explored by Warm, it was possible to know the mean bias, and so the derivative of this expected value was subtracted from the log likelihood. Regarding the Weibull model discussed here, expected biases are so far unknown. Second, Warm assumed that the weight is subtracted; in the present work, the relationship between the weights and the function to be minimized is not predetermined but is the result of algebraic manipulations.

This paper leaves one open question: What are the sampling distributions of  $W_{2}$  and  $W_{3}$ ? Having them in closed form would avoid the use of approximations (Tables 3 and 4), might increase the precision of the estimates, and speed up the fitting process. Currently, we have look-up tables for  $G_{2}, G_{3}, J_{2}$ , and  $J_{3}$  that are accurate to two digits only. Further, for the third weight, we only have the approximations as a function of  $\gamma$  between  $\gamma = 0.25$  and  $\gamma = 2.75$  in steps of 0.25. In between, linear interpolations are used, even though the functions  $E_{3}, G_{3}$ , and  $J_{3}$  are not linear with respect to  $\gamma$  ( $J_{3}$  was seen in Figure 2). Despite these approximations, the technique can be programmed and automatized. $^{5}$

# Appendix A: The two-step MLE for the Weibull distribution

In this appendix, we derive the MLE for the Weibull distribution. First note that for the Weibull distribution,

$$
\log (\ell (\gamma , \beta , \alpha | X)) = - n \gamma \log (\beta) + n \log (\gamma) + (\gamma - 1) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) - \beta^ {- \gamma} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}.
$$

The derivative with respect to the scale parameter is

$$
\frac {\partial \log (\ell (\gamma , \beta , \alpha | X))}{\partial \beta} = - \frac {n \gamma}{\beta} + \beta^ {- \gamma - 1} \gamma \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}.
$$

Setting it to zero and rearranging gives

$$
\beta = \sqrt [ \gamma ]{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}. \tag {A.1}
$$

It is the only parameter which can be isolated on the left-hand side of an equation. Note that computing it requires knowledge of the other two parameters.


The derivative with respect to  $\gamma$  yields:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial \log (\ell (\gamma , \beta , \alpha | X))}{\partial \gamma} = \frac {n}{\gamma} - n \log (\beta) + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) + \beta^ {- \gamma} \log (\beta) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma} \\ - \beta^ {- \gamma} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha)\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

Replacing  $\beta^{-\gamma}$  with  $1 / ((1 / n)\sum_{i = 1}^{n}(x_i - \alpha)^{\gamma})$  (from (A.1)), setting the equation equal to 0, and dividing by  $n$  leads to:

$$
\frac {1}{\gamma} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma}} = 0. \tag {A.2}
$$

Similarly for  $\alpha$ , we get

$$
\frac {\partial \log (\ell (\gamma , \beta , \alpha | X))}{\partial \alpha} = - (\gamma - 1) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \alpha} + \beta^ {- \gamma} \gamma \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma - 1}
$$

which is set equal to 0. After some reorganization, including replacement of  $\beta^{\gamma}$  by  $(1 / n)\sum_{i = 1}^{n}(x_i - \alpha)^{\gamma}$ , we get

$$
\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \alpha} \times \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma - 1}} - \frac {\gamma}{\gamma - 1} = 0. \tag {A.3}
$$

Equations (A.2) and (A.3) implicitly define the two parameters  $\gamma$  and  $\alpha$ , which in turn can be used to derive  $\beta$ . This solution is referred to in this paper as a two-step MLE method.

# Appendix B: Deriving the weighted MLE solution from algebraic manipulations

In this appendix, we show how to derive the weighted MLE solution for the Weibull distribution using algebraic manipulations. First, let us define  $W_{1}$  as

$$
\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right), \tag {B.1}
$$

where  $F(x_{(i)})$  stands for  $F(x_{i}|\gamma ,\beta ,\alpha)$  . From the Weibull cdf, we see that

$$
W _ {1} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\frac {x _ {i} - \alpha}{\beta}\right) ^ {\gamma} = \frac {1}{n \beta^ {\gamma}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma},
$$

so that

$$
\beta = \sqrt [ \gamma ]{\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}. \tag {B.2}
$$

Note that (B.2) is nearly identical to the standard MLE solution except for the presence of the term  $W_{1}$ . Similarly, we can find the second weighted MLE equation with the following manipulations. Start with

$$
\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right) = \left(\frac {x _ {i} - \alpha}{\beta}\right) ^ {\gamma} = \frac {\left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma}}{\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma}}, \tag {B.3}
$$

obtained by inserting the definition of  $\beta^{\gamma}$  (B.2). Taking the logarithms, we get

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) = \log \left(\frac {(x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}\right) \\ = \gamma \log \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) - \log \left(\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma}\right). \tag {B.4} \\ \end{array}
$$

Multiply (B.3) and (B.4) together, and take the average:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right) \log \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) \\ = \gamma W _ {1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {\log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}} - W _ {1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {\log \left(\frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{n W _ {1}}\right) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}} \\ = \gamma W _ {1} \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}} - W _ {1} \log \left(\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}\right) \\ \end{array}
$$

Dividing both sides by  $W_{1}$  and replacing  $W_{1}$  by its definition (B.1), we get

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right) \log \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right)\right)}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right)} \\ = \gamma \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {1} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}} - \log \left(\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}\right). \\ \end{array}
$$

Subtracting from this equation the mean of (B.4), we obtain

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right) \log \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right)}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) \\ = \gamma \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}} - \log \left(\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}\right) - \frac {\gamma}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) \\ + \log \left(\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}\right) \\ = \gamma \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}} - \frac {\gamma}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha). \\ \end{array}
$$

Defining  $\mathbf{W}_2$  as the lhs of the above equation, we get:

$$
W _ {2} = \gamma \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}} - \frac {\gamma}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha), \tag {B.5}
$$

so that

$$
\frac {W _ {2}}{\gamma} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (x _ {i} - \alpha) (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}} = 0. \tag {B.6}
$$

Again, this formula is pretty much the same as the standard MLE solution except for the presence of a term  $W_{2}$ . Finally, we derive the last equation. We first examine

$$
\frac {\beta}{x _ {i} - \alpha} = \left(\sqrt [ \gamma ]{\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)}\right) ^ {- 1} = \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {- 1 / \gamma}.
$$

By taking the summation and dividing both sides by  $n$ , we have

$$
\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {\beta}{x _ {i} - \alpha} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {- 1 / \gamma}.
$$

We can further simplify the left-hand side:

$$
\frac {\beta}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \alpha} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right)\right) ^ {- 1 / \gamma}. \tag {B.7}
$$

In parallel, we examine the ratio

$$
\left(\frac {x _ {i} - \alpha}{\beta}\right) ^ {\gamma - 1} = \left(\sqrt [ \gamma ]{\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)}\right) ^ {\gamma - 1} = \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {(\gamma - 1) / \gamma},
$$


which yields, after summation and division by  $n$  on both sides,

$$
\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\frac {x _ {i} - \alpha}{\beta}\right) ^ {\gamma - 1} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {(\gamma - 1) / \gamma}
$$

or

$$
\frac {1}{n \beta^ {\gamma - 1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(x _ {i} - \alpha\right) ^ {\gamma - 1} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F \left(x _ {i}\right)}\right)\right) ^ {(\gamma - 1) / \gamma}. \tag {B.8}
$$

If we divide (B.7) by (B.8), we get for the right-hand side

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\frac {\beta}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \alpha}}{\frac {1}{n \beta^ {\gamma - 1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma - 1}} = \beta^ {\gamma} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{(x _ {i} - \alpha) \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma - 1}} \\ = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \alpha} \times \frac {\frac {1}{n W _ {1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma - 1}}, \\ \end{array}
$$

and for the left-hand side

$$
\frac {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {- 1 / \gamma}}{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {(\gamma - 1) / \gamma}}.
$$

Defining  $W_{3}$  as

$$
W _ {3} = W _ {1} \frac {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {- 1 / \gamma}}{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\log \left(\frac {1}{1 - F (x _ {i})}\right)\right) ^ {(\gamma - 1) / \gamma}},
$$

$$
\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \alpha} \times \frac {\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma}}{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - \alpha) ^ {\gamma - 1}} - W _ {3} = 0, \tag {B.9}
$$

which is identical to the third MLE equation except for the term  $W_{3}$ .
