# Generalization of the Method of Maximum Likelihood

Jean Jacquelin

Alcatel Alsthom Recherche,
Electrical Engineering Department, Marcoussis,
France

## ABSTRACT

The method of ‘maximum likelihood’ (MLH), frequently used to study experimental findings in accordance with Weibull statistics, is more biased, the smaller the number of samples. Semi-empirical corrective coefficients are proposed to attempt to correct the deficiency, which is unsatisfactory from a theoretical standpoint. In this paper, it is demonstrated that MLH contains an implicit hypothesis, which validity is discussed. Generalization of the basic MLH formulae enables other hypotheses to be chosen. In particular, if the hypothesis chosen is derived from the ‘Median Rank Function’, the whole becomes coherent.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

THE Weibull distribution is commonly used for statistical analysis of the results of failure tests on electrical insulation and in many other cases. The cumulative Weibull distribution function F considered is

$$
F (t) = 1 - \exp \left[ - \left(\frac {t}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta} \right]\tag{1}
$$

where t is the random variable, duration, breakage stress, etc., $\alpha$ the scale parameter, and $\beta$ the shape parameter.

To simplify the explanations and concentrate the discussion on what is essential, we therefore consider neither a threshold value for t nor censored nor incomplete failure data.

A population of n samples will witness failures occurring at the following stresses $t_{1}, t_{2}, t_{3}, \ldots, t_{n}$ .

Knowing these n measured values, the problem is to arrive at the best estimation on $\alpha$ and $\beta$ for Equation (1).

In fact, two questions then arise. (a): The best estimate with relation to what criterion? and (b): How to achieve this objective?

The first question (a) sets the problem of choosing a 'Rank Function', assumed to approach the function $F(t_i)$ for discrete values $t_i$ . Various rank functions have been considered, such as $F = (i - 0.5) / n$ or $F = i / (n + 1)$ . The following function (2) is an excellent approximation for the 'Median Rank Function'

$$
F (i, n) = \frac {i - 0 . 3}{n + 0 . 4}\tag{2}
$$

This has been established and verified either by theoretical statistical calculations or by statistical experiments (the Monte-Carlo method). There is no need to dwell further on this point, which is accepted by many writers [1].

The second question (b) sets the problem of choosing a method of calculating $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in such a way that on the one hand Equation (1) and on the other a rank function match to the best possible degree. The first methods used were either graphical (on Weibull paper [2]) or more accurate ones by linear regression. Reference [3] gives the complete relevant equations. A more recent method, that of 'Maximum Likelihood' (MLH), now commonly used, offers major advantages. In this method, no rank function would seem at first sight to appear, thus miraculously appearing to eliminate the delicate question of (a). We propose to return to this point later. A clear algorithm of this method is given in [4]. However, it has appeared that the common MLH method is more biased, the smaller the population studied. To counter this difficulty, it has been proposed to apply an 'unbiasing factor' to the MLH results [5]. This corrective coefficient is a function of $n$ . Whilst in practice this method constitutes a remedy, it is unsatisfactory from the theoretical standpoint. If a semi-empirical coefficient is applied to the result of MLH, this result becomes incoherent with the basis hypotheses of MLH.

The purpose of this paper is to clarify this point. We propose to show that MLH embodies an implicit hypothesis, that this hypothesis is questionable in the case of low n, that MLH can be generalized in accordance with the rank functions, and that the generalized MLH corresponding to the median rank function eliminates the incoherence and the contradiction.

To avoid the ambiguities of notations, we propose to adopt the following designations.

hrMLH the hidden rank function MLH, the method in its present form

grMLH the generalized rank function MLH, the generalized method in which any rank function can be introduced

mrMLH the median rank function MLH, the specific case in which the median rank function is introduced into grMLH.

## 2. FIRST IMPLICIT HYPOTHESIS OF hrMLH

Let $F_{i}$ designate the value of the Equation (1) for $t = t_{i}$ .

$$
F i = 1 - \exp - \left[ \frac {t _ {i}}{\alpha} \right] ^ {\beta}\tag{3}
$$

The following can then easily be deduced:

$$
\ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] = \left[ \frac {t _ {i}}{\alpha} \right] ^ {\beta}\tag{4}
$$

$$
S _ {1} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {i = n} \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] = \frac {1}{\alpha^ {\beta} n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}\tag{5}
$$

$$
\alpha = \left[ \frac {1}{S _ {1} n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \right] ^ {1 / \beta}\tag{6}
$$

In hrMLH, the Equation giving $\alpha$ is exactly similar to (6), but without the appearance of the term $S_{1}$ . Hence, an initial implicit hypothesis of hrMLH is that $S_{1}=1$ . This will be discussed later. In grMLH, we propose a method to calculate $S_{1}$ which can thus differ from 1.

## 3. SECOND IMPLICIT HYPOTHESIS OF hrMLH

The second hypothesis is distinctly more complicated. First, we must find the formula giving $\sum \ln \{(\ln (1 / (1 - F_i)))\}$ . Equation (7) is obtained by substituting $\alpha$ deduced from (6) in (4)

$$
\ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] = \frac {S _ {1} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}}{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}}\tag{7}
$$

$$
\ln \left[ \ln [ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} ] \right] = \beta \ln (t _ {i}) - \ln \left[ \frac {1}{S _ {1} n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \right]\tag{8}
$$

$$
\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln \left[ \ln [ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} ] \right] = \frac {\beta}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (t _ {i}) - \ln \left[ \frac {1}{S _ {1} n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \right]\tag{9}
$$

The expression $\sum \ln (1 / (1 - F_i))\ln \{\ln (1 / (1 - F_i))\}$ is then calculated by multiplying (7) and (8) and then as the sum from 1 to $n$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left[ \ln [ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} ] \ln \left[ \ln [ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} ] \right] \right] = \\ S _ {1} \beta \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \ln (t _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}} - S _ {1} \ln \left[ \frac {1}{S _ {1} n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \right] \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

$S_{1}$ is taken from (5) and substituted in (10)

$$
\frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln [ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} ] \ln \left[ \ln [ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} ] \right]}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln [ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} ]} = \beta \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \ln (t _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}} - \ln \left[ \frac {1}{S _ {1} n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \right]\tag{11}
$$

By subtracting (9) from this Equation, the following Equation $S_{2}$ can then be found

$$
S _ {2} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] \ln \left[ \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] \right]}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right]} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln \left[ \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] \right]
$$

$$
= \beta \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \ln (t _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}} - \frac {\beta}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (t _ {i})\tag{12}
$$

This equation can be written as

$$
\frac {S _ {2}}{\beta} - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \ln (t _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (t _ {i}) = 0\tag{13}
$$

In hrMLH, the equation leading to $\beta$ is exactly similar to (12), but without the appearance of the term $S_{2}$ . The second implicit hypothesis of hrMLH is hence $S_{2} = 1$ . This will be discussed later. In grMLH, we propose a method to calculate $S_{2}$ which can thus differ from 1.

## 4. PROPOSED GENERAL HYPOTHESIS FOR grMLH

In the above mathematical argument, we have striven to separate in the equations on the one hand all terms containing $F_{i}$ and on the other hand all those containing $t_{i}$ . Accordingly, each formula can be calculated in two different ways, either from the values of $F_{i}$ , or from those of $t_{i}$ . This is the key to good definition of the problem and clearly formulating the hypothesis of the relation between the form in $F_{i}$ and that in $t_{i}$ .

The hypothesis proposed to generalize MLH is as follows: given a rank function $F(i, n)$ , which is hence a discrete function, it is agreed that it equals the Weibull distribution (1) which is a continuous function, at points 1, 2, ..., i, ..., n. The immediate consequence of this is that the formulae for calculating $S_1$ and $S_2$ are known from (5) and (12) in which the values $F_i$ are now known: $F_1, F_2, \ldots, F_i, \ldots, F_n$ are directly given by the chosen rank function. Hence

$$
S _ {1} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln [ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} ]\tag{14}
$$

$$
S _ {2} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] \ln \left[ \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] \right]}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right]} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln \left[ \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] \right]\tag{15}
$$

We therefore see that grMLH does not escape the general question (a) initially asked: a rank function must be chosen anyway. The role of grMLH is then well defined. It is that of answering question (b) and its role is strictly limited to this function. This is more logical.

It may appear contradictory that we have spoken in the introduction of a single implicit hypothesis and then subsequently that two have appeared, one relevant to $S_{1}$ , the second relevant to $S_{2}$ . However, we have seen that there is indeed only one main hypothesis, which is the relation with a rank function, which splits into two and brings about the two others.

## 5. DISCUSSION ON THE SPECIFIC CASE OF hrMLH

This clarifies the specific case of hrMLH where as we have seen $S_{1}=1$ and $S_{2}=1$ .

We therefore see that any discrete function $F(i)$ satisfying condition (14) with $S_{1}=1$ and (15) with $S_{2}=1$ will be coherent with hrMLH. As there are only two conditions for a considerable number of variables, it can be deduced that a considerable number of functions $F(i)$ are compatible with hrMLH. However, this is not the case for all the functions. For example, let us test the median rank function (2). For n=10, by executing (14) and (15), we find $S_{1}=0.9276$ and $S_{2}=0.7913$ , instead of 1. Consequently, the rank function (2) is not compatible with hrMLH. This lies at the origin of the incompatibility of displaying results of hrMLH on Weibull plotting papers and hrMLH deviations from the statistical tests.

It should be pointed out that for large populations (high values of n), $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ tend towards 1 and hence hrMLH displays no deviation under these conditions.

## 6. SPECIFIC CASE OF mrMLH

mrMLH is a specific case of grMLH when this is rendered compatible with the median rank function (2). It suffices for this to substitute formula (2) in (14) and (15) to calculate $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ . The calculation algorithm, Figure 2, is very simple and fast. Naturally, one can always seek more accuracy by using for $F(i)$ not the approximation (2), but an exact mathematical expression, as for instance given in [1]: this was done to compute the values $S_{1}(n)$ and $S_{2}(n)$ given in Table 1. But, in practice, in a software program, it is more simple and sufficient to use the algorithm indicated in Figure 2.

For example, in the case n = 10, we can compare the results. From Table 1, $S_{1} = 0.9290$ and $S_{2} = 0.7952$ , while from Figure 2, $S_{1} = 0.9276$ and $S_{2} = 0.7913$ .

## 7. TEST OF mrMLH BY AN 'EXACT' CUMULATIVE PROBABILITY ESTIMATE

The tests conducted yielded highly satisfactory results. For example, Table 2 is deduced from Fothergill's study [1]. Column $F(i)$ reproduces the exact values for the case $n = 10$ . Column $t_i$ is deduced from this by Weibull distribution (1) for the case $\alpha = 50$ , $\beta = 5$ .

The test consists in checking that mrMLH gives these values of $\alpha = 50$ , $\beta = 5$ , when entering the values $t_{1}, t_{2}, \ldots, t_{10}$ of Table 2 in the algorithm. (The values $F(i)$ of Table 2 are not the algorithm input data).

The result of mrMLH is $\alpha = 50.002$ , and $\beta = 4.99$ . The result of hrMLH is $\alpha = 49.762$ , $\beta = 5.76$ .

This clearly shows the deviation of hrMLH and the advantage of mrMLH which gives the correct result directly, without applying an unbiasing factor to $\beta$ .

## 9. CONCLUSION

THIS study clarifies the ambiguities involved in the maximum likelihood method in its original form (hr-MLH). A generalization is proposed for making the method coherent with any rank function (grMLH).

## 10. APPENDIX FUNDAMENTALS

## 10.1 THE DIRECT FITTING

Consider a given rank function $F(i,n)$ and a standard sample $T_{1},\ldots,T_{i},\ldots,T_{n}$ , fitted with the rank function

$$
\begin{array}{r l} F _ {i} & = 1 - \exp \left[ - \left(\frac {T _ {i}}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta} \right] \\ & = 1 - \exp \left[ - \frac {T _ {i} ^ {\beta}}{\theta} \right] \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

$$
\Phi_ {i} = \ln \left[ \frac {1}{1 - F _ {i}} \right] = \frac {T _ {i} ^ {\beta}}{\theta}\tag{17}
$$

Let $\pmb{\nu}$ be any real number

$$
T _ {i} ^ {\nu} = \theta^ {\nu / \beta} \Phi^ {\nu / \beta}
$$

$$
\ln (T _ {i}) = \frac {1}{\beta} [ \ln (\theta) + \ln (\Phi_ {i}) ]\tag{18}
$$

(19)

$$
T _ {i} ^ {\nu} \ln (T _ {i}) = \frac {1}{\beta} \theta^ {\nu / \beta} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\nu / \beta} [ \ln (\theta) + \ln (\Phi_ {i}) ]\tag{20}
$$

The sums corresponding to (18), (19), (20) are

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\nu} = \theta^ {\nu / \beta} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\nu / \beta}\tag{21}
$$

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) = \frac {n}{\beta} \ln (\theta) + \frac {1}{\beta} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (\Phi_ {i})\tag{22}
$$

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\nu} \ln (T _ {i}) =\tag{23}
$$

$$
\frac {\theta^ {\nu / \beta}}{\beta} \left[ \ln (\theta) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (\Phi_ {i} ^ {\nu / \beta}) + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\nu / \beta} \ln (\Phi_ {i}) \right]
$$

Then, we divide Equation (23) by Equation (21) and subtract Equation (22) divided by n, to obtain Equation (24).

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\nu} \ln (T _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\nu}} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) = \\ \frac {1}{\beta} \left[ \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\nu / \beta} \ln (\Phi_ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\nu / \beta}} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (\Phi_ {i}) \right] \end{array}\tag{24}
$$

Equation (24) is a general relationship (any $\nu$ ) between $(T_{1},\ldots,T_{i},\ldots,T_{n})$ and $(\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{i},\ldots,\Phi_{n})$ . For example in the case $\nu = \beta$ we obtain from (21), then from (24)

$$
\theta = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\beta}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i}} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\beta}}{n S _ {i}} = \alpha^ {\beta}\tag{25}
$$

$$
S _ {2} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} \ln (\Phi_ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i}} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (\Phi_ {i})\tag{26}
$$

$$
\frac {S _ {2}}{\beta} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\beta} \ln (T _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\beta}}\tag{27}
$$

## 10.2 THE hrMLH HYPOTHESIS

The usual MLH process consists in searching the maximum value for the likelihood function. This process fails to give the correct parameters $(\beta, \theta)$ , but provides biased parameters $(\gamma, \mu)$ , according to the absolute likelihood function $L_{T}$ :

$$
L _ {T} = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {i = n} \left[ \frac {\gamma}{\mu} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma - 1} \exp \left(- \frac {T _ {i} ^ {\gamma}}{\mu}\right) \right]\tag{28}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln (L _ {T}) = n \ln (\gamma) - n \ln (\mu) + \\ (\gamma - 1) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) - \frac {1}{\mu} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \end{array}\tag{29}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial \ln (L _ {T})}{\partial \mu} = - \frac {n}{\mu} + \frac {1}{\mu^ {2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} = 0\tag{30}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial \ln (L _ {T})}{\partial \gamma} = \frac {n}{\gamma} + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) - \frac {1}{\mu} \sum T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \ln (T _ {i}) = 0\tag{31}
$$

$$
\mu = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma}\tag{32}
$$

$$
\frac {1}{\gamma} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \ln (T _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma}} = 0\tag{33}
$$

Now, consider the general Equation (24) in the case $\nu = \gamma$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \ln (T _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma}} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) = \\ \frac {1}{\beta} \left[ \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\gamma / \beta} \ln (\Phi_ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\gamma / \beta}} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (\Phi_ {i}) \right] \end{array}\tag{34}
$$

The comparison between (33) and (34) leads to

$$
\frac {1}{\gamma / \beta} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (\Phi_ {i}) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\gamma / \beta} \ln (T _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Phi_ {i} ^ {\gamma / \beta}} = 0\tag{35}
$$

Equation (35) has the same form than (33), but with the given sample $(\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{i},\ldots,\Phi_{n})$ instead of $(T_{1},\ldots,T_{i},\ldots,T_{n})$ . This equation gives the ratio $(\gamma/\beta)$ , hence gives $\beta_{hrMLH}/\beta_{grMLH}$ . So, instead of the direct process, i.e. Equations (26) and (27), there is another process; computing first $\gamma=\beta_{hrMLH}$ by Equation (33), then computing $(\gamma/\beta)$ by Equation (35), and $\beta=\beta_{grMLH}=\beta_{hrMLH}/\gamma/\beta$ .

## 10.3 THE grMLH HYPOTHESIS

Now, consider a random sample consisting of n observations: $t_{1}, \ldots, t_{2}, \ldots, t_{n}$ . The absolute likelihood function $L_{t}$ of this sample is

$$
L _ {t} = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {i = n} \left[ \frac {\gamma}{\mu} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma - 1} \exp \left(- \frac {t _ {i} ^ {\gamma}}{\mu}\right) \right]\tag{36}
$$

The usual hypothesis is $\partial L_{t}/\partial\mu = 0$ and $\partial L_{t}/\partial\gamma = 0$ . However, this hypothesis is not correct in case of low n. A direct evidence of discrepancy is given in the main part of this paper. Hence $\theta \neq \mu$ and $\beta \neq \gamma$ . So, a different hypothesis is necessary. Probably, many ways could be taken to introduce a new hypothesis. Discussion is open. We propose here a way consistent with the grMLH hypothesis. Instead of considering only the absolute likelihood function $L_{t}$ , we consider the relative likelihood function $L_{r}$ , with respect to a standard sample $(T_{1}, \ldots, T_{i}, \ldots, T_{n})$ , which absolute likelihood function $L_{T}$ is defined by Equation (28).

The relative likelihood function $(L_{r})$ of the random sample is

$$
L _ {r} = \frac {L _ {t}}{L _ {T}}\tag{37}
$$

The grMLH hypothesis consists in:

$$
\frac {\partial L _ {r}}{\partial \mu} = 0
$$

$$
\frac {\partial L _ {r}}{\partial \gamma} = 0\tag{38}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln (L _ {r}) = (\gamma - 1) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (t _ {i}) - \\ \frac {1}{\mu} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma} - \left[ (\gamma - 1) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) - \frac {1}{\mu} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \right] \end{array}\tag{39}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial \ln (L _ {r})}{\partial \mu} = \frac {1}{\mu^ {2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma} - \frac {1}{\mu^ {2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} = 0\tag{40}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial \ln (L _ {r})}{\partial \gamma} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (t _ {i}) - \frac {1}{\mu} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \ln (t _ {i}) -
$$

$$
\left[ \sum \ln (T _ {i}) - \frac {1}{\mu} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \ln (T _ {i}) \right] = 0\tag{41}
$$

This lead to the relationship between the random sample $(t_i)$ and the standard sample $(T_i)$

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma}\tag{42}
$$

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (t _ {i}) - \frac {1}{\mu} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \ln (t _ {i}) =\tag{43}
$$

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (T _ {i}) - \frac {1}{\mu} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \ln (T _ {i})
$$

If we had supposed $\partial L_{t}/\partial\mu=0$ and $\partial L_{t}/\partial\gamma=0$ , we would have failed to obtain these relationships between the random sample $(t_{i})$ and the standard sample $(T_{i})$ . The standard sample $(T_{1},\ldots,T_{i},\ldots,T_{n})$ has the properties studied in Sections 10.1 and 10.2. The Equations (32) and (33) can be used. The terms containing $T_{i}$ in (32) and in (33) can be replaced by the terms containing $t_{i}$ in (42) and (43), respectively.

$$
\mu = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma}\tag{44}
$$

$$
\frac {1}{\gamma} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (t _ {i}) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma} \ln (t _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\gamma}} = 0\tag{45}
$$

Thus, to obtain the unbiased parameters $(\beta, \theta)$ , we can use alternatively the both methods given in Sections 10.1 and 10.2, either using (35) to compute $(\gamma/\beta)$ , or directly the Equations (46) and (47) derived from (25) and (27). $S_{2}$ is given by Equation (26). These parameters $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ are the same as $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ from the Equations (14) and (15), since $\Phi_{i} = \mathrm{Ln}(1/(1 - F_{i}))$ .

$$
\theta = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}}{\Phi_ {i}} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}}{n S _ {1}} = \alpha^ {\beta}\tag{46}
$$

$$
\frac {S _ {2}}{\beta} + \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln (t _ {i}) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta} \ln (t _ {i})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} ^ {\beta}} = 0\tag{47}
$$

This is the proof, according to hypothesis (38), of the correctness of the Equations (6) and (13) presented in the main part of this paper.
