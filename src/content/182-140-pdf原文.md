## Estimation of the threshold parameter of a wear-out failure period in the case of a three-parameter Weibull distribution

Chuo University, 1-13-27, Kasuga, Bunkyo-ku, Tokyo, 112-8551, Japan Email: stakakaz@gmail.com

Abstract: We aimed to estimate the threshold parameter for the wear-out failure period of a three-parameter Weibull distribution. In this paper, we propose the minimum-variance linear unbiased estimator based on order statistics, which is denoted by $\ ' T _ { B E S T } )$ . In Section 2, we verify the validity of $T _ { B E S T }$ by comparing it with the existing threshold parameter estimator, based on simulation studies of bias and mean squared error (MSE). Our results show that T requires all order statistics, except for the case of an exponential distribution, in which $T _ { B E S T }$ is reduced to an unbiased estimator based on the smallest observation only. In Section 3, by simulation studies, we compare $T _ { B E S T }$ and other estimators known to have good performances. In the simulation results, bias and MSE of $T _ { B E S T }$ were the smallest in most cases. We also show a numerical example to measure fatigue lives in hours of ten bearings from McCool (1974).

Keywords: estimation; threshold parameter; minimum-variance linear unbiased estimator; MVLUE; Weibull distribution.

## 1 Introduction

The Weibull distribution is used most widely in reliability and lifetime studies (Murthy et al., 2004). The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the Weibull distribution is written as follows:

$$
G (x; m, \eta , \gamma) = 1 - \exp \left\{- \left(\frac {x - \gamma}{\eta}\right) ^ {m} \right\},\tag{1}
$$

where $\eta > 0 , m > 0 .$ , and $\gamma <$ x are the scale, shape and threshold parameters, respectively. The probability density function (pdf) is

$$
g (x; m, \eta , \gamma) = \frac {m}{\eta} \left(\frac {x - \gamma}{\eta}\right) ^ {m - 1} \exp \left\{- \left(\frac {x - \gamma}{\eta}\right) ^ {m} \right\}.\tag{2}
$$

When the threshold parameter is unknown, the Weibull distribution of the three parameters does not satisfy the maximum likelihood estimation because the pdf depends on an unknown parameter. Then, the maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) may not exist, even if they do, they may not have classical asymptotic properties. For this reason, alternative methods have been sought in the literature (Smith, 1985; Johnson et al., 1994; Nagatsuka and Kamakura, 2003, 2005; Suzuki et al., 2008; Yamamoto et al., 2010; Nagatsuka et al., 2013). Lawless (1994), Murthy et al. (2004) and Ng et al. (2012) have given detailed descriptions of the various methods used for parameter estimation in a three-parameter Weibull distribution.

In Section 2, we describe existing methods for estimating the three parameters in the Weibull distribution. In Section 3, we propose improved methods for estimating the threshold parameter. As the scale and shape parameters are known, we verify the efficacy of the proposed estimation method compared to existing methods in simulation studies. In Section 4, using a numerical example, we verify the efficacy of the proposed estimation method. Our conclusions are presented in Section 5.

## 2 On the minimum-variance linear unbiased estimator (MVLUE) of the threshold parameter $\gamma$

Let $X _ { 1 } , X _ { 2 } , \ldots , X _ { n }$ be a sample from the three parameters in the Weibull distribution with scale $\eta ,$ , shape $m ,$ and threshold $\gamma$ . For $i = 1 , 2 , \ldots , n \left( n \geq 2 \right)$ , let $X _ { ( i ) }$ denote the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ order statistic, $0 < X _ { ( 1 ) } < X _ { ( 2 ) } < \cdots < X _ { ( n ) } ( X _ { ( i ) } \neq X _ { ( j ) }$ , for some $i \neq j )$

Now suppose that statistics $T _ { 1 }$ and $T _ { 2 }$ are unbiased estimators. Then, the linear combination $c _ { 1 } T _ { 1 } + c _ { 2 } T _ { 2 }$ is also an unbiased estimator ${ \mathrm { i f } } c _ { 1 } + c _ { 2 } = 1$ . The variance of it is written by:

$$
\mathrm{V} \left(c _ {1} T _ {1} + c _ {2} T _ {2}\right) = c _ {1} ^ {2} \mathrm{V} \left(T _ {1}\right) + 2 \left(1 - c _ {1}\right) c _ {1} \operatorname{Cov} \left(T _ {1}, T _ {2}\right) + \left(1 - c _ {1}\right) ^ {2} \mathrm{V} \left(T _ {2}\right),\tag{3}
$$

which is minimised by

$$
c _ {1} = \frac {\mathrm{V} (T _ {2}) - \operatorname{Cov} (T _ {1} , T _ {2})}{\mathrm{V} (T _ {1}) + \mathrm{V} (T _ {2}) - 2 \operatorname{Cov} (T _ {1} , T _ {2})},\tag{4}
$$

$$
c _ {2} = \frac {\mathrm{V} (T _ {1}) - \operatorname{Cov} (T _ {1} , T _ {2})}{\mathrm{V} (T _ {1}) + \mathrm{V} (T _ {2}) - 2 \operatorname{Cov} (T _ {1} , T _ {2})}.\tag{5}
$$

The minimised value is given by

$$
\min _ {c _ {1} + c _ {2} = 1} \mathrm{V} \left(c _ {1} T _ {1} + c _ {2} T _ {2}\right) = \frac {\mathrm{V} \left(T _ {1}\right) \mathrm{V} \left(T _ {2}\right) - \left\{\operatorname{Cov} \left(T _ {1} , T _ {2}\right) \right\} ^ {2}}{\mathrm{V} \left(T _ {1}\right) + \mathrm{V} \left(T _ {2}\right) - 2 \operatorname{Cov} \left(T _ {1} , T _ {2}\right)}.\tag{6}
$$

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, this is obviously smaller than or equal to $\mathrm { V } \left( T _ { 1 } \right)$ and $\mathrm { ~ V ~ } ( T _ { 2 } )$ . Let us generalise this for the case of an unbiased estimator $T _ { 1 } , T _ { 2 } , \dots , T _ { k }$ . Let the covariance matrix be $V = \left( v _ { i , j } \right)$ where $v _ { i , j } = \mathrm { C o v } \left( T _ { i } , \ T _ { j } \right)$ . Then, the MVLUE is written as:

$$
T = \frac {1}{\mathbf {1} ^ {\prime} V ^ {- 1} \mathbf {1}} \mathbf {1} ^ {\prime} V ^ {- 1} \left( \begin{array}{c} T _ {1} \\ \vdots \\ T _ {k} \end{array} \right),\tag{7}
$$

where $\mathbf { l } ^ { \prime } = \left( 1 \cdots 1 \right)$

In the three-parameter Weibull distribution, we have

$$
E [ X _ {(1)} ] = \gamma + \eta n ^ {- \frac {1}{m}} \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right),\tag{8}
$$

$$
E [ X _ {(2)} ] = \gamma + \eta n (n - 1) \left\{(n - 1) ^ {- \frac {1}{m} - 1} - n ^ {- \frac {1}{m} - 1} \right\} \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right),\tag{9}
$$

$$
V [ X _ {(1)} ] = \eta^ {2} n ^ {- \frac {2}{m}} \left\{\Gamma \left(1 + \frac {2}{m}\right) - \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right) ^ {2} \right\},\tag{10}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} V [ X _ {(2)} ] = \eta^ {2} n (n - 1) \bigg [ \left\{(n - 1) ^ {- \frac {2}{m} - 1} - n ^ {- \frac {2}{m} - 1} \right\} \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {2}{m}\right) \\ - n (n - 1) \left\{(n - 1) ^ {- \frac {1}{m} - 1} - n ^ {- \frac {1}{m} - 1} \right\} ^ {2} \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right) ^ {2} \bigg ], \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname{Cov} (X _ {(1)}, X _ {(2)}) = \eta^ {2} \bigg [ \frac {m n}{m + 1} (n - 1) ^ {- \frac {2}{m} - 1} \Gamma \left(2 + \frac {2}{m}\right) \\ \qquad \times_ {2} F _ {1} \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}, 2 + \frac {2}{m}; 2 + \frac {1}{m}; \frac {1}{1 - n}\right) - n ^ {1 - \frac {1}{m}} \\ \qquad \times (n - 1) \left\{(n - 1) ^ {- \frac {1}{m} - 1} - n ^ {- \frac {1}{m} - 1} \right\} \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right) ^ {2} \bigg ]. \end{array}\tag{12}
$$

Then, we obtain two unbiased estimators $T _ { 1 }$ and $T _ { 2 }$ as follows

$$
T _ {1} = X _ {(1)} - \eta n ^ {- \frac {1}{m}} \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right),\tag{13}
$$

$$
T _ {2} = X _ {(2)} - \eta n (n - 1) \left\{(n - 1) ^ {- \frac {1}{m} - 1} - n ^ {- \frac {1}{m} - 1} \right\} \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right).\tag{14}
$$

Note that $T _ { 1 }$ is based on Nagatsuka et al. (2013). Generally, we have the following:

$$
E [ X _ {(i)} ] = \gamma + \eta \frac {n ! \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right)}{(i - 1) ! (n - i) !} \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} \frac {(- 1) ^ {i - 1 - j}}{(n - j) ^ {1 + \frac {1}{m}}} \binom {i - 1} {j},\tag{15}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} V [ X _ {(i)} ] = \eta^ {2} \frac {n !}{(i - 1) ! (n - i) !} \bigg [ \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {2}{m}\right) \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} \frac {(- 1) ^ {i - 1 - j}}{(n - j) ^ {1 + \frac {2}{m}}} \binom {i - 1} {j} \\ - \frac {n ! \Gamma \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}\right) ^ {2}}{(i - 1) ! (n - i) !} \left\{\sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} \frac {(- 1) ^ {i - 1 - j}}{(n - j) ^ {1 + \frac {1}{m}}} \binom {i - 1} {j} \right\} ^ {2} \bigg ]. \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

when $i < j$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname{Cov} [ X _ {(i)}, X _ {(j)} ] = \eta^ {2} \bigg [ \frac {n ! \Gamma \left(2 + \frac {2}{m}\right)}{(i - 1) ! (j - i - 1) ! (n - j) !} \frac {m}{m + 1} \\ \sum_ {s = 0} ^ {i - 1} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {j - i - 1} \binom {i - 1} {s} \binom {j - i - 1} {t} \frac {(- 1) ^ {j - s - t}}{(n - i - t) ^ {2 + \frac {2}{m}}} \\ \times_ {2} F _ {1} \left(1 + \frac {1}{m}, 2 + \frac {2}{m}; 2 + \frac {1}{m}; - \frac {i - s + t}{n - i - t}\right) \bigg ] \\ - (E [ X _ {(i)} ] - \gamma) (E [ X _ {(j)} ] - \gamma). \end{array}\tag{17}
$$

when $n = 5 , m = 5$ , and $\eta = 1$ , we have an MVLUE $T _ { B E S T }$ as

$$
\begin{array}{l} T _ {B E S T} = 0. 1 5 5 8 (X _ {(1)} - 0. 6 6 5 5) + 0. 1 4 0 7 (X _ {(2)} - 0. 8 1 7 3) \\ \qquad + 0. 1 7 3 0 (X _ {(3)} - 0. 9 2 5 7) + 0. 2 1 7 0 (X _ {(4)} - 1. 0 2 7 8) \\ \qquad + 0. 3 1 3 5 (X _ {(5)} - 1. 1 5 4 5). \end{array}
$$

Generally, $T _ { B E S T }$ is written as

$$
T _ {B E S T} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} c _ {(i)} (X _ {(i)} - B _ {(i)})\tag{18}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l} c _ {(i)} = \frac {\boldsymbol {I} ^ {\prime} S ^ {- 1}}{\boldsymbol {I} ^ {\prime} S ^ {- 1} \boldsymbol {I}}, \boldsymbol {I} ^ {\prime} = (1 \dots 1), S = (\operatorname{Cov} (X _ {(i)}, X _ {(j)})), \\ B _ {(i)} = \eta \frac {n ! \Gamma (1 + \frac {1}{m})}{(i - 1) ! (n - i) !} \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} \frac {(- 1) ^ {i - 1 - j}}{(n - j) ^ {1 + \frac {1}{m}}} \binom {i - 1} {j}. \end{array}
$$

We obtain the following covariance matrix:

$$
\begin{array}{l} S = (\operatorname{Cov} (X _ {(i)}, X _ {(j)})) _ {i, j = 1, \dots , 5} \\ = \left( \begin{array}{c} 0. 0 2 3 2 3   0. 0 1 1 5 2   0. 0 0 7 2 7   0. 0 0 4 9 0   0. 0 0 3 1 7 \\ 0. 0 1 5 6 3   0. 0 0 9 9 6   0. 0 0 6 7 5   0. 0 0 4 3 9 \\ 0. 0 1 3 0 4   0. 0 0 8 9 2   0. 0 0 5 8 4 \\ 0. 0 1 2 4 8   0. 0 0 8 2 7 \\ \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \end{array} \right). \end{array}
$$

Thus, we obtain the variance of $T _ { B E S T } , ~ V ( T _ { B E S T } ) = 0 . 0 0 8 5 6$ . Compared to $V ( T _ { 1 } ) = 0 . 0 2 3 2 3$ , the variance of $T _ { B E S T }$ is smaller.

In this case, the variance of the unbiased estimator $X _ { ( 4 ) } - 1 . 0 2 7 8$ is the minimum variance 0.01248. This means that $X _ { ( 4 ) }$ is the better estimator of the threshold parameter $\gamma$ than the smallest sample $X _ { ( 1 ) }$ , and also $X _ { ( 2 ) } , X _ { ( 3 ) }$ , and $X _ { ( 5 ) }$ . There is the idea that the smallest sample is the most important for estimating threshold, however, the results shown are contrary to this notion and thus very interesting.

## 3 New method for estimating the threshold parameter

As m and $\eta$ increase, parameter estimations become more difficult. Based on Nagatsuka and Kamakura (2003), the threshold parameter estimator is defined as follows:

$$
\hat {\gamma} = \bar {X} - \hat {\eta} \bar {G},\tag{19}
$$

where

$$
\bar {X} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} X _ {(i)},
$$

$$
\bar {G} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} G _ {(i)},
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} {G _ {(i)} = \left(\log \frac {1}{1 - \hat {p} _ {i}}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{\hat {m}}},} \\ {\hat {p} _ {i} = \frac {i - 0 . 5}{n},} \end{array}
$$

ηˆ and mˆ are estimators of $\cdot _ { \eta }$ and $m ,$ respectively. In this paper, $\hat { \gamma }$ is referred to as the statistic R. We generated random numbers from the three-parameter Weibull distribution $( m = 3 , 5 ,$ $\eta = 1 , 2$ and $\gamma = 4 )$ in sample sizes $n = 5 , 5 0$ , 100. As the scale and shape parameters are known, we were able to obtain 100,000 estimations $T _ { 1 } , T _ { 2 } , T _ { 3 } , T _ { 4 } , T _ { 5 } , R , T _ { 1 }$ , and $T _ { B E S T }$ of the threshold parameter $\gamma$ . We summarise the simulation results of T , T , T , T , $T _ { 5 }$ using bias and mean squared error (MSE) in Table 1 $( n = 5 , m = 5 , \eta = 1 , \gamma = 4 )$ , and the simulation results of $R , T _ { 1 } ,$ , and $T _ { B E S T }$ using bias and MSE in Tables 2–4. Details of the simulation results are shown for several cases. Table 5 shows the coefficients of $B _ { ( i ) }$ and $c _ { ( i ) }$ in $T _ { B E S T }$ . Histograms of the estimated values of $\cdot _ { \gamma }$ used by $R , T _ { 1 }$ , and $T _ { B E S T }$ are presented in Figure 1.

## 5 Conclusions

We discussed the MVLUE of the threshold parameter $\gamma ,$ and proposed the statistic $T _ { B E S T }$ to estimate this. The closest value to the threshold parameter γ was the smallest sample value $X _ { ( 1 ) }$ , suggesting the use of $X _ { ( 1 ) }$ in creating the estimator, namely, $T _ { 1 }$ . However, upon further consideration of $T _ { B E S T } ,$ particularly for the case involving a sample size of5, we know that the other samples $X _ { ( i ) } ( i = 2 , 3 , \cdots )$ also include important information regarding the estimation of $\gamma .$ . The scale parameter $\eta = 1$ in $T _ { B E S T }$ strongly affects threshold parameter estimates. In simulation studies, our results showed that a scale parameter of $\eta = 1 , 2$ provided a better estimate in sample size of $n = 5 , 5 0$ , 100 than other statistical approaches and was superior to that of other cases involving $\eta > 2 .$ , and other shape parameters m.
