# Parameter estimation of three-parameter Weibull distribution by hybrid gray genetic algorithm with modified maximum likelihood method with small samples

Jianyi Gu<sup>1</sup>, Xiangwei Kong<sup>1,2,3</sup>, Jin Guo<sup>1</sup>, Haochen Qi<sup>1</sup> and Zinan Wang

<sup>1</sup>School of Mechanical Engineering and Automation, Northeastern University, Shenyang, Liaoning 110819, China, <sup>2</sup>Key Laboratory of Vibration and Control of Aero-Propulsion System, Ministry of Education, Northeastern University, Shenyang 110819, China, <sup>3</sup>Liaoning Province Key Laboratory of Multidisciplinary Design Optimization of Complex Equipment, Northeastern University, Shenyang 110819, China

Abstract Due to the increased cost and reliability, only few life failure data can be gathered. Therefore, it is significant to improve the parameter estimation accuracy of Weibull distribution with small samples. In this study, a Weibull parameter estimation method based on hybrid gray genetic algorithm with modified maximum likelihood method (HGLM) is proposed. In the HGLM, the solution model for the location parameter is established by using gray genetic algorithm. On the basis of the location parameter, the shape and scale parameters are acquired with improved maximum likelihood method. Then, a full Monte-Carlo sampling simulation study is conducted to compare the parameter estimation accuracy and fitting of the HGLM with three existing methods. Results show the HGLM can accurately and stably estimate the Weibull parameters with small samples. Finally, two cases are analyzed to demonstrate the application of the HGLM method and the feasibility of sample expansion is also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Reliability is the probability that a system will perform the required function under specified conditions, which must be considered throughout the life cycle of products [1, 2]. However, due to increasing costs and product reliability, fewer life data can be collected [3, 4]. This results in large deviations from existing parameter estimation methods. In this background, it is crucial to propose new methods to improve the estimation accuracy and stability of the Weibull distribution with small samples.

Typically, the product lifetime can be viewed as random variable which obeys a certain type of distribution. In this paper, we assume that the lifetime data follows Weibull distribution, which has been used in reliability engineering. The Weibull distribution was first proposed by Waloddi Weibull of Sweden [5], who used it in his first studies of material strength. The Weibull distribution is one of the most popular models for analyzing data in reliability, engineering, and many other fields [6, 7]. The main types of Weibull distributions include the three-parameter Weibull distribution and the two-parameter Weibull distribution. Numerous studies have shown that the three-parameter Weibull distribution provides better fitting accuracy than two-parameter Weibull distribution [8]. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the three-parameter Weibull distribution is

$$
F (t, \gamma , m, \eta) = 1 - e ^ {- \left(\frac {t - \gamma}{\eta}\right) ^ {m}}\tag{1}
$$

where $\gamma , m$ and <sub>η</sub> are the location, shape and scale parameters, $t > \gamma \geq 0 , m > 0 , \eta > 0$ The probability density function (PDF) is

$$
f (t, \gamma , m, \eta) = \frac {m}{\eta} \left(\frac {t - \gamma}{\eta}\right) ^ {m - 1} e ^ {- \left(\frac {t - \gamma}{\eta}\right) ^ {m}}.\tag{2}
$$

Due to the wide range of applications of the Weibull distribution, researchers have proposed many statistical methods to estimate the Weibull parameters. The maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and the method of moments (MoM) are more commonly used methods for parameter estimation. However, these methods have certain shortcomings: Cousineau [9] stated that when the shape parameter $\mathsf m < 1$ , the MLE do not exist, and for $1 < \mathsf { m } < 2 ,$ the MLE exist but do not satisfy the regularity conditions. Similarly, the MoM is not efficient when the regularity conditions are satisfied [10].

To solve these problems, researchers introduced many models, such as generalized spacing estimation (GSE), modified maximum likelihood estimation I (MMLE-I), modified maximum likelihood estimation II (MMLE-II), Tiku's modified maximum likelihood estimation (TMMLE), least squares estimation (LSE), weighted least squares estimation (WLSE), and percentile counting estimation (PCE) [11, 12].

Although the above methods have made contributions to the field of parameter estimation for the Weibull distribution, the traditional methods still have some disadvantages with small samples, such as difficulty in convergence, nonconvergence, and hard to determine the prior distribution, etc. Ng et al. [13] investigated the estimation of parameters for three-parameter Weibull distribution based on progressively type-II right censored sample, and different estimation procedures for complete samples were generalized to the case of progressively censored data. They also proposed a censored estimation method to obtain reliable initial estimates for iterative procedure, which provides a good working idea for this study.

In this paper, the hybrid gray genetic algorithm with modified maximum likelihood method (HGLM) is proposed to estimate the three Weibull parameters with small samples. The core method of the HGLM is gray genetic algorithm, which is a branch of gray system theory. The gray system theory was firstly developed by Deng in 1982, which has been widely used in the field of deep learning and fault diagnosis field [14-16]. And this algorithm is a very effective prediction method in case of less data [17]. There are two main models of gray genetic algorithm, one is the first-order gray differential model GM (1, 1) and the other is the gray Bernoulli model GBM (1, 1) [18]. The GM (1, 1) model has been the focus of research because of its mathematical simplicity [19-21]. Researchers are also constantly revising the GM model. Zhou et al. [22] used particle swarm optimization technique to optimize the parameters of gray model. In 2010, Hsin [23] introduced an optimization mechanism to improve the prediction accuracy of the gray model. Wang et al. [24-26] formulated the parameter optimization of the NGBM (1, 1) model as a combinatorial optimization problem and solved it collectively using advanced technologies. More importantly, they optimized the initial conditions and considered the results of the weighted analysis. Ma et al. [27-29] used different algorithms for power exponential optimization and extended GM (1, 1) to the multidimensional case, which leads to applications in areas such as predicting natural gas consumption. Until today, the GM (1, 1) gray model is still the focus of research and widely used to solve different real parameters simulation problems.

It is worth noting that when the sample size is small, the bias of estimating the Weibull distribution parameters can be large. Therefore, improving the estimation accuracy of Weibull distribution with small samples has become an urgent problem. Under these backgrounds, we propose the hybrid gray genetic algorithm with modified maximum likelihood method (HGLM). The main idea of this method is to establish the solution model for the location parameter by using gray genetic algorithm, and then use the modified maximum likelihood method for solving the scale and shape parameters. Compared to the traditional methods, the advantages of using the HGLM are as follows:

1) As an update to the parameter estimation method for three-parameter Weibull distribution, it requires less modeling information, has high modeling accuracy, and can achieve less biased estimation of the Weibull distribution.

2) It is suitable for solving systems with short time series, few statistics and incomplete information, which has high simulation accuracy and stability.

3) It can be used to expand the samples and improve the poor-information situation by accumulating new sequences which show the inherent patterns of the original data.

4) Its parameters solutions are always unique and do not re quire a priori distributional information.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Sec. 2, the existing methods and the HGLM are presented, and the five method evaluation parameters are further described. In Sec. 3, the comparisons between the HGLM and existing methods are made in terms of accuracy and stability. Two real data examples are illustrated in Sec. 4. The feasibility of the HGLM for sample expansion is discussed in Sec. 5. Finally, the conclusion is provided in Sec. 6.

## 2. Parameter estimation of the Weibull distribution

## 2.1 Existing methods

## 2.1.1 Maximum likelihood method (MLE)

The MLE obtain parameter estimation results by maximizing the likelihood function, which requires solving a system of iterative equations to determine the shape, scale, and location parameters of the Weibull distribution. The log-likelihood function for the MLE is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln [ L (m, \eta , \gamma | X) ] = - N m \ln (\eta) + N \ln (m) \\ + (m - 1) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \ln \left(x _ {i} - \eta\right) - \eta^ {- m} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left(x _ {i} - \eta\right) ^ {m}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

After defining ln (L), in order to avoid solving complex iterative system of equations, the shape and location parameters can be obtained by solving two minimizing systems of equations. According to this approach, γ and m are computed by

$$
\left(\frac {1}{m} + \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \log (x _ {i} - \gamma) - \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \log (x _ {i} - \gamma) (x _ {i} - \gamma) ^ {m}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} (x _ {i} - \gamma) ^ {m}}\right) ^ {2}\tag{4}
$$

$$
\left(\frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \frac {1}{x _ {i} - \gamma} \times \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left(x _ {i} - \gamma\right) ^ {m}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left(x _ {i} - \gamma\right) ^ {m - 1}} - \frac {m}{m - 1}\right) ^ {2}.\tag{5}
$$

The equation for solving the scale parameter is

$$
\eta_ {M L E} = \sqrt [ m ]{\frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left(x _ {i} - \gamma\right) ^ {m}}.\tag{6}
$$

The three parameters of the Weibull distribution can be obtained by solving the above equations, but this method is more difficult to solve and results obtained are biased estimates.

## 2.1.2 Correlation coefficient method and least squares method (CCMLSE)

The CCMLSE is a hybrid method with high accuracy, which has been widely used in life prediction of Weibull distribution. In this section, the correlation coefficient method (CCM) is used to estimate the location parameter, and then the shape and scale parameters are estimated by using the least square estimation (LSE). If $t _ { 1 } , t _ { 2 } \cdots t _ { N }$ are the failure times, and correlation coefficient can be calculated as

$$
r _ {x y} = \frac {l _ {x y}}{\sqrt {l _ {x x} l _ {y y}}}\tag{7}
$$

where the estimators $l _ { x x } , l _ { y y }$ and $l _ { x y }$ are

$$
l _ {x x} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left[ \ln (t _ {i} - \gamma) \right] ^ {2} - \frac {1}{N} \left[ \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \ln (t _ {i} - \gamma) \right] ^ {2}\tag{8}
$$

$$
l _ {x y} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \ln (t _ {i} - \gamma) y _ {i} - \frac {1}{N} \left[ \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \ln (t _ {i} - \gamma) \right] \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} y _ {i}\right)\tag{9}
$$

$$
l _ {y y} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left(y _ {i} ^ {2} - 2 y _ {i} \bar {y} + \bar {y} ^ {2}\right) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} y _ {i} ^ {2} - N \bar {y} ^ {2}.\tag{10}
$$

According to the definition of correlation coefficient, when $\left| r _ { x y } \right|$ is closer to 1, the effect of regression is better. Therefore, the maximum value of the correlation coefficient $\left| r _ { x y } \right|$ as

$$
\frac {\mathrm{d} \left[ r _ {x y} (\gamma) \right] ^ {2}}{\mathrm{d} \gamma} = 2 r _ {x y} ^ {2} \cdot \left(\frac {1}{l _ {x y}} \cdot \frac {\mathrm{d} l _ {x y}}{\mathrm{d} \gamma} - \frac {1}{2 l _ {x x}} \cdot \frac {\mathrm{d} l _ {x x}}{\mathrm{d} \gamma}\right) = 0.\tag{11}
$$

Let the intermediate parameters $l _ { \mathrm { x 0 } }$ and $l _ { y 0 }$ replace the parts of Eq. (11) are

$$
l _ {\mathrm{x} 0} = - \frac {\mathrm{d} l _ {x x}}{2 \mathrm{d} \gamma} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \frac {\ln (t _ {i} - \gamma)}{t _ {i} - \gamma} - \frac {\left[ \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \ln (t _ {i} - \gamma) \right]}{N \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} t _ {i} - \gamma\right)}\tag{12}
$$

$$
l _ {y 0} = - \frac {\mathrm{d} l _ {x y}}{\mathrm{d} \gamma} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \frac {y _ {i}}{t _ {i} - \gamma} - \frac {1}{N} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} y _ {i}\right) \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \frac {1}{t _ {i} - \gamma}\right).\tag{13}
$$

Substituting Eqs. (12) and (13) into Eq. (11) gives $\mathsf E ( \hat { \gamma } )$ is

$$
E (\hat {\gamma}) = \frac {l _ {x 0}}{l _ {x x}} - \frac {l _ {y 0}}{l _ {x y}} = 0\tag{14}
$$

where $\hat { \gamma }$ is the predicted value of $\gamma$ , and when $\hat { \gamma } < \gamma$ $E \left( \hat { \gamma } \right) > 0$ . Conversely $E \left( \hat { \gamma } \right) > 0$ . According to this principle, the programming calculation can be carried out as follows:

1) Let $\hat { \gamma } = 0 ,$ , and calculate $E ( 0 )$ by substituting Eq. (14). We let $t _ { \boldsymbol { m i n } } = m i n \{ t _ { 1 } , t _ { 2 } \cdots t _ { N } \}$ If $E ( 0 ) \leq 0$ , take $\hat { \gamma } = 0$ . If $E ( 0 ) > 0$ , then divide the interva $\left( 0 , t _ { \mathrm { m i n } } \right)$ in half into two intervals, and let $\hat { \gamma } = t _ { \mathrm { m i d } }$

2) Compute $E \big ( t _ { \mathrm { m i d } } \big )$ by substituting Eq. (14). If $E \big ( t _ { \mathrm { m i d } } \big ) < 0 .$ then $\gamma$ must lie in the interval $\left( 0 , t _ { \mathrm { m i d } } \right) . \mathrm { } \forall \ : \ : E \left( t _ { \mathrm { m i d } } \right) > 0 ,$ , then must lie in the interval $\left( t _ { \mathrm { m i d } } , t _ { \mathrm { m i n } } \right)$ . In either case, the original interval can be halved. Then after many cycles the $\hat { \gamma }$ can be found according to the required accuracy.

Then, the shape and scale parameters are solved by using the LSE. The Weibull distribution is expressed in linear form as

$$
\ln \left[ \ln \left(\frac {1}{1 - F _ {N} \left(t _ {i}\right)}\right) \right] = m l n (t _ {i} - \gamma) - m l n \eta\tag{15}
$$

where $F _ { _ { N } } { \big ( } t _ { i } { \big ) }$ is the empirical distribution function, which can be calculated by

$$
F _ {N} \left(t _ {i}\right) = \frac {i - 0 . 3}{N + 0 . 4}.\tag{16}
$$

The regression linear equation to be fitted by the LSE is

$$
y = a x + b.\tag{17}
$$

Let $x _ { i } = \ln \left( t _ { i } - \gamma \right)$ and $y _ { i } = \ln \left[ \ln \left( 1 / 1 - F _ { _ N } \left( t _ { i } \right) \right) \right]$ , and the shape and scale parameters can be calculated as

$$
\hat {m} = b\tag{18}
$$

$$
\hat {\eta} = \exp \left(- \frac {a}{b}\right).\tag{19}
$$

Since the solution process is complex, it must be solved by numerical computation or computer programming.

## 2.1.3 Greenwood PWM method (GPWM)

The Greenwood PWM was first proposed by Greenwood et al. [30]. This method has become a standard procedure for estimating certain statistical distribution parameters in various fields [31]. The GPWM of a random variable with CDF is

$$
M _ {p, r, s} = \int_ {0} ^ {1} [ x (F) ] ^ {p} F ^ {r} (1 - F) ^ {s} \cdot \mathrm{d} F\tag{20}
$$

where $p , r , s \in N$ . In this paper, we take $r = 0 , p = 1$ , thus

$$
M _ {0} = \gamma + \eta \Gamma_ {m}\tag{21}
$$

$$
M _ {1} = \frac {\gamma}{2} + \frac {\eta}{2 ^ {1 + \frac {1}{m}}} \Gamma_ {m}\tag{22}
$$

$$
M _ {2} = \frac {\gamma}{3} + \frac {\eta}{3 ^ {1 + \frac {1}{m}}} \Gamma_ {m}.\tag{23}
$$

It is necessary to know the value of the GPWM $\mathsf { M } _ { 0 } ,$ M<sub>1</sub>, $\mathsf { M } _ { 2 }$ to solve the Eqs. (21)-(23). For this reason, we need to order the experimental data. Let $x _ { 1 } < x _ { 2 } < \cdots < x _ { \mathrm { { N } } }$ be the order sample. Then the three GPWMs M<sub>s</sub> [32] are given by:

$$
M _ {0} = \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} x _ {i}\tag{24}
$$

$$
M _ {1} = \frac {1}{N (N - 1)} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N - 1} (N - i) x _ {i}\tag{25}
$$

$$
M _ {2} = \frac {1}{N (N - 1) (N - 2)} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N - 2} (N - i) (N - i - 1) x _ {i}.\tag{26}
$$

By substituting the estimators given by Eqs. (24)–(26) in the Eqs. (21)–(23) the value of the parameters are determined.

## 2.2 Proposed method

2.2.1 Theory background of gray genetic algorithm The key of the gray genetic algorithm is data accumulation, through which the overall attributes embedded in disordered data can be visualized. The accumulation formulas are

$$
x ^ {(0)} = \left[ x ^ {(0)} (1), x ^ {(0)} (2), \dots , x ^ {(0)} (N) \right]
$$

$$
x ^ {(1)} (k) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} x ^ {(0)} (i).\tag{27}
$$

(28)

As the main model of the gray genetic algorithm, the GM (1, 1) is a differential equation for discrete sequences as

$$
\frac {d x}{d t} + \alpha x = u.\tag{29}
$$

According to the definition of derivative, Eq. (29) can be expressed as

$$
\lim _ {\Delta t \rightarrow 0} \frac {x (t + \Delta t) - x (t)}{\Delta t} + \alpha x = u.\tag{30}
$$

When $\Delta t = 1$ , the approximate equation for the differential can be expressed by

$$
x (t + 1) - x (t) = \frac {\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \Delta [ x (k + 1) ].\tag{31}
$$

The above equations involve the average of the two moments, so the independent variable $x ^ { ( 1 ) }$ can be replaced by

$$
z ^ {(i + 1)} (i) = \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(i + 1)} (i + 1) + x ^ {(i + 1)} (i) \right]\tag{32}
$$

$$
x ^ {(1)} (k) = \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (k + 1) + x ^ {(1)} (k) \right].\tag{33}
$$

Then, we can derive

$$
\Delta^ {(1)} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (k + 1) \right] = x ^ {(1)} (k + 1) - x ^ {(1)} (k) = x ^ {(0)} (k + 1)
$$

$$
\Delta^ {(1)} [ x (k + 1) ] + a [ x (k + 1) ] = u.\tag{34}
$$

(35)

By associating Eqs. (29)-(35), the differential prediction model of the gray genetic algorithm is

$$
x ^ {(0)} (k + 1) = a \left[ - \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (k) + x ^ {(1)} (k + 1) \right] \right] + u.\tag{36}
$$

Write the Eq. (36) as matrix expression as

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} x ^ {(0)} (2) \\ x ^ {(0)} (3) \\ \vdots \\ x ^ {(0)} (N) \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} - \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (2) + x ^ {(1)} (1) \right] & 1 \\ - \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (3) + x ^ {(1)} (2) \right] & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ - \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (N) + x ^ {(1)} (N - 1) \right] & 1 \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} a \\ u \end{array} \right].\tag{37}
$$

Then the Eq. (37) can be simply written as

$$
y = \left[ x ^ {(0)} (2), x ^ {(0)} (3), \dots , x ^ {(0)} (N) \right] ^ {T}\tag{38}
$$

$$
B = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} - \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (2) + x ^ {(1)} (1) \right] & 1 \\ - \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (3) + x ^ {(1)} (2) \right] & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ - \frac {1}{2} \left[ x ^ {(1)} (N) + x ^ {(1)} (N - 1) \right] & 1 \end{array} \right]\tag{39}
$$

$$
U = \left[ \begin{array}{c} a \\ u \end{array} \right]
$$

$$
y = B U.\tag{40}
$$

(41)

Suppose $\hat { U }$ is the least square solution of $U$ , which satisfied

$$
(Y - B \hat {U}) ^ {2} = \min _ {U} (Y - B U) ^ {2}.\tag{42}
$$

Let $\beta = \hat { U }$ and $Q \big ( \beta \big ) = \rvert \rvert Y - B \beta \lvert \rvert ^ { 2 }$ , then finding the least square solution of $U$ transforms into finding the minimum value of $\mathcal Q ( \beta )$ . The partial derivative of $\mathcal Q ( \beta )$ on $\beta$ is

$$
\left| B ^ {T} B \right| = \left| \begin{array}{c c} \sum_ {i = 2} ^ {N} \left[ z ^ {(1)} (i) \right] ^ {2} & - \sum_ {i = 2} ^ {N} z ^ {(1)} (i) \\ - \sum_ {i = 2} ^ {N} z ^ {(1)} (i) & n - 1 \end{array} \right| \neq 0.\tag{43}
$$

We let $\hat { U } = \left( B ^ { T } B \right) ^ { - 1 } B ^ { T } Y$ . Therefore, the ultimate differentia GM prediction model of the gray genetic algorithm is

$$
\hat {x} ^ {(1)} (k + 1) = \left[ x ^ {(1)} (1) - \frac {\hat {u}}{\hat {a}} \right] e ^ {- a k} + \frac {\hat {u}}{\hat {a}}.\tag{44}
$$

2.2.2 Hybrid gray genetic algorithm with modified maximum likelihood method (HGLM)

Compared to the two-parameter Weibull distribution, the three-parameter Weibull distribution introduces location parameter, which leads to a more complex parameter estimation process. If the location parameter can be predicted first, then with the help of parameter estimators depended on the location parameter, the estimation of the three Weibull parameters can be achieved. The Weibull parameter estimation methods, such as MLE, enable unbiased estimation of shape and scale parameters. If the location parameter is unbiased, the three parameters of the Weibull distribution can potentially be unbiased. However, most of the estimation methods, such as GPWM, for the location parameter are biased.

Consequently, the main idea of estimating the Weibull parameters in this paper is to apply the model given in Eq. (44) to the prediction of the location parameter, and use the modified MLE method to propose the scale and shape parameters. First, let $R \left( x \right) = 1 - F \left( x \right)$ . Then transform the Eq. (1) as

$$
x = \eta e ^ {\frac {1}{m} \ln \ln \frac {1}{R (x)}} + \gamma .\tag{45}
$$

Let $t _ { i } = - l n l n { \bigl ( } 1 / R { \bigl ( } x { \bigr ) } { \bigr ) }$ , the Eq. (45) can be written as

$$
x _ {t _ {i}} = \eta e ^ {- \frac {1}{m} t _ {i}} + \gamma .\tag{46}
$$

Note $\eta = c \ , \ 1 / m = a \ , \ \gamma = b$ , we get $x = c e ^ { - a t _ { i } } + b$ , This form is as same as the solution of the GM(1,1) model, and the location parameter has been separated. Thus, we can use the gray genetic algorithm to solve the location parameter of Weibull distribution. We define $z ^ { ( 1 ) }$ to be the mean values of the immediate neighbours of $X ^ { ( 1 ) }$ , the equation as

$$
z ^ {(1)} (t _ {i}) = \lambda x ^ {(1)} (t _ {i}) + (1 - \lambda) x ^ {(1)} (t _ {i + 1})\tag{47}
$$

where λ is the weighting coefficient, $\lambda \in [ 0 ,$ , 1], and the gray genetic algorithm can be used to determine suitable λ for different raw data sequences. Since the simulation accuracy of the gray genetic algorithm directly affects the result of $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { N } \biggr | x ^ { ( 0 ) } \left( k \right) - \hat { x } ^ { ( 0 ) j } \left( k \right) \biggr | } \end{array}$ . Referring to the evaluation principle that higher fitness represents a better individual in genetic algorithm. The fitness function of each weighting coefficient $\lambda ( j )$ is determined as $f [ \lambda ( j ) ]$ , which can be expressed as

$$
f [ \lambda (j) ] = \frac {(1 - \lambda) ^ {2}}{1 - \lambda + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {N} \left| x ^ {(0)} (k) - \hat {x} ^ {(0) j} (k) \right|}.\tag{48}
$$

We substituting the time variable containing the reliability information into the gray genetic algorithm, which can be obtained as the following equations

$$
\frac {x ^ {(0)} \left(t _ {i + 1}\right)}{t _ {i + 1} - t _ {i}} + a \left[ x ^ {(1)} \left(t _ {i + 1}\right) \right] = u\tag{49}

## 2.3 Method evaluation parameters

## 2.3.1 Bias and ARE

The Bias and ARE are the most direct parameters for evaluating the accuracy of parameter estimation, and the small values of them represent the high accuracy of the method. The equations for Bias and ARE can be given by

$$
B i a s = \left| x ^ {*} - x \right|\tag{56}
$$

$$
A R E = \frac {\left| x ^ {*} - x \right|}{x ^ {*}}\tag{57}
$$

where $x ^ { * }$ is the true value of the parameter and x represents the parameter estimate.

## 2.3.2 Akaike information criterion (AIC)

The Akaike information criterion (AIC) links the relationship between the estimated and true distribution to the MLE. which can be used to measure the goodness of method fitting [33-35]. When the AIC is small, it means that the method fits the discrete parameters well. The AIC value is calculated by

$$
A I C = - 2 \ln (L) + 2 k\tag{58}
$$

$$
\ln (L) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \ln f (x _ {i})\tag{59}
$$

where ln(L) is the value of the maximized log-likelihood, k is the number of estimated parameters (k = 3 for 3P-Weibull distribution and k = 2 for 2P-Weibull distribution).

## 2.3.3 RMSE and NRSME

The root mean square error (RMSE) and normalized root mean square error (NRMSE) are also commonly used in statistics. These two parameters are well able to evaluate the effect of linear regression, and the equations are given by

$$
R M S E = \sqrt {\frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left[ F _ {N} (t _ {i}) - \hat {F} _ {N} (t _ {i}) \right] ^ {2}}{N}}\tag{60}
$$

$$
N R M S E = \sqrt {\frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left[ F _ {N} (t _ {i}) - \hat {F} _ {N} (t _ {i}) \right] ^ {2}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left[ F _ {N} (t _ {i}) \right] ^ {2}}}\tag{61}
$$

where $\hat { F } _ { \scriptscriptstyle N } ( t _ { i } )$ is the value of the empirical distribution function computed based on the parameter estimation. Both RMSE and NRMSE are excellent ways to express the accuracy, and the smaller RMSE or NRMSE means better prediction.

## 3. Empirical evaluation of the proposed method

## 3.1 Simulation parameter design

In this study, some Monte-Carlo simulations are used to generate Weibull pseudo-random numbers as inputs from uniformly distributed random numbers. To investigate the fitness of the method for different location, shape and scale parameters without loss of generality, we choose the location parameter as $\gamma = 1$ . Due to the influence of the coefficient of variation, it is not appropriate to select too large scale parameter, so in this paper we take the scale parameter as $\mathsf { n } = \mathsf { 1 }$ , 2 and 4. Furthermore, in order to investigate the connection between the shape parameter and the location parameter, and to link the hybrid method together, we take the shape parameter as $\mathsf { m } =$ 0.5, 1, and 2. For the purpose of investigating the accuracy of each method with small samples, the sample size is considered to be 5, 10, 25, and 50, and the convergence precision is taken as $\varepsilon = 1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ . Each simulation experiment is repeated 2000 times and all Weibull parameter estimation results are averaged. The above is the main idea of this study. Since there are a large number of simulation experiment data and in order to save space in the paper, the inputs and outputs for each set of experiments are not fully listed, but it is easy to implement in the MATLAB program, which mainly uses the Weibull random number function to generate 2000 inputs for each set of experiments, and then averages the 2000 sets of outputs by computation as the parameter prediction results. Therefore, all the simulation experiments can be reproduced by combining the equations given in Sec. 2. All experiments in this paper are performed in MATLAB 2022 on a PC equipped with a Windows 11 system 64-bit, 3.6 GHz CPU, and 6 GB of RAM.

## 5. Discussion

In this paper, we use the HGLM to get the best parameter estimation results. To demonstrate that the HGLM has high parameter estimation accuracy with small samples, we performed Monte Carlo simulations and compared the results with other existing methods. In addition, as the center of HGLM, the gray genetic algorithm also has the function of the sample expansion, which has not been studied, and the expanded samples are not evaluated. The sample expansion method based on gray genetic algorithm steps are as follows:

Step 1: Let the original sequence containing negative numbers be X , which can be expressed as

$$
X = \left\{x (1), x (2), \dots , x (N) \right\}.\tag{62}
$$

The original sequence X containing negative numbers is transformed into a non-negative sequence by a translation transformation $X ^ { * }$ is

$$
X ^ {*} = X + H \cdot \emptyset\tag{63}
$$

where H is the sequence with the same capacity as X and all data are 1. ∅ is the translation parameter, and $\emptyset > \left| \operatorname* { m i n } \left\{ x ( 1 ) , x ( 2 ) , \cdots , x ( N ) \right\} \right|$

Step 2: Compute the parameters of non-negative sequence $X ^ { * }$ , let $T = M / m$ , parameters M and m are given by

$$
M = \max \left\{x ^ {*} (1), x ^ {*} (2), \dots , x ^ {*} (N) \right\}\tag{64}
$$

$$
m = \min \left\{x ^ {*} (1), x ^ {*} (2), \dots , x ^ {*} (N) \right\}.\tag{65}
$$

If the parameter T of the non-negative sequence $X ^ { * }$ is too large, the smoothness of the resulting sequence in the subsequent transformations will be affected. Thus, the translation transformation can be used to transform the sequence $X ^ { * }$ into sequence with appropriate value of $T$

Step 3: Transform the non-negative sequence $X ^ { * }$ into a non-negative monotonically increasing sequence $X ^ { * } D _ { \imath }$ by using the accelerated exponential transformation $D _ { 1 }$ , thus the expression for $X ^ { * } D _ { \imath }$ is

$$
X ^ {*} D _ {1} = \left\{x ^ {*} (1) d _ {1}, x ^ {*} (2) d _ {1}, \dots , x ^ {*} (N) d _ {1} \right\}\tag{66}
$$

where $x ^ { * } ( k ) d _ { 1 } = x ^ { * } ( k ) T ^ { k - 1 }$ and $k = 1 , 2 , \cdots , N$

Step 4: Let the sequence $V = X ^ { * } D _ { \bf \Phi }$ and $\nu ( k ) = x ^ { * } ( k ) d _ { 1 }$ The geometric mean generating transformation $D _ { \gamma }$ is used to transform the sequence $V$ of non-negative monotonically increasing sequence into smooth non-negative monotonically increasing sequence $V D _ { 2 }$ , the expression is given by

$$
V D _ {2} = \left\{v (1) d _ {2}, v (2) d _ {2}, \dots , v (N) d _ {2} \right\}\tag{67}
$$

where $\nu ( k ) d _ { 2 } = \left[ \Pi _ { i = 1 } ^ { k } \nu ( i ) \right] ^ { \frac { 1 } { k } }$

If the processed data sequence still satisfies the conditions of the three-parameter Weibull distribution, data prediction can be accomplished by using Eq. (44). Sample expansion of the processed sequence can be achieved by adding the predicted data to the processed sequence. Then, the sample expansion sequence needs to be inversely transformed to satisfy the initial sequence. The specific steps of the inverse transformation are as follows:

Step 1: Geometrically averaging the simulated sequence $\hat { X } ^ { ( 0 ) }$ to generate the inverse transform sequence $\hat { V } = \left[ \hat { \nu } ( 1 ) \right.$ ${ \hat { \nu } } ( 2 ) , \cdots , { \hat { \nu } } ( s ) { \ ] }$ , parameter vˆ can be expressed as

$$
\hat {v} (k + 1) = \frac {\left(\hat {x} ^ {(0)} (k + 1)\right) ^ {k + 1}}{\left(\hat {x} ^ {(0)} (k)\right) ^ {k}} \hat {v} (1) = \hat {x} ^ {(0)} (1).\tag{68}
$$

Step 2: Further accelerating the exponential inverse transformation of V to obtain the sequence as

$$
\hat {X} ^ {*} = \left[ \hat {x} ^ {*} (1), \hat {x} ^ {*} (2), \dots , \hat {x} ^ {*} (s) \right]\tag{69}
$$

where $\hat { x } ^ { * } \left( k \right) = \hat { \nu } \left( k \right) / T ^ { k - 1 }$

Step 3: A simulated sequence $\hat { X }$ of the original sequence $\hat { X } ^ { * }$ is obtained by applying a translational inverse transform to the sequence $X$ , where $\hat { X } = \left[ \hat { x } ( 1 ) , \hat { x } ( 2 ) , \cdots , \hat { x } ( s ) \right]$ and $\hat { x } ( k ) = \hat { x } ^ { * } ( k ) - \gamma$

Step 4: After obtaining the simulated sequence $\hat { X }$ according to the genetic gray algorithm, it is necessary to judge the accuracy of the simulated sequence and determine the appropriate sample size. The simulation accuracy is usually expressed by the average error, which can be expressed as

$$
\overline {{\Delta}} = \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {N} \left| \frac {x (k) - \hat {x} (k)}{x (k)} \right|.\tag{70}
$$

Step 5: If the average error of simulated sequence $\overline { { \Delta } } \leq 0 . 0 5 ,$ then the simulation accuracy is enough, and the original sequence can be expanded by gray genetic algorithm. If the average error $0 . 0 5 < \overline { { \Delta } } \leq 0 . 2$ , then the simulation accuracy is low, and a small amount of data should be expanded. If $\overline { { \Delta } } > 0 . 2$ then the sample should be expanded a little or not at all.

Step 6: Since the sample capacity of the original sequence $X$ is N and the sample capacity of the simulated sequence $X$ is $\pmb { \mathscr { s } } \left( \mathnormal { s } \geq N \right)$ , the data $\{ \hat { x } \big ( N + 1 \big ) , \hat { x } \big ( N + 2 \big ) , \ \cdots , \hat { x } \big ( s \big ) \}$ in sequence X<sup>ˆ</sup> are added to the original sequence X as the predicted data to achieve sample expansion.

The above is the feasibility analysis of the sample expansion function of the gray genetic algorithm, but we have not conducted further research, which is the focus of our next research.

## 6. Conclusion

Inspired by the challenges faced in engineering, this study investigates the problem of parameter estimation for the threeparameter Weibull distribution with small samples. Specifically, we propose a Weibull parameter estimation method based on hybrid gray genetic algorithm with improved maximum likelihood method (HGLM). Firstly, based on the connection between the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the Weibull distribution and the gray genetic algorithm, the location parameter can be obtained by matrix operations. Then, based on the location parameter, the scale and shape parameters of the fault data can be obtained with improved maximum likelihood method. Finally, the Weibull parameters obtained from the hybrid method are utilized to assess the parameter estimation accuracy. It is worth noting that this study provides explicit functions for the location, scale and shape parameters, which makes the parameter estimation results unique.

In this study, the HGLM is compared with existing methods such as MLE, CCMLSE, GPWM with small samples. Specifically, the estimation performance of the HGLM is investigated in terms of parameter estimation with small samples $( \mathsf { n } = 5 , 1 0 ,$ 15, 20), and five parameters are selected to evaluate the accuracy and method fit of the proposed methods. Through comparison, it can be proved that the parameter estimation accuracy and method fitting of the HGLM is higher than other three methods. It is also demonstrated that the relationship between shape and location parameters has some influence on the results of parameter estimation. At the end of the study, two life data obtained from practical engineering are used to show the applicability and accuracy of the HGLM, and the gray genetic algorithms sample expansion function is proved to be feasible.

In summary, the HGLM improves the accuracy and fitting of Weibull parameter estimation with small samples, which also solves the problem of difficult estimation for location parameter by using improved gray genetic algorithm. It is verified that the HGLM can serve as an effective parameter estimation method for the three-parameter Weibull distribution with small samples.
