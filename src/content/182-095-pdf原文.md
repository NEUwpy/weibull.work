# On nonlinear weighted errors-in-variables parameter estimation problem in the three-parameter Weibull model

**Abstract**

This paper is concerned with the three-parameter Weibull distribution which is widely used as a model in reliability and lifetime studies. In practice, the Weibull model parameters are not known in advance and must be estimated from a random sample. Difficulties in applying the method of maximum likelihood to three-parameter Weibull models have led to a variety of alternative approaches in the literature. In this paper we consider the nonlinear weighted errors-in-variables (EIV) fitting approach. As a main result, two theorems on the existence of the EIV estimate are obtained. An illustrative example is also included.

## 1. Introduction

The Weibull distribution is one of the most widely used model in reliability and lifetime studies (see [8,16–18]). This distribution was introduced by the Swedish statistician Waloddi Weibull who used it for the first time in 1939 in connection with his studies on the strength of materials (see [24]). Two popular forms of this skewed distribution are two- and threeparameter Weibull distributions.

In this paper we consider the nonlinear weighted errors-in-variables parameter estimation problem for the three-parameter Weibull distribution (model)

$$
F (t; \alpha , \beta , \eta) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 - \mathrm {e} ^ {- \left(\frac {t - \alpha}{\eta}\right) ^ {\beta}}, & t > \alpha , \\ 0, & t \leqslant \alpha , \end{array} \right. \tag {1}
$$

where $\alpha \geqslant 0$ is a location parameter, $\eta > 0$ the scale, and $\beta > 0$ the shape parameter [25]. The corresponding probability density is given by

$$
f (t; \alpha , \beta , \eta) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {\beta}{\eta} \left(\frac {t - \alpha}{\eta}\right) ^ {\beta - 1} e ^ {- \left(\frac {t - 2}{\eta}\right) ^ {\beta}}, & t > \alpha , \\ 0, & t \leqslant \alpha . \end{array} \right. \tag {2}
$$

If $\alpha = 0$ , the resulting distribution is called the two-parameter Weibull distribution. A nonnegative random variable T is said to follow the three-parameter Weibull distribution if its probability density function (PDF) and the cumulative distribution function (CDF) are given by (2) and (1), respectively. Murthy et al. [17] have written an excellent monograph on the Weibull models, in which various Weibull models are classified into seven different groups.

The Weibull three-parameter distribution is very flexible and by an appropriate choice of the shape parameter $\beta$ the distribution can assume a wide variety of shapes (see Fig. 1). In the case when $\beta > 1$ , the distribution is bell-shaped. It is often proposed that the Weibull distribution with a value of $\beta$ lying between 3 and 4 gives a fair approximation to the normal distribution; the most common value of $\beta$ used to approximate the normal distribution is $\beta = 3 . 4 $ . When $\beta = 1$ , the Weibull distribution becomes the two-parameter exponential distribution; when $\beta = 2$ , it is identical to the Rayleigh distribution; when $\beta = 2 . 5$ , it approximates the lognormal distribution. That is the reason why the Weibull distribution is one of the most widely used models in reliability and lifetime studies. There are many applications of the Weibull distribution in medicine, biology, agriculture, forestry and engineering sciences. In addition, the Weibull distribution has found applications as a trend curve in many applied research (see [3,20,22]). The reliability function R and the hazard (instantaneous failure rate) function $h$ for the three-parameter Weibull distribution are given by

$$
R (t; \alpha , \beta , \eta) = \mathrm {e} ^ {- \left(\frac {t - \alpha}{\eta}\right) ^ {\beta}}, \quad t > \alpha
$$

and

$$
h (t; \alpha , \beta , \eta) = \frac {f (t ; \alpha , \beta , \eta)}{1 - F (t ; \alpha , \beta , \eta)} = \frac {\beta}{\eta} \left(\frac {t - \alpha}{\eta}\right) ^ {\beta - 1}, \quad t > \alpha .
$$

The Weibull distribution hazard function is monotone increasing if $\beta > 1$ , decreasing if $0 < \beta < 1$ , and constant for $\beta = 1$ .

Statistical estimation of unknown parameters from a random sample is an important problem that can be solved by many methods. Maximum likelihood (ML) estimation is a traditional method for parameter estimation since it possesses beneficial properties such as asymptotic normality and consistency. But, the ML estimate does not necessarily exist, and it is not necessarily unique. For the two-parameter Weibull distribution, the results of Pratt [19] and Burridge [5] guarantee the existence of a unique ML estimate. However, our attention here is focused on the three-parameter Weibull distribution. In this case many additional difficulties arise. For example, there is no standard ML estimate for any observation from density (2) (see e.g. [12,16]). In the literature, considerable effort has been devoted to such difficulties with the maximum likelihood approach (see e.g. [1,2,7,23]). There are several other statistical methods for estimating model parameters such as the method of moments, the method of percentiles and the Bayesian method. Unfortunately, none of these methods (excluding the Bayesian one) is appropriate for small data sets (see [16–18]).

A very popular method for parameter estimation is the least squares (LS) method. The problem of nonlinear weighted least squares fitting of the three-parameter Weibull distribution to the given data $( w _ { i } , t _ { i } , y _ { i } )$ , $i = 1 , \ldots , n ,$ is considered by Jukic´ et al. [12]. Part $w _ { i } > 0$ of the data stands for data weights. They showed that the best LS estimate exists provided that the data satisfy just the following two conditions: (i) $0 < t _ { 1 } < t _ { 2 } < \cdot \cdot \cdot < t _ { n }$ and (ii) $0 < y _ { 1 } < y _ { 2 } < \cdot \cdot \cdot < y _ { n } < 1$ . Since the Weibull random variable T is nonnegative and since numbers $y _ { i }$ denote empirical CDF values, these two conditions are natural.

In the LS regression model the observations $t _ { i }$ of the independent variable are assumed to be exact and only the observations $y _ { i }$ of the dependent variable are subject to random errors. Unfortunately, this assumption does not seem to be very realistic in practice, and many errors (sampling errors, human errors, modeling errors and instrument errors) prevent us

In such situation, when also the observations of the independent variable contain errors, then it seems reasonable to estimate the unknown parameters so that the weighted sum of squares of all errors is minimized. This approach, known as errors-in-variables regression (EIV regression), is a natural generalization of the LS regression (see e.g. [9]). In numerical analysis, errors-in-variables regression is known as the total least squares method (see [10]).

Numerical methods for solving the nonlinear EIV problem are described in Boggs et al. [6] and Schwetlick and Tiller [21]. Before the iterative minimization of the sum of squares it is still necessary to ask whether the EIV estimate exists. In the case of nonlinear EIV problems it is still extremely difficult to answer this question (see [4,13,14]).

The authors have so far not come across any paper dealing with a nonlinear EIV fitting problem for the three-parametric Weibull distribution, in spite of the many papers on Weibull models. The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section 2, we briefly describe the EIV method and present our main results (Theorems 1 and 2) which guarantee the existence of the EIV estimate for the three-parametric Weibull distribution. An illustrative numerical example is given in Section 3. All proofs are given in Section 4.

## 2. Main results-the EIV regression existence theorems

The EIV regression method can be described as follows: Suppose we are given the data $( w _ { i } , t _ { i } , y _ { i } ) , i = 1 , \ldots , n , n > 3$ $n > 3$ , where

$$
0 <   t _ {1} <   t _ {2} <   \dots <   t _ {n} \tag {3}
$$

denote the values of the independent variable (observations of the nonnegative Weibull random variable T),

$$
0 <   y _ {1} <   y _ {2} <   \dots <   y _ {n} <   1 \tag {4}
$$

are the respective estimates of the empirical CDF and $w _ { i } > 0$ are the data weights which describe the assumed relative accuracy of the data.

In the EIV regression model all observations are subject to random errors. If we assume that $y _ { i }$ contain unknown additive error $\varepsilon _ { i }$ and that $t _ { i }$ has unknown additive error $\delta _ { i }$ , then the mathematical model becomes

$$
y _ {i} = F (t _ {i} + \delta_ {i}; \alpha , \beta , \eta) + \varepsilon_ {i}, \quad i = 1, \dots , n.
$$

The unknown parameters $\alpha , ~ \beta$ and $\eta$ of function (1) have to be estimated by minimizing the weighted sum of squares of all errors, i.e. by minimizing the functional (see [6,9,11,15,21])

$$
T (\alpha , \beta , \eta , \delta) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left\{\left[ F \left(t _ {i} + \delta_ {i}; \alpha , \beta , \eta\right) - y _ {i} \right] ^ {2} + \delta_ {i} ^ {2} \right\}
$$

on the set ${ \mathcal { P } } \times \mathbb { R } ^ { n }$ , where

$$
\mathscr {P} := \left\{\left(\alpha , \beta , \eta\right) \in \mathbb {R} ^ {3}: \alpha \geqslant 0; \beta , \eta > 0. \right.
$$

A point $( \alpha ^ { \star } , \beta ^ { \star } , \eta ^ { \star } )$ in $\mathcal { P }$ is called the errors-in-variables estimate (EIV estimate) of the unknown parameters $( \alpha , \beta , \eta )$ for the three-parameter Weibull distribution, if there exists $\delta ^ { \star } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { n }$ such that

$$
T (\alpha^ {\star}, \beta^ {\star}, \eta^ {\star}, \delta^ {\star}) = \inf  _ {(\alpha , \beta , \eta , \delta) \in \mathcal {P} \times \mathbb {R} ^ {n}} T (\alpha , \beta , \eta , \delta).
$$

Geometrically, minimization of functional T corresponds to minimization of the weighted sum of squares of the distances from the data points $( t _ { i } , y _ { i } )$ to the curve $t \mathrm { \mapsto } F ( t ; \alpha , \beta , \eta )$ . Since in the case of the EIV problem one has to deal with a large number of independent variables, special numerical methods for finding the EIV estimate were designed (see e.g. [6,21]).

If the problem of the weighted EIV estimation for the Weibull distribution is considered, $y _ { i }$ comes from the empirical CDF and conditions (3) and (4) are natural. But, if the analogue EIV estimation procedure is considered from the nonlinear regression curve fitting point of view, then there is no reason to expect that conditions (3) and (4) are naturally fulfilled. The main results of this paper which follows can be applied in both cases.

Theorem 1. If the data $( w _ { i } , t _ { i } , y _ { i } ) , i = 1 , \ldots , n , n > 3$ $n > 3$ , satisfy conditions (3) and (4), then the EIV estimate for the threeparametric Weibull distribution exists.

Instead of minimizing functional T, sometimes a more adequate criterion to estimate the unknown parameters a; $\beta$ and $\eta$ of function (1) is to minimize on the set $\mathcal { P } \times \mathbb { R } ^ { n }$ the following sum of squares:

$$
G (\alpha , \beta , \eta , \delta) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} \left[ F \left(t _ {i} + \delta_ {i}; \alpha , \beta , \eta\right) - y _ {i} \right] ^ {2} + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} p _ {i} \delta_ {i} ^ {2},
$$

where $w _ { i }$ ; $p _ { i } > 0$ are some weights.

Theorem 2. If the points $( t _ { i } , y _ { i } ) , i = 1 , \ldots , n , n > 3$ $( t _ { i } , y _ { i } )$ $n > 3$ , satisfy conditions (3) and (4), then there exists a point $( \alpha ^ { \star } , \beta ^ { \star } , \eta ^ { \star } , \delta ^ { \star } ) \in \mathcal { P } \times \mathbb { R } ^ { n }$ such that

$$
G (\alpha^ {\star}, \beta^ {\star}, \eta^ {\star}, \delta^ {\star}) = \inf  _ {(\alpha , \beta , \eta , \delta) \in \mathscr {P} \times \mathbb {R} ^ {n}} G (\alpha , \beta , \eta , \delta).
$$
