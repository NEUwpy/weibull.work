# An Efficient Method of Parameter and Quantile Estimation for the Three-Parameter Weibull Distribution Based on Statistics Invariant to Unknown Location Parameter

HIDEKI NAGATSUKA<sup>1,\*</sup> AND N. BALAKRISHNAN<sup>2,3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Faculty of System Design, Tokyo Metropolitan University, Asahigaoka, Tokyo, Japan

<sup>2</sup>Department of Mathematics and Statistics, McMaster University, Hamilton, Canada

<sup>3</sup>Department of Statistics, King Abdulaziz University, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia

The three-parameter Weibull distribution is widely used in life testing and reliability analysis. In this article, we propose an efficient methodfor the estimation ofparameters and quantiles of the three-parameter Weibull distribution, which avoids the problem of unbounded likelihood, by using statistics invariant to unknown location. Through a Monte Carlo simulation study, we show that the proposed method performs well compared to other prominent methods based on bias and MSE. Finally, we present two illustrative examples.

Keywords Bayesian likelihood estimators; Maximum likelihood estimators; Order statistics; Spacings; Threshold parameter; Weighted maximum likelihood estimators.

Mathematics Subject Classification 62F10; 62N01.

## 1. Introduction

The three-parameter Weibull distribution, with an unknown origin, is a widely used model in life-testing, reliability analysis, meteorology, hydrology, and economics wherein it is reasonable to assume that there is a nonzero origin below which no event can occur. The cumulative distribution function (cdf) of such a three-parameter Weibull distribution is given by

$$
F (x; \alpha , \beta , \gamma) = 1 - \exp \left\{- \left(\frac {x - \gamma}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta} \right\},\tag{1}
$$

where $\alpha > 0 , \beta > 0$ and $\gamma < x$ are the scale, the shape and the threshold parameters, respectively. The corresponding probability density function (pdf) is

$$
f (x; \alpha , \beta , \gamma) = \frac {\beta}{\alpha} \left(\frac {x - \gamma}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta - 1} \exp \left\{- \left(\frac {x - \gamma}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta} \right\},\tag{2}
$$

for $\alpha > 0 , \beta > 0$ and $\gamma < x$

The maximum likelihood (ML) method tends to perform better than its competitors and has the desirable properties of being consistent, asymptotically normal, and asymptotically efficient under the classical regularity conditions. However, it is well known that the regularity conditions are not satisfied for the ML estimation of the three-parameter Weibull distribution since the support of the pdf in (2) depends on the unknown parameter when the threshold parameter $\gamma$ is unknown, and the ML estimators may not exist, and may not have the classical asymptotic properties even if they exist (see Cheng and Amin 1983; Smith 1985 for details).

For this reason, various alternative methods have been proposed in the literature such as the method of moments (Newby, 1980, 1984; Cran, 1988) and its modifications (Cohen and Whitten, 1982), the methods of percentiles (Dubey, 1967; Zanakis, 1979; Wyckoff et al., 1980), the maximum product of spacings (MPS) (Cheng and Amin, 1983; Ranneby, 1984), the corrected likelihood (CL) method (Cheng and Iles, 1987), the quantile maximum probability (QMP) method (Heathcote and Brown, 2004; Heathcote et al., 2002), the Bayesian likelihood (BL) method (Hall and Wang, 2005), and the weighted maximum likelihood (w-ML) method (Cousineau, 2009b). References Nelson (1982); Balakrishnan and Cohen (1991); Johnson et al. (1994); Meeker and Escobar (1998); Lawless (2003); Murthy et al. (2004); Cousineau (2009); $\mathrm { N g }$ et al. (2012) have all given detailed accounts on various methods for the estimation of parameters of the three-parameter Weibull distribution.

Recently, an efficient method was proposed by Nagatsuka et al. (2013), which will be briefly introduced in the next section. We refer to this method as the Location and Scale Parameters Free Maximum Likelihood (abbreviated as LSPF) method. The main idea of this method is that, to avoid the unbounded likelihood problem, statistics not depending on the location and scale parameters are used in place of the original sample. One of the main features of the LSPF method is that it results in the estimates of all the parameters that exist uniquely and that they have consistency over entire parameter space. The LSPF method generally outperforms other existing methods based on bias and RMSE for the estimation of parameters. However, one drawbacks in the method is that, while obtaining statistics not depending on the location and scale parameters from the original sample, for the estimation of the parameter $\beta _ { : }$ , two random variables among them are always degenerate. Reduction in the number of degenerate variables may yield better performance in the estimation of $\boldsymbol { \beta }$ . Yet another feature of the LSPF method is that, the estimates of α, $\beta ,$ and $\gamma$ are obtained almost separately. This enables us to avoid computational problems that are usually encountered in multidimensional numerical search, but the method may perform poorly in the case when all three estimates are needed simultaneously as in the case of estimation of quantiles.

Motivated by these drawbacks of the LSPF method, we propose here a new method of estimation for the parameters of the three-parameter Weibull distribution. The rest of this article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present a brief description of the LSPF method and then present the new method. We further give a brief discussion about some of the properties of these methods. In Section 3, a Monte Carlo simulation study is presented which shows that the new method generally outperforms other prominent estimators for the estimation of parameters α, $\beta _ { ; }$ , and $\gamma$ and the quantiles. In Section $^ { 4 , }$ , we present two illustrative examples, and finally some concluding remarks are made.

## 2. Methods of Estimation

Let $X _ { 1 } , \ldots , X _ { n }$ be n independent random variables with common cdf in (1). For $i =$ $1 , \ldots , n .$ , let $X _ { ( i ) }$ denote the ith order statistic among $X _ { 1 } , \ldots , X _ { n }$ . We first describe the LSPF method and then detail the new method of estimation of parameters of the threeparameter Weibull distribution. In addition, we discuss some of the properties of these methods. At the end of this section, we discuss briefly the quantile estimation.

## 2.1. LSPF Method

Recently, Nagatsuka et al. (2013) presented a new method of estimation for the threeparameter Weibull distribution, which is discussed briefly here.

The LSPF method consists of two stages. In the first stage, $\beta$ is estimated by using location and scale free statistics. In the second stage, α and $\gamma$ are estimated via bias-reduced estimators after replacing the parameter $\beta$ by its estimate obtained from the first stage.

In the first stage, define the following statistics:

$$
W _ {(i)} = \frac {X _ {(i)} - X _ {(1)}}{X _ {(n)} - X _ {(1)}}, \quad i = 1, \dots , n.\tag{3}
$$

It is seen that $W _ { ( i ) } { } ^ { \ } \mathbf { \bar { s } }$ do not depend on $\gamma$ and $\alpha ,$ but do depend on $\beta .$ . We observe that $W _ { ( 1 ) }$ takes on the value 0 and $W _ { ( n ) }$ takes on the value 1 constantly, that is, the two random variables $W _ { ( 1 ) }$ and $W _ { ( n ) }$ are degenerate.

Nagatsuka et al. (2013) then considered the ML estimation of $\beta$ based on (3). The joint density of $W _ { ( 2 ) } , \dots , W _ { ( n - 1 ) }$ is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \psi (w _ {2}, \ldots , w _ {n - 1}; \beta) \\ = n!   \beta^ {n} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} v ^ {n - 2} \left\{\prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (u + w _ {i}   v) \right\} ^ {\beta - 1} \\ \times \exp \left[ - \left\{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (u + w _ {i}   v) ^ {\beta} \right\} \right] d v   d u, \\ 0 \leq w _ {2} \leq \dots \leq w _ {n - 1} \leq 1, \end{array}
$$

where $w _ { 1 } = 0$ and $w _ { n } = 1$ . We then obtain the likelihood function of $\beta$ based on $W _ { ( 1 ) } , \ldots , W _ { ( n ) }$ as

$$
l _ {W} \left(\beta ; w _ {2}, \dots , w _ {n - 1}\right) = \psi \left(w _ {2}, \dots , w _ {n - 1}; \beta\right),\tag{4}
$$

where $w _ { 2 } , \cdots , w _ { n - 1 }$ are the realized values of $W _ { ( 2 ) } , \dots , W _ { ( n - 1 ) }$ . Then, the MLE of $\beta$ based on $W _ { ( i ) } { } ^ { \ ' } \mathbf { s } .$ , denoted by ${ \hat { \beta } } _ { W }$ , is obtained by maximizing $l _ { W } ( \beta ; w _ { 2 } , \ldots , w _ { n - 1 } )$ with respect to $\beta$ .

In the second stage, the bias-corrected estimators of $\gamma$ and $\alpha$ may be obtained as follows:

$$
\hat {\gamma} _ {W} = X _ {(1)} - n ^ {- 1 / \hat {\beta} _ {W}} \hat {\alpha} _ {i n i t} \Gamma (1 + 1 / \hat {\beta} _ {W}),\tag{5}
$$

$$
\hat {\alpha} _ {W} = \left\{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \hat {\gamma} _ {W}) ^ {\hat {\beta} _ {W}} \right\} ^ {1 / \hat {\beta} _ {W}},\tag{6}
$$

where

$$
\hat {\gamma} _ {i n i t} = X _ {(1)},
$$

$$
\hat {\alpha} _ {i n i t} = \left\{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \hat {\gamma} _ {i n i t}) ^ {\hat {\beta} _ {w}} \right\} ^ {1 / \hat {\beta} _ {w}}.
$$

Nagatsuka et al. (2013) proved that these estimates always exist uniquely and that they have consistency over the entire parameter space. Further, the LSPF method generally outperforms other existing methods based on bias and RMSE.

However, one of the drawbacks of the LSPF method is that, while obtaining statistics not depending on the location and scale parameters from the original sample, for the estimation of $\beta ,$ , two random variables among them are always degenerate, hence reducing the sample size by two. Yet another feature of the LSPF method is that the estimates of the parameters $\alpha _ { \mathrm { { ; } } }$ , $\beta ,$ , and $\gamma$ are obtained almost separately. This enables us to avoid computational problems after encountered in multidimensional numerical search, but the method may perform poorly when all three estimates are needed simultaneously as in the case of estimation of quantiles.

## 2.2. New Method

Motivated by these drawbacks of the LSPF method, we propose here a new method of estimation for the parameters of the three-parameter Weibull distribution, which also consists in two stages. One of the main ideas of the method is to use statistics not depending on $\gamma ,$ which results in the number of degenerate variables being only one. Another idea is to estimate the parameters $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ simultaneously, and not sequentially, by using the usual ML method after substituting for $\beta$ the estimate of $\beta$ obtained from the first stage.

We consider the following statistics:

$$
V _ {(i)} = X _ {(i)} - X _ {(1)}, \quad i = 1, \dots , n.\tag{7}
$$

It is evident that $\overset { \triangledown } { V _ { ( i ) } } \mathit { \bar { s } }$ do not depend on $\gamma .$ , but do depend on α and $\beta .$ . We observe that $V _ { ( 1 ) }$ takes on the value 0 constantly, that is, only one random variable $V _ { ( 1 ) }$ is degenerate in this case.

We consider the ML estimators of α and $\beta$ based on $V _ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , V _ { ( n ) }$ . The likelihood function of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ based on $V _ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , V _ { ( n ) }$ might be bounded as it does not dependent on $\gamma$ . To obtain the ML estimators of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ based on $V _ { ( i ) } { ' } \mathbf { s }$ , we derive the joint density of $V _ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , V _ { ( n ) }$

Proposition 2.1 For $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta > 0 ,$ , the joint density of $V _ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , V _ { ( n ) }$ is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \phi (v _ {2}, \dots , v _ {n}; \alpha , \beta) \\ = n! \left(\frac {\beta}{\alpha}\right) ^ {n} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\frac {u + v _ {i}}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta - 1} \end{array}
$$

$$
\times \exp \left[ - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\frac {u + v _ {i}}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta} \right] d u,
$$

$$
v _ {1} = 0, 0 \leq v _ {2} \leq \dots \leq v _ {n}.
$$

Proof. See Appendix A.1.

From Proposition 2.1, we can obtain the likelihood function of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ based on $V _ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , V _ { ( n ) }$ as

$$
l _ {V} (\alpha , \beta ; v _ {2}, \dots , v _ {n}) = \phi (v _ {2}, \dots , v _ {n}; \alpha , \beta),\tag{8}
$$

where $v _ { 2 } , \ldots , v _ { n }$ are the realized values of $V _ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , V _ { ( n ) }$ . Then, the MLEs of α and $\beta$ based on $V _ { ( i ) } { } ^ { \ , } \mathbf { s } ,$ , denoted by $\hat { \alpha } _ { V }$ and ${ \hat { \beta } } _ { V }$ , are obtained by maximizing $l _ { V } ( \alpha , \beta ; v _ { 2 } , \ldots , v _ { n } )$ with respect to α and $\beta$ .

Note that the likelihood function in (8) is bounded, for every $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta > 0 .$ , and so the proposed method avoids the unbounded likelihood problem just as the LSPF method does.

In the first stage, we obtain the estimators of α and $\beta , \hat { \alpha } _ { V _ { 0 } }$ and ${ \hat { \beta } } _ { V }$ . In the second stage, we use only ${ \hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } } } _ { V }$ . Extensive simulations reveal that $\hat { \alpha } _ { V _ { 0 } }$ has slightly poorer performance than estimators in other methods and the reason for this may be due to the fact that $\hat { \alpha } _ { V _ { 0 } }$ is obtained from only $n - 1$ random variables $V _ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , V _ { ( n ) }$

Once we obtain the estimate of $\beta ,$ by using the method outlined above, we can adopt the usual ML method to obtain the estimates of α and $\gamma$ , in which the shape parameter $\beta$ is replaced by ${ \hat { \beta } } _ { V }$ . Then, the likelihood equations are given by, after replacing $\beta$ by ${ \hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } } } _ { V }$ ,

$$
\frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \gamma) ^ {\hat {\beta} _ {V}} \log (X _ {i} - \gamma)}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \gamma) ^ {\hat {\beta} _ {V}}} - \frac {1}{\hat {\beta} _ {V}} - \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log (X _ {i} - \gamma) = 0,\tag{9}
$$

$$
\alpha = \left\{\frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \gamma) ^ {\hat {\beta} _ {V}} \right\} ^ {\frac {1}{\hat {\beta} _ {V}}}.\tag{10}
$$

The proposed estimators of $\alpha$ and $\gamma _ { : }$ , which are denoted by $\hat { \alpha } _ { V }$ and $\hat { \gamma } _ { V }$ , are then obtained by solving the likelihood equations in (9) and (10). We refer to this method as the Location Parameters Free Maximum Likelihood (abbreviated as LPF) method.

## 2.3. Maximum Likelihood Estimation Based on Spacings

We discuss here the ML method based on spacings defined as

$$
S _ {(i)} = X _ {(i)} - X _ {(i - 1)}, \quad i = 2, \dots , n.\tag{11}
$$

The MPS method, proposed by Cheng and Amin (1983) and Ranneby (1984) independently, is a well known method base on the spacings, but it is not an exact ML method based on the spacings. The exact ML method based on spacings for the three-parameter Weibull distribution has not been discussed in the literature. So, it is important to observe that the LPF method is equivalent to the ML method based on the spacings in the sense of the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1 The estimators from the LPF method agree with the estimators from the ML method based on the spacings in (11).

We also present a similar result for the LSPF method.

Theorem 2.2 The estimators from the LSPF method agree with the estimators from the ML method based on the following spacings:

$$
T _ {(i)} = \frac {X _ {(i)} - X _ {(i - 1)}}{X _ {(n)} - X _ {(1)}}, \quad i = 2, \dots , n - 1.
$$

Proofs of Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 are given in Appendix A.2.

## 2.4. Quantile Estimation

Let us consider the estimation of the quantile of the three-parameter Weibull distribution with cdf in (1). For $0 < p < 1$ , the pth quantile of the three-parameter Weibull distribution is given by

$$
x _ {p} = \gamma + \eta \left(\log \frac {1}{1 - p}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{\beta}}.\tag{12}
$$

The quantile estimator $\hat { x } _ { p }$ of the quantile $x _ { p }$ is obtained simply by substituting the estimators of $\alpha , \beta$ , and $\gamma$ for the parameters in (12).

## 3. Empirical Evaluation of the Proposed Method

We carried out a Monte Carlo simulation study to evaluate the performance of the proposed estimators and the corresponding quantile estimators. The proposed LPF method is compared with the LSPF method of Nagatsuka et al. (2013) as well as the BL method of Hall and Wang (2005), and the weighted w-ML method of Cousineau (2009b).

In the simulation study, the values of the shape parameter $\beta$ were selected as 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, and 4.0. For the location and scale parameters, we took $\gamma = 0$ and $\alpha = 1$ without loss of any generality. The sample size is taken to be 10, 20, 50, 100, and 500.

For comparative purposes, we computed the bias and $\mathrm { R M S E } { = } \sqrt { \nu a r i a n c e + b i a s ^ { 2 } }$ for the estimates of all three parameters. In addition, to evaluate the marginal performance of bias and MSE of the three estimators, the sum of absolute values of bias, and the root of the trace of the MSE matrix of the estimators were also compared.

In the LPF method, we computed the estimate of $\beta$ by maximizing the likelihood function in (8). The implementation of the likelihood function in (8) required numerical integration, and for this purpose we used the double exponential formulas of Takahasi and Mori (1974). We computed the estimates by the LPF and LSPF methods with initial values of the parameters given as the estimates of the BL methods, and the initial values of $\beta , \gamma ,$ and α for the iterative process in the BL method were the simple estimate of $\beta$ proposed by Dubey (1967), $x _ { ( 1 ) } - 1 0 ^ { - 1 }$ and the modified moment estimate of α provided by Cohen and Whitten (1982), respectively, where $x _ { ( 1 ) }$ is the observation of $X _ { ( 1 ) }$ . For computing the estimates of the w-ML method, we follow the same manner in Nagatsuka et al. (2013).

All programs in this numerical study were written in the computer language C. The data for each configuration were generated by the use of Mersenne-Twister algorithm, MT19937, proposed by Matsumoto and Nishimura (1998).

## 5. Concluding Remarks

In this article, we have proposed a new method of estimation for the three-parameter Weibull distribution, motivated by the drawbacks of the LSPF method. We have carried out a simulation study to examine the performance of the proposed method and to compare it with some other prominent methods. Based on the empirical results, for the estimation of parameters, we suggest the use of the LPF method in consideration of both bias and RMSE when the sample size is large. When the sample size is small or moderate, if RMSE of the estimator of $\beta$ is of concern, we suggest the use of the w-ML method, and otherwise we suggest the use of the LPF method based on both bias and RMSE. For the estimation of quantiles, we recommend the use of the LPF method, giving due considerations to bias and RMSE.

We have not considered here properties of the proposed estimators other than bias and MSE for finite-sample situation by Monte Carlo simulations. It will be of interest to study some properties of the proposed estimators such as the existence and uniqueness as well as consistency.

The proposed approach can be adapted to censored and trancated data. However, there are some key issues to resolve in these cases, such as the choice of the location-free statistics, the derivation of the likelihood function, and the analysis of properties of the estimators.

The embedded model problem, in addition to the nonregular problem, is also important problem in estimation for the three-parameter Weibull distribution. Cheng and Iles (1990) and Hirose and Lai (1997) have given detailed accounts on this issue.

Although we have only considered the three-parameter Weibull distribution as an underlying distribution, the proposed method can be applied to a variety of other distributions.

We hope to take these problems on as our future research.

## A.2 Proofs of Theorems 2.1 and 2.2

It is sufficient to present just the proof of Theorem 2.2 since Theorem 2.1 can be proved in an analogous manner.

Recalling (12), we have

$$
W _ {(i)} = T _ {(i)} + T _ {(i - 1)} + \dots + T _ {(2)}, \quad i = 2, \ldots , n - 1,
$$

or

$$
T _ {(i)} = W _ {(i)} - W _ {(i - 1)}, \quad i = 2, \dots , n - 1.\tag{A.1}
$$

It is easily verified that the Jacobian of the transformation in (13) is 1.

Recalling that the joint density of $W _ { ( 2 ) } , \dots , W _ { ( n - 1 ) }$ is given by (4), we have the joint density of $T _ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , T _ { ( n - 1 ) }$ as
