## a b s t r a c t

The Weibull distribution is widely used in applications such as reliability and lifetime studies. Although this distribution has three parameters, for simplicity, literature pertaining to Weibull parameter estimation relaxes one of its parameters in order to estimate the other two. When the three-parameter Weibull distribution is of interest, the estimation procedure is complicated. For example, the likelihood function for a three-parameter Weibull distribution is hard to maximize. In this paper, a Cross Entropy (CE) method is developed in the context of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) of a three-parameter Weibull distribution. Performing a simulation study, a comparative analysis between the newly developed method and two existing methods is conducted. The results show the proposed method has better performance in terms of accuracy, precision and run time for different parameter settings and sample sizes.

## 1. Introduction

The application of the three-parameter Weibull distribution stems from different curve shapes [1]. This capability has made it a practical distribution with a wide range of applications in real-world problems. For example, estimating available bandwidth on each link is frequently modeled by the Weibull distribution when the aim of the research is to determine endto-end available bandwidth measurements [2]. This distribution has been also used for determining wind energy potential [3]. Fatigue-life distributions for composite material properties such as glass-fiber reinforced polymeric composites can be studied by this distribution [4,5]. When performing statistical analyses of satellite reliability, parameters are usually fitted with a Weibull distribution [6]. Furthermore, the distribution is especially useful for failure analysis in different types of systems in which increasing and decreasing failure rates are taken into account [7]. For predicting guarantee models and lifetime analyses, a Weibull distribution was fitted to the data in this study which were obtained from different electronic components [8].

The Weibull probability density and cumulative probability functions are given in Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively [9].

$$
f (x) = \frac {\beta}{\alpha} \left(\frac {x - \delta}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta - 1} e ^ {- \left(\frac {x - \delta}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta}} \quad \alpha , \beta > 0, x \geq \delta ,\tag{1}
$$

$$
F (x) = 1 - e ^ {- \left(\frac {x - \delta}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta}}.\tag{2}
$$

Here, a, b and d are the scale, shape and location parameters, respectively. The practicality of a Weibull distribution can be diminished by the difficulty of estimating its three parameters. In particular, successful applications of a Weibull distribution will be obtained when the parameters are estimated precisely. The current two-parameter density does not suggest satisfactory estimates of the parameters and relies on a numerical procedure

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: A chronological literature review pertaining to different estimation methods for the Weibull distribution is presented in Section 2. Section 3 explains the concept of the MLE. In Section 4, a brief explanation of general CE methods is presented. In Section 5, a newly developed (CE method is introduced and then its performance is improved by some modification. In Section 6, the drawbacks of some previous methods used for comparing and evaluating the estimations are illustrated via an example. Then, an appropriate evaluating criterion is developed based on the Kullback–Leibler distance. Test problems considering large numbers of replications appear in Section 7, where the results of the proposed method are compared with two recent methods proposed by Abbasi et al. [10,11]. Finally, Section 8 is de voted to conclusions and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Literature review

Regarding the three-parameter Weibull distribution, Hirose [12] achieved the percentile point by using an extended maximum likelihood estimate (EMLE) whose convergence was assessed by likelihood rather than parameters. They proved that whenever it is difficult to find converging parameters, EMLE is able to easily estimate related percentile points. Moreover, EMLE obtains the percentile points which are considerably more precise. Using the percentiles and a cumulative distribution function, a stepwise algorithm consisting of eight phases was established by Qiao and Tsokos [13] in order to determine a satisfying estimate of the Weibull distribution. The effectiveness of their method was also shown by a group of numerical examples. Nosal and Nosal [14] used both a Monte Carlo simulation and an array processing language to investigate the performance of the gradient random search minimization procedure for fitting a Weibull distribution to a given data set using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov minimum-distance approach. Luus and Jammer [15] showed that MLE gives the most reliable parameter estimation in comparison with the errors-in-variables and least-squares methods. Abbasi et al. [16] employed a simulated annealing (SA) method to maximize the likelihood function formed to estimate the parameters of a threeparameter Weibull distribution. Furthermore, Abbasi et al. [10] developed a neural network (NN) based method to estimate the Weibull parameters. Their method resembles the moment method as it estimates the three parameters by using mean, standard deviation, median, skewness and kurtosis of the data. For monitoring production processes with control charts, the Weibull distribution was applied by Surucu and Sazak [17] to model inter-failure times. Then, they prepared a basic method for estimating the parameters of the Weibull distribution. A method for estimating the location parameter of the Weibull distributions was introduced by Chen and Chen [18]. This method builds up the confidence interval of the location parameter. Markovic et al. [19] developed an approach which initially took a nonparametric approximation for each of the parameters. Later, the obtained estimations were improved as much as possible by using both parametric and nonparametric methods. For estimating parameters of the three-parameter Weibull distribution, a fitting approach – namely the nonlinear weighted errors-in-variables (EIV) approach with two fundamental theorems on the existence of the EIV – was introduced by Jukic and Markovic [20]. Subsequently, this approach was extended further by Markovic and Jukic [21] via a fitting approach – namely the nonlinear weighted total least-squares (TLS) approach to estimate the parameters of the three-parameter Wei bull distribution. In order to improve the performance of the SA method and estimate the parameters with minimum com puter CPU time, Abbasi et al. [11] proposed a hybrid variable neighborhood search and simulated annealing method (Hybrid). Ismail [22] used a Newton–Raphson algorithm to maximize the MLE for hybrid censored data. He also calculated the biases and mean square errors of the MLE in order to evaluate their performances through a Monte Carlo simulation. Eventually. Teimouri et al. [23] classified the estimation methods into five groups: the method of maximum likelihood estimation; the method of logarithmic moments; the percentile method; the method of moments; and, the method of L-mo ments. They provided a comprehensive comparison of these estimation methods

In this paper, a newly developed CE method is introduced that improves the performance of the general CE method for estimating three parameters of Weibull. The CE method is one of the modern simulation-based optimization methods first proposed as a simulation method to estimate probabilities of rare events [24]. Then, it was developed as an advanced optimization method [25].

## 3. MLE method

Estimation theory plays an important role in statistical analyses and methods such as MLE, graphical procedure [26], moments [27,28], and weighted least-square methods [29,30] which are some commonly used techniques for estimating parameters.

For the MLE that is asymptotically unbiased with minimum variances estimators [31], let $x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , \ldots , x _ { n }$ be n independent observations drawn from a probability density function, and $f _ { X _ { i } } ( x _ { i } ; \theta )$ , where $\theta = ( \theta _ { 1 } , \ldots , \theta _ { k } )$ is a vector of k unknown constant parameters which need to be estimated. Then, the likelihood function (L) is given by the following equation:

$$
L = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f _ {X _ {i}} (x _ {i}, \stackrel {\rightharpoonup} {\theta}).\tag{3}
$$

The aim is to find a vector that maximizes the likelihood function. In practice, it is often more convenient to work with the logarithm of the likelihood function (log-likelihood function). Estimations are obtained by solving the following equation set:

$$
\frac {\partial}{\partial \stackrel {-} {\theta}} L n (L) = \stackrel {-} {0}.\tag{4}
$$

For the Weibull distribution, the L function and its logarithm are given in Eqs. (5) and (6).

$$
L \left(x _ {1}, \dots , x _ {n}; \alpha , \beta , \delta\right) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {\beta}{\alpha} \left(\frac {x _ {i} - \delta}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta - 1} e ^ {- \left(\frac {x _ {i} - \delta}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta}}; \quad \alpha , \beta > 0, x _ {i} \geq \delta ,\tag{5}
$$

$$
L n \left[ L \left(x _ {1}, \dots , x _ {n}; \alpha , \beta , \delta\right) \right] = L n \left(\frac {\beta}{\alpha}\right) + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(- \left(\frac {x _ {i} - \delta}{\alpha}\right) ^ {\beta} + (\beta - 1) L n \left(\frac {x _ {i} - \delta}{\alpha}\right)\right).\tag{6}
$$

Applying common optimization techniques to maximize L (or Ln(L)) in Eqs. (5) and (6) is hard. The gradient method is not suitable methodology to use in this case because it suffers from dependency on the partial derivations of the objective function. Therefore, it is difficult for the algorithm to assess the gradient terms and the objective function itself at different points. In addition, there is no longer a need to derive the gradient of this complicated function. As a result, the CE method can be a suitable approach.

## 4. Cross-Entropy algorithm

The CE method was originally designed for estimating probabilities of rare events. Later, it was modified to solve optimization problems. Several recent applications demonstrate the power of the CE method as a generic and practical tool for solving NP-hard problems. The CE begins with an initial uniform distribution over a feasible region, and then updates it based on random solutions sampled from the feasible region. This process should converge to a degenerate distribution that assigns a probability of 1 to an optimal solution.

There is extensive literature on CE theory and its applications. Many useful materials related to CE are on the Cross-Entropy method [32] and a full treatment of CE can be found in work by Rubinstein and Kroese [33]. Kroese et al. [34] demonstrated the effectiveness of the CE method for solving difficult continuous optimization problems. In this sense, to maximize function $S ( x ) , x \in X ,$ , we first define a family of pdfs $\{ f ( . , \nu ) , \nu \in V \}$ on set $X .$ Letting $\gamma ^ { * } = \Pi \mathbf { a } \mathbf { x } _ { x \in X } ( S ( x ) )$ , by taking a random sample $x _ { 1 } , \ldots , x _ { N }$ , we can estimate

$$
\ell (\gamma) = \operatorname * {P r} (S (x) \geqslant \gamma | X \sim f (., v), v \in V) = E \left(I _ {\{S (x) \geq \gamma \}} | X \sim f (., v), v \in V\right).\tag{7}
$$

Note that $S ( X ) > \gamma$ is a rare event when $\gamma$ is set to $( 1 - \mathcal { L } )$ quantile of S(X) where $( 1 - \mathcal { L } )$ is close to 1. By taking an efficient strategy to update m, the CE method generates a sequence of tuples $\{ ( \gamma _ { t } , \nu _ { t } ) \}$ which converge promptly to a small neighborhood of the optimal tuple $( \gamma ^ { * } , \nu ^ { * } )$ [33].

m is updated in an iterative procedure. In each iteration, $\nu _ { t }$ is updated based on the current sample from $f ( . , \nu _ { t - 1 } )$ that is $( x _ { 1 } ^ { ( t - 1 ) } , \cdot \cdot \cdot , \dot { x } _ { N } ^ { ( t - 1 ) } )$ by using $\operatorname { E q . } \left( 8 \right)$ ; the parameters are then smoothed by Eq. (9).

$$
\tilde {v} _ {t} = \underset {v} {\operatorname{argmax}} \left(\frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} I _ {\left\{S (x _ {i} ^ {(t - 1)}) \geq \hat {\gamma} _ {t - 1} \right\}} l n f \left(x _ {i} ^ {(t - 1)}, v\right)\right),\tag{8}
$$

$$
\hat {v} _ {t} = \tau \tilde {v} _ {t} + (1 - \tau) \tilde {v} _ {t - 1},\tag{9}
$$

where $\hat { \gamma } _ { t - 1 }$ is $( 1 - \mathcal { L } )$ -quantile of $S ( x )$ obtained from $x _ { 1 } ^ { ( t - 1 ) } , \ldots , x _ { N } ^ { ( t - 1 ) }$ and s is called the smoothing parameter chosen from (0,1). Iteration will proceed to meet a defined stopping condition. In typical applications when the function in Eq. (8) is convex and differentiable with respect to m [35], the solution of Eq. (8) may be readily obtained by solving the following system of equations:

$$
\frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} I _ {\{S (x _ {i} ^ {(t - 1)}) \geq \hat {\gamma} _ {t - 1} \}} \nabla L n (f (x _ {i} ^ {(t - 1)}, v)) = 0,\tag{10}
$$

where the gradient is in respect to m. The advantage of this approach is that the solution of Eq. (10) can often be calculated analytically. In particular, this happens if the distributions of the random variables belong to an exponential family. For further details, we refer to Rubinstein and Kroese [33].

Using Algorithm 1 to maximize the log-likelihood function, the result was partially satisfactory. However, the algorithm sometimes settled into local optimal points and was a little slow. This motivated us to develop a new CE method to reach better estimations. The modification includes employing the multivariate normal distribution instead of independent normal varieties to generate the samples. The CE method, taking into account both dependent and independent varieties, were implemented in this paper and the results show that the dependent one is significantly more efficient in all aspects for estimation of the Weibull parameters.

The main idea to use dependency emerged from the fact that independent normal distributions are a special case of multivariate normal distribution (when all correlations are zero). Instead of considering the varieties independently, we would want to allow information from the data sampled to determine the amount of dependency; that is, the first iteration starts with independent normal distributions since dependency among the varieties of the best solutions in each iteration are measured in order to be used in sampling the next generation. It was surmised that the data would determine if independency is better (by taking correlation values close to zero). Considering the dependency of the sample elements, we call our proposed approach the Dependent CE method. Although Kroese et al. [34] first mentioned that one can generate a sample dependently, there is no sufficient work to be done in order to show the comparison between the general CE and Dependent CE methods. In this paper, we present the advantages of our Dependent CE method in making more accurate and reliable estimations. Algorithm 2 outlines the proposed Dependent CE method.

## 6. Criteria for comparing estimators

Some direct methods for evaluating the accuracy of the estimators have been performed. These methods measure differences between the estimated parameters and their initial given values on which the data were generated (reference parameters). For example, absolute differences and sum of squared differences between the estimated parameter and refer ence parameters are employed by Abbasi et al. [10,11]. Although they can be considered as criteria, they suffer from some weaknesses depicted in the following illustration.

The three-parameter Weibull shape is not sensitive to its parameters; by the same token, different combinations of these three parameters can lead to very similar densities. In this respect, an example is illustrated in Fig. 1 where the red graph is the Weibull density with (157) parameters. Consider the blue and black graphs as two estimations for the red graph. In this example, the sum of squared differences between the estimated parameters (parameters of blue and black graphs) and ref erence parameters (157) is used as an error criterion to compare the blue and red estimations.

Although there is a large amount of error (494.4775) for the blue density, it is quite similar to the red graph. On the other hand, there is a small amount of error (0.75) for the black graph.

Since the aim of parameter estimation is to recognize a density which can show the manner of the given data set, it is not logical to compare the estimated parameters with the reference parameters directly. Instead, an evaluating method based on the Kullback–Leibler distance can be applied to compare the real density with the estimated density.

The Kullback–Leibler distance is a convenient measure of the distance between two densities, g and h, that is also termed the cross-entropy between g and h. The Kullback–Leibler distance is defined [36] as:

$$
\mathcal {D} (g, h) = \mathbb {E} _ {g} L n \frac {g (X)}{h (X)} = \int g (x) L n g (x) d x - \int g (x) L n h (x) d x.\tag{11}
$$

In Eq. (11), let g(x) be an unknown density with n available samples and $h ( x , \overline { { \theta } } )$ be a known density with unknown param eters which tries to estimate g(x). Therefore, hðx; hÞ (h represents the parameters of $h ( x ) )$ to be determined so that the Kullback–Leibler distance between $g ( x )$ and $h ( x )$ is minimized. To achieve this, h should be chosen such that $\begin{array} { r } { - \int _ { g } ( x ) L n ( h ( x , \theta ) ) , } \end{array}$ Þdx becomes minimized, which is equivalent to solving the following maximization problem:

$$
\max _ {\vec {\theta}} \int g (x) L n (h (x, \vec {\theta})) d x = E _ {g} (L n (h (x, \vec {\theta}))).\tag{12}
$$

Although $g ( x )$ is unknown and we cannot calculate the mentioned integral, this expected value can be estimated via following equation:

$$
E _ {g} (L n (h (x, \vec {\theta}))) \approx \widehat {E _ {g}} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} L n (h (x _ {i}, \vec {\theta})).\tag{13}
$$

## 8. Conclusions

In this paper, we developed a CE algorithm to estimate three parameters of the Weibull distribution. Furthermore, drawbacks of some methods used to evaluate the estimated parameters were discussed and a criterion based on Kullback–Leibler distance was introduced to compare and evaluate the estimations. We conducted simulation studies to evaluate and compare the newly developed CE method with two other methods (NN-based method and a Hybrid VNS and SA method). The simulation results pointed out that the proposed method significantly outperforms the NN method and performs better than the Hybrid algorithm in all aspects and cases. The obtained results in this paper encourage us to recommend the proposed CE method for estimating parameters of other distributions with complicated likelihood functions. When the three-parameter Weibull distribution is of interest, we seldom have complete data for a reliability analysis. Therefore, it seems reasonable to deal with censored data in future research
