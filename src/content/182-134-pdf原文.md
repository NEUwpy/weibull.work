# The Moments of Log-Weibull Order Statistics

John S. White

University of Minnesota

Let $X_{1n} \leq X_{2n} \leq, \cdots, \leq X_{nn}$ be the order statistics of a random sample of size n. For any integrable function $g(x)$ define $E(i, n) = E(g(X_{in}))$ and $M(n) = E(1, n) = E(g(X_{1n}))$ . A number of formulae expressing $E(i, n)$ in terms of $M(j), j \leq n$ , are developed. For example,

$$
E (i, n) = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {j = i - 1} \binom {n} {j} \Delta^ {i} M (n - j).
$$

These results are applied to obtain the means and variances of the order statistics of a log-Weibull distribution $(F(x) = 1 - \exp(-\exp x))$ . Tables of these means and variances are given for $1 \leq i \leq n$ , $n = 1 \pmod{50}$ 100. The computations were made using a set of 100 decimal place logarithms of integers. Examples of the use of these tables in obtaining weighted least squares estimates from censored samples from a Weibull distribution are also given.

## 1. THE MOMENTS OF ORDER STATISTICS

Let $X_{1n} < X_{2n} < \cdots < X_{nn}$ be the order statistics of a random sample of size n from a population with distribution function $F(x)$ and density function $f(x) = dF(x)/dx$ . The density function of $X_{in}$ is then

$$
f _ {i n} (x) = \frac {n !}{(i - 1) ! (n - i) !} F (x) ^ {i - 1} (1 - F (x)) ^ {n - i} f (x).\tag{1.1}
$$

For any integrable function $g(x)$ we have

$$
E (g (X _ {i n})) = \int g (x) f _ {i n} (x) d x\tag{1.2}
$$

and in particular

$$
E (g (X _ {1 n})) = n \int g (x) (1 - F (x)) ^ {n - 1} f (x) d x\tag{1.3}
$$

To simplify the notation we write

$$
\begin{array}{r l} E (i, n) & = E (g (X _ {i n})) \\ M (j) & = E (1, j) = E (g (X _ {1 j})). \end{array}\tag{1.4}
$$

If, in (1.2), we write $F^{i-1}(x) = (1 - (1 - F(x)))^{i-1}$ and expand by the binomial theorem, we have

$$
\begin{array}{r l} E (i, n) & = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} \frac {(- 1) ^ {i} n !}{(i - 1) ! (n - i) !} \binom {i - 1} {j} \int g (x) (1 - F (x)) ^ {n - i + j} f (x) d x \\ & = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i = 1} \frac {(- 1) ^ {i} n !}{(i - 1) ! (n - i) !} \binom {i - 1} {j} \frac {M (n - i + j + 1)}{n - i + j + 1} \end{array}\tag{1.5}
$$

Alternate forms of (1.5) are

$$
E (i, n) = (- 1) ^ {i - 1} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {i} \frac {n !}{(i - 1) ! (n - i) !} \binom {i - 1} {j} \frac {M (n - j)}{n - j}\tag{1.6}
$$

$$
E (i, n) = (- 1) ^ {n - i + 1} \sum_ {j = n - i + 1} ^ {n} (- 1) ^ {j} \binom {n} {j} \binom {j - 1} {n - i} M (j)\tag{1.7}
$$

We note that for $i = n$ , (1.7) reduces to

$$
E (n, n) = - \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} (- 1) ^ {i} \binom {n} {j} M (j)\tag{1.8}
$$

From (1.5) we have

$$
E (i + 1, n) - E (i, n) = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i} \binom {n} {i} (- 1) ^ {j} \binom {i} {j} M (n - i + j)\tag{1.9}
$$

We introduce the difference operator

$$
\Delta M (k) = M (k + 1) - M (k)
$$

and apply the well-known formula (e.g. Abramovitz and Stegum (1964), p. 823)

$$
\Delta^ {i} M (n - i) = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i} \binom {i} {j} (- 1) ^ {i - j} M (n - i + j)
$$

to obtain

$$
E (i + 1, n) - E (i, n) = (- 1) ^ {i} \binom {n} {i} \Delta^ {i} M (n - i)\tag{1.10}
$$

which, combined with the initial condition $E(1, n) = M(1)$ , gives

$$
E (i, n) = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {j} \binom {n} {j} \Delta^ {j} M (n - j)\tag{1.11}
$$

This is a generalization of a result of Kimball (1947).

Another form of (1.11) is

$$
E (i, n) = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {i} \binom {n - i + j} {j} \Delta^ {i} M (n - i + 1)\tag{1.12}
$$

which, for $i = n$ , reduces to

$$
E (n, n) = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {n - 1} (- 1) ^ {j} \Delta^ {j} M (1)\tag{1.13}
$$

Numerous other formulae for $E(i, n)$ may be obtained by algebraic manipulation of (1.5). Srikantan (1962) lists a number of formulae, including (1.7), which give $E(i, n)$ in terms of $E(j, k)$ . One interesting result is the recurrence relation

$$
i E (i + 1, n) - (n - i) E (i, n) = n E (i, n - 1) \quad \text { for } \quad 1 \leq i <   n\tag{1.14}
$$

The problem in applying any of these formulae for the numerical computation of $E(i, n)$ , for large $n$ , is the buildup of roundoff errors. For example, if $n = 100$ , (1.8) becomes

$$
E (1 0 0, 1 0 0) = - \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {1 0 0} (- 1) ^ {j} \binom {1 0 0} {j} M (j)
$$

which is an alternating series of terms ranging in size from $M(100)$ to

$$
\binom {1 0 0} {5 0} M (5 0) = 1 0 0 8 9, 1 3 4 4 5, 4 5 5 6 4, 1 9 3 3 3, 4 8 1 2 4, 9 7 2 5 6 M (5 0)\tag{1.15}
$$

Thus we require 31 decimal place accuracy in $M(50)$ to insure 1 decimal place accuracy in $\binom{100}{50}M(50)$ . The same problem arises in the other formulae. In (1.13) and (1.14) the differencing conceals the difficulties, but they are there none-the-less.

## 2. THE LOG-WEIBULL DISTRIBUTION

The formulae in the preceding section were derived to assist in the computation of the moments of order statistics from a log-Weibull distribution. In many problems of wear-out or fatigue type failure analysis, the Weibull distribution is used as a model for the time (cycles, miles, etc.) to failure. We say that the random variable T is a Weibull variable with slope $\beta$ and characteristic life $\theta$ if

$$
\text { Prob } (T <   t) = 1 - \exp (- (t / \theta) ^ {\beta})\tag{2.1}
$$

The random variable $Y = \log T$ is called a log-Weibull variable. We have

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \operatorname{Prob} (Y <   t) & = \operatorname{Prob} (\log T <   t) \\ & = \operatorname{Prob} (T <   e ^ {t}) \\ & = 1 - \exp (- (e ^ {t} / \theta) ^ {\beta}) \\ & = 1 - \exp (- \exp (\beta (t - \log \theta))) \end{array}\tag{2.2}
$$

The random variable $X = \beta (Y - \log \theta)$ is called a reduced log-Weibull variable. The distribution function of X is

$$
\begin{array}{r l} F (x) & = \text { Prob } (X <   x) \\ & = \text { Prob } (\beta (Y - \log \theta) <   x) \\ & = 1 - e ^ {- e ^ {s}} - \infty <   x <   \infty \end{array}\tag{2.3}
$$

The moments of $X$ are given by

$$
\begin{array}{r l} E (X ^ {k}) & = \int x ^ {k} d F (x) \\ & = \int_ {- \infty} ^ {\infty} x ^ {k} e ^ {x - s ^ {x}} d x \\ & = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} (\log t) ^ {k} e ^ {- t} d t \quad (t = e ^ {x}) \\ & = \frac {d ^ {k}}{d s ^ {k}} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} t ^ {s} e ^ {- t} d t | _ {s = 0} \\ & = \frac {d ^ {k}}{d s ^ {k}} \Gamma (s + 1) | _ {s = 0} \end{array}\tag{2.4}
$$

In particular we have

$$
\begin{array}{r l} E (X) & = \Gamma^ {\prime} (1) = - \gamma = -. 5 7 7 2 1 5 6 6 4 \dots \\ E (X ^ {2}) & = \Gamma^ {\prime \prime} (2) = \pi^ {2} / 6 + \gamma^ {2} \\ \operatorname{Var} (X) & = \pi^ {2} / 6 = 1. 6 4 4 9 3 4 0 6 7 \dots \end{array}
$$

We note that the relationship between Weibull and log-Weibull variables is the reverse of that between normal and log-normal variables. That is, if $\log Z$ is normal then Z is called a log-normal variable, while if $\exp(Y)$ has a Weibull distribution then we have said that Y is a log-Weibull variable. Perhaps a more appropriate name than log-Weibull variable could have been chosen, but on the other hand one might also argue that the name “log-normal” is misapplied. We also note that the log-Weibull distribution is a form of Gumbel’s Type I extreme value distribution, while the Weibull distribution itself is related to Gumbel’s Type II distribution (Gumbel (1958), p. 157). The distribution function of the first order statistic in a sample of size n from a reduced log-Weibull distribution is

$$
\text { Prob } (X _ {1 n} <   x) = 1 - (1 - F (x)) ^ {n} = 1 - e ^ {- n e ^ {x}} = F (x + \log n).
$$

This implies that $X_{\mathrm{in}}$ has the same distribution as $X - \log n,^{*}$ and hence, in particular,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} E (X _ {1 n}) & = E (X) - \log n = - \gamma - \log n \\ \operatorname{Var} (X _ {1 n}) & = \operatorname{Var} (X) = \pi^ {2} / 6 \\ E (X _ {1 n} ^ {2}) & = \pi^ {2} / 6 + (\gamma + \log n) ^ {2}. \end{array}\tag{2.5}
$$

The moments of $X_{in}$ may be expressed in terms of the moments of $X_{1i}$ , $j \leq n$ , by setting $g(x) = x^k$ in any of the formulae (1.5)-(1.14). In particular for $g(x) = x$ , we have

$$
M (j) = E \left(X _ {1 i}\right) = - \gamma - \log j
$$

and (1.11) yields

$$
\begin{array}{r l} E (X _ {i n}) & = E (i, n) \\ & = \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {j} \Delta^ {j} M (n - j) \\ & = - \gamma - \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {j} \binom {n} {j} \Delta^ {j} \log (n - j) \end{array}\tag{2.6}
$$

Similarly for $g(x) = x^2$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} M (j) & = \pi^ {2} / 6 + \gamma^ {2} + 2 \gamma \log n + \log n ^ {2} \\ E (X _ {i n} ^ {2}) & = \pi^ {2} / 6 + \gamma^ {2} + 2 \gamma \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {i} \binom {n} {j} \Delta^ {j} \log (n - j) \\ & \quad + \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {i} \binom {- n} {j} \Delta^ {i} (\log (n - j)) ^ {2} \\ \operatorname{Var} (X _ {i n}) & = \pi^ {2} / 6 + \sum_ {j = 0} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {j} \binom {n} {j} \Delta^ {j} (\log (n - j)) ^ {2} \\ & \quad - \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {i - 1} (- 1) ^ {i} \binom {n} {j} \Delta^ {j} \log (n - j)\right) ^ {2} \end{array}\tag{2.7}
$$

Obviously any of the other formulae (1.5)-(1.14) could have been used to obtain the moments of $X_{in}$ , however, (1.11) was best adapted to the computational procedures used for the evaluation of the moments.

## 3. APPLICATIONS

We consider a life testing experiment in which n similar components are run simultaneously until k components have failed. The sample data are then

$$
t _ {1} \leq t _ {2} \leq \dots \leq t _ {k}
$$

the ordered failure times (miles, cycles, etc.). The problem then is to make certain inferences about the underlying population of failure time on the basis of the sample data.

We assume that the underlying distribution of failure times is a Weibull distribution with unknown parameters $\beta$ and $\theta$ . The problem then is to estimate $\beta$ and $\theta$ , or more generally, to estimate the distribution function

$$
\text { Prob   (Failure   time } <   t) = P (t) = 1 - \exp {(- (t / \theta) ^ {\beta})}.\tag{3.1}
$$

We start the analysis by plotting the sample data points on Weibull probability graph paper. This is a special $(t, p)$ coordinate graph paper designed so that the plot of t vs. $p = P(t)$ will appear as a straight line when $P(t)$ is a Weibull distribution function. If we rewrite (3.1) as

$$
\log t = \log \theta + (1 / \beta) \log (- \log (1 - p)),\tag{3.2}
$$

and then set

$$
y = \log t, \quad x = \log (- \log (1 - p)), \quad A = \log \theta , \quad B = 1 / \beta ,
$$

(3.1) is transformed into

$$
y = A + B x,\tag{3.3}
$$

the equation of a straight line on rectangular $(x, y)$ graph paper.

Weibull probability graph paper may be constructed from rectangular graph paper by labelling as t the grid line corresponding to $y = \log t$ and labelling as 100p the grid line corresponding to $x = \log (-\log (1 - p))$ . Thus the plot of the points $(t, p)$ , where p and t satisfy (3.1), on Weibull probability graph paper is equivalent to the plot of the points $(x, y)$ , where $y = A + Bx$ , on rectangular graph paper. We note that most Weibull probability graph paper has its axes reversed; that is, the horizontal axis is labelled t and the vertical axis is labelled p.

If we knew $p_{i} = P(t_{i})$ , for each sample failure time $t_{i}$ , then the line through the plotted points would completely determine the distribution function $P(t)$ and we would have no problem. The next step then is to determine some estimate of $p_{i}$ so that we may plot the points $(t_{i}, p_{i})$ . Since $t_{i} = t_{in}$ is the value taken on by the Weibull order statistic $T_{in}$ , we may consider $y_{i} = \log t_{i}$ as the value taken on by the log Weibull order statistic $Y_{in}$ and $x_{i} = \beta(y_{i} - \log \theta)$ as the value taken on by the reduced log Weibull order statistic $X_{in} = \beta(Y_{in} - \log \theta)$ . Thus we have

$$
\log T _ {i n} = Y _ {i n} = \log \theta + (1 / \beta) X _ {i n} = A + B X _ {i n},\tag{3.4}
$$

$$
\log t _ {i} = y _ {i} \log \theta + (1 / \beta) x _ {i} = A + B x _ {i}.
$$

The sample value $x_{i}$ depends on the unknown parameters $B(=1/\beta)$ and

$A(=\log\theta)$ , and hence can only be estimated. In this paper we shall estimate $x_{i}$ as the mean value of the reduced order statistic $X_{in}$ . That is

$$
x _ {i} = E (X _ {i n})\tag{3.5}
$$

where $E(X_{in})$ is the value listed in Table 1.

Thus we estimate the plotting position $100p_{in} = P_{in}$ as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} p _ {i n} & = \text { Prob } (T _ {i n} <   t _ {i}) = P (t _ {i}) \\ & = \text { Prob } (Y _ {i n} <   y _ {i}) \\ & = \text { Prob } (X _ {i n} <   x _ {i} = E (X _ {i n})) \\ & = 1 - \exp (- \exp (E (X _ {i n}))). \end{array}\tag{3.6}
$$

Values of $P_{in}$ as obtained from (3.6) are listed in Table 3.

This is only one of many possible procedures for plotting on probability graph paper. Other candidates for plotting positions include

$$
p _ {i n} = i / (n + 1), \quad p _ {i n} = (i -. 5) / n, \quad p _ {i n} = F (\text { median } (X _ {i n})).
$$

A survey of plotting techniques and extensive references to earlier work are given by B. F. Kimball (1960). The question of which plotting positions are “best” is still open. The plotting positions proposed here are a natural byproduct of the least squares process. In passing, we might note from Table A that our plotting positions are quite close to those generated by $p_{in} = (i - .5)/n$ .

The data in Table A are the results of a life testing experiment in which n = 100 similar components were run simultaneously until k = 15 had failed, at which time the experiment was suspended.

a. That the 20-hour reliability of these components is 95%.

b. That not more than 1% of the components will fail before 10 hours.

The parameters of the distribution may also be read from the graph. Since

$$
P (t = \theta) = 1 - \exp (- (\theta / \theta) ^ {\beta}) = 1 - e ^ {- 1} = . 6 3 2 1 2.
$$

The time at which 63.212% fail is the characteristic life $\theta$ . From the graph we estimate $\theta = 100$ . Similarly the slope $\beta$ is the slope of the line with respect to the rectangular coordinates which we estimate as $\beta = 2$ .

Rather than fit the line by eye, it is more appealing to use some analytic technique. The problem is to fit a line of the type

$$
y = A + B x
$$

to a given set of points $(x_{i}, y_{i})$ , $i = 1, k$ . The linear regression or least squares estimates of $A$ and $B$ are

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {B} = \frac {\sum (x - \bar {x}) (y - \bar {y})}{\sum (x - \bar {x}) ^ {2}} \\ \hat {A} = \bar {y} - B \bar {x}. \end{array}\tag{3.7}
$$

The estimated line is then

$$
\hat {y} = \hat {A} + \hat {B} x.
$$

The computations for our example data are given in Table A. The estimates of the slope and characteristic life of the underlying distribution of failure times are then

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {\theta} = \exp (\hat {A}) = \exp (4. 5 2 1 0 2) = 9 1. 9 2 9 7, \\ \hat {\beta} = 1 / \hat {B} = 1 /. 4 5 1 5 0 3 = 2. 2 1 4 8 3. \end{array}\tag{3.8}
$$

Corresponding to the regression line

$$
\hat {y} = \hat {A} + \hat {B} x = 4. 5 2 1 0 2 +. 4 5 1 5 0 3 x
$$

we have, in terms of the original data,

$$
\log \hat {t} = \log \hat {\theta} + (1 / \hat {\beta}) \log (- \log (1 - p))\tag{3.9}
$$

or

$$
\hat {t} = \theta (- \log (1 - p)) ^ {1 / \hat {\beta}} = 9 1. 9 2 9 7 (- \log (1 - p)). ^ {4 5 1 5 0 3}\tag{3.10}
$$

as an estimate of the time at which 100p% of the population will fail. The plot of (3.10) appears in Fig. 2.

The statistical model which leads to the estimates (3.7) assumes that the data points $y_{i}$ are the values taken on by a set of random variables $Y_{i}$ which

![](/182-134-图片/72562c90f470f34f1f400d1f9185214d80ba8f842b26fd06d4fda34ab16a83e1.jpg)

satisfy

$$
E (Y _ {i}) = A + B x _ {i}\tag{a}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var} \left(Y _ {i}\right) = \sigma^ {2}\tag{b}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Cov} \left(Y _ {i}, Y _ {j}\right) = 0 \quad (i \neq j)\tag{c}
$$

where $\sigma^{2}$ , A and B are unknown parameters and the $x_{i}$ are known constants. If (a), (b), and (c) are satisfied then the estimates $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ are unbiased and have minimum variance in the set of all linear estimators for A and B (e.g., see White (1965)).

For our problem $Y_{i} = Y_{in}$ is a log Weibull order statistic with

$$
E (Y _ {i}) = A + B E (X _ {i n}) = A + B x _ {i}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var} \left(Y _ {i}\right) = B ^ {2} \operatorname{Var} \left(X _ {i n}\right)\tag{3.11}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Cov} \left(Y _ {i}, Y _ {j}\right) = B ^ {2} \operatorname{Cov} \left(X _ {i n}, X _ {j n}\right)
$$

where $X_{in}$ is a reduced log Weibull order statistic (2.9), (2.10). Thus assumptions (b) and (c) do not hold. However, it may still be shown that the estimators $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ are unbiased (Scheffe (1959), pp. 20–21).

The so-called weighted least squares model replaces the common variance assumption (b) with the assumption

$$
\operatorname{Var} \left(Y _ {i}\right) = \sigma^ {2} / w _ {i}\tag{b'}
$$

where the $w_{i}$ are known “weights.”

The estimates for this model are

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {B} = \frac {\sum (y - \bar {y}) (x - \bar {x}) w}{\sum (x - \bar {x}) ^ {2} w} \\ \hat {A} = \bar {y} - B \bar {x} \end{array}\tag{3.12}
$$

where $\tilde{y}$ and $\bar{x}$ are the weighted means

$$
\bar {y} = \frac {\sum y w}{\sum w}, \quad \bar {x} = \frac {\sum x w}{\sum w}
$$

These estimates are unbiased and, if assumption (c) is also satisfied, they have minimum variance.

For our problem we may use the weights

$$
w _ {i} = 1 / \operatorname{Var} (X _ {i n})\tag{3.13}
$$

where $\operatorname{Var}(X_{in})$ is obtained from Table 2.

The weighted least squares computations for our example data are given in Table A. We note that the estimates of the slope and characteristic life are

$$
\begin{array}{r} \hat {\beta} = 2. 0 0 2 6 8 \\ \hat {\theta} = 1 0 4. 0 2 3 \end{array}\tag{3.14}
$$

as compared with

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \hat {\beta} & = 2. 2 1 4 8 3 \\ \hat {\theta} & = 9 1. 9 2 9 7 \end{array}
$$

the “unweighted” least squares estimates (3.8). The weighted least squares line is plotted in Fig. 2.

The “best” least squares line for this problem would be based not only on the variances of the order statistics $Y_{in}$ but also on the covariances

$$
\operatorname{Cov} \left(X _ {i n}, X _ {j n}\right) = \sigma_ {i j}.\tag{c'}
$$

If $(w_{ij})$ is the inverse of the variance-covariance matrix $(\sigma_{ij})$ then the linear unbiased minimum variance estimates of A and B are

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {B} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {k} (y _ {i} - \bar {y}) (x _ {i} - \bar {x}) w _ {i j}}{\sum \sum (x _ {i} - \bar {x}) (x _ {i} - \bar {x}) w _ {i j}} \\ \hat {A} = \bar {y} - B \bar {x} \end{array}\tag{3.15}
$$

where $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are the weighted means

$$
\bar {x} = \frac {\sum \sum x _ {i} w _ {i j}}{\sum \sum w _ {i j}} \quad y = \frac {\sum \sum y _ {i} w _ {i j}}{\sum \sum w _ {i j}}
$$

We note that if $\sigma_{ij}=0, i\neq j$ these estimates reduce to the weighted least squares estimates (3.12) and if also $\sigma_{ii}=\operatorname{Var}(Y_{i})=\sigma^{2}$ , they further reduce to the simple least squares estimates (3.7).

Unfortunately the evaluation of $\sigma_{ij}$ is complicated and tedious. Lieblein (1953) set forth the basic technique for computing $\sigma_{ij}$ for the log-Weibull distribution which he and Zelen (1956) used to compute $\sigma_{ij}$ for $1 \leq i, \leq j \leq n \leq 6$ . The Lieblein and Zellen tables have been extended to n = 20 by White (1964) and to n = 25 by Mann (1968). It seems unlikely that these tables will be extended to higher values of n, except perhaps for small values of i and j. Blom (1962) gives a method of estimating $\sigma_{ij}$ and $w_{ij}$ which gives results that appear to be close to the exact results.
