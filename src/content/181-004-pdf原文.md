## 1. INTRODUCTION
The Weibull distribution, initially proposed by Waloddi Weibull in 1939 [1], has emerged as a widely employed statistical tool in the fields of reliability engineering and failure analysis [2–4]. Its versatility in representing diverse failure modes contributes to its popularity. This distribution is defined by two essential parameters: the shape parameter ($\beta$) and the scale parameter ($\eta$). The shape parameter effectively captures the inherent characteristics of the failure mode, distinguishing between early-life, constant, or wear-out phenomena. 

In addition to the two-parameter Weibull distribution (2P WD), the three-parameter Weibull distribution (3P WD) adds a location parameter ($\gamma$). This allows the distribution to be shifted along the x-axis, providing greater flexibility in modeling failure data. Although 3P WD is more complex than 2P WD, it better reflects the actual situation in modeling failure life data and is thus widely used in reliability studies. 3P WD is particularly utilized in dealing with structural fatigue and reliability [5,6], and in evaluating the wear life of mechanical parts. 

The probability density function (PDF) of 3P WD is 
$$f(t) = \frac{\beta}{\eta} \left( \frac{t - \gamma}{\eta} \right)^{\beta-1} \exp\left[ -\left( \frac{t - \gamma}{\eta} \right)^\beta \right]; \tag{1}$$
the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of 3P WD is 

$$F(t) = 1 - \exp\left[ -\left( \frac{t - \gamma}{\eta} \right)^\beta \right], \tag{2}$$
where $\beta > 0, \eta > 0, \gamma > 0, t \geq \gamma$. It can be described for 2P WD when $\gamma$ is equal to 0. 
The mean $E(t)$ and the variance $\text{Var}(t)$ of 3P WD are given by 

$$E(t) = \eta \Gamma\left( 1 + \frac{1}{\beta} \right) + \gamma, \tag{3}$$

$$\text{Var}(t) = \eta^2 \left[ \Gamma\left( 1 + \frac{2}{\beta} \right) - \Gamma^2\left( 1 + \frac{1}{\beta} \right) \right], \tag{4}$$
where $\Gamma$ represents the Gamma function.

### 2.1 Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), a highly efficient and versatile approach for parameter estimation, holds a crucial role in tackling parameter estimation challenges, particularly in the context of incomplete lifetime problems. It offers distinct advantages, yet solving the three transcendental equations through an iterative method can prove considerably intricate.

The log-likelihood function of 3P WD is

$$\ln L(t_1,\cdots,t_n;\beta,\eta,\gamma)= n\ln\beta+(\beta-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(t_i-\gamma)-n\beta\ln\eta-n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{t_i-\gamma}{\eta}\right)^\beta .(5)$$

Smith (1985)[16] and Hirose (1996) [17] argued that when$1 < \beta < 2$, the maximum likelihood estimate of γ is generally present but not asymptotically normal; when$\beta \geq 2$, the maximum likelihood solution is always present and the information matrix is asymptotically normal, which is important in interpreting the assumptions of the Weibull model. The traditional MLE for solving the likelihood equation is no longer applicable and one of the possibilities to address the difficulty is to transform it into an optimization problem. Abdassa (2006) [18] proposed a simulated annealing algorithm (SA) for MLE with good results. Barakat and Sakalaukas (2008) [19] proposed an improved analytical algorithm for MLE. Moenii (2013) [20] proposed a cross-entropy method to compute MLE. The new method was analyzed in comparison with two existing methods (the neural network method and the hybrid VNA-SA method) through a simulation study. The simulation results in this paper reveal the superior performance of the proposed method in terms of accuracy, precision, and computational efficiency for different parameter configurations and sample sizes. Particle swarm optimization (PSO) is a population-based heuristic optimization technique that has been widely explored by researchers (Orkcu (2015) [21], Acitas et al. (2019) [22]). The efficacy of PSO relies heavily on its control parameters, including inertia weights and acceleration coefficients, and slight variations in these settings can produce different performance results. There are other algorithms for MLE of 3P WD, such as the covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES) [23], the differential evolution optimization method (DEOM) (2021) [24], and the dragonfly algorithm (DA) [25]. In Abdessalem’s work [26], an effective acceptance-rejection algorithm, combined with a sophisticated nested sampling technique, was introduced for the MLE of 3P WD. Through extensive Monte Carlo simulations, the algorithm’s effectiveness and robustness were demonstrated. Moreover, domestic scholars [27-32] have also contributed to the research on MLE solutions.

### 2.2 Modified maximum likelihood estimation (MMLE)
Cohen and Whitten (1982) [9] proposed four improved maximum likelihood methods based on a log-likelihood function (Eq. (5)) for 3P WD. When$\beta$is less than 2.2156, MMLE is better than MLE. MMLE applies to both the estimation of complete data and censored data. The log-likelihood function (Eq. (5)) is maximized according to the following constraints: 

$$
\ln\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)=\left(\frac{t_{(0)}^{(n)}-\gamma}{\eta}\right)^{\beta}, \tag{6}
$$

$$
E\left(t_{(1)}\right)=\gamma+\eta n^{\frac{1}{\beta}} \Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\beta}+1\right), \tag{7}
$$

$$
\bar{\tau}=\gamma+\eta \Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\beta}+1\right), \tag{8}
$$

$$
t_m=\gamma+\eta(\ln (2))^{\frac{1}{\beta}}, \tag{9}
$$

where$t_m$is the sample median. 

### 2.3 Weighted maximum likelihood estimation (WMLE)
In Cousineau’s publication (2009) [10], a weighted maximum likelihood (WMLE) method was introduced to alleviate the inherent bias present in the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) equation. This approach is applicable when the global parameters are known, but the expected weights remain unknown. Notably, the two expected values of the three weights solely rely on the sample size, while the third weight additionally depends on the population shape parameter. The WMLE technique can be succinctly characterized as follows: 

$$
\{\hat{\beta}, \hat{\gamma}\}=\arg \min \left\{\frac{W_1}{\beta}+\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(t_i-\gamma\right)^{\beta} \ln \left(t_i-\gamma\right)-\frac{\left(\bar{t}-\gamma\right)^{\beta} \ln (\bar{t}-\gamma)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(t_i-\gamma\right)^{\beta}}\right\}, \tag{10}
$$

$$
[\hat{\eta} | \hat{\beta}, \hat{\gamma}]_{W M L E}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n W_1} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(t_i-\hat{\gamma}\right)^{\hat{\beta}}}. \tag{11}
$$

The median of W1, W2, and W3 can be used, see Cousineau (2009) [10]. 

### 2.4 Maximum product spacing (MPS)
MPS emerges as a compelling alternative to the conventional MLE technique, catering to the parameter estimation of continuous univariate distributions, as proposed by Cheng and Amin (1983) [11]. By rectifying potential inaccuracies in the ML estimator, the MPS method endeavors to recalibrate the continuous univariate distribution. Notably, the MPS estimator exhibits the desirable properties of asymptotic normality and efficiency, as meticulously demonstrated by Cheng and Amin (1979) [11]. Herein, the underlying workings of the MPS method are shown in the following manner: 

$$
S(\beta, \eta, \gamma)=\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \ln \left[F\left(t_{(i)} ; \beta, \eta, \gamma\right)-F\left(t_{(i-1)} ; \beta, \eta, \gamma\right)\right]-\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \ln \left\{\exp \left[-\left(\frac{t_{(i)}-\gamma}{\eta}\right)^{\beta}\right]-\exp \left[-\left(\frac{t_{(i-1)}-\gamma}{\eta}\right)^{\beta}\right]\right\}, \tag{12}
$$

where$F\left(t_{(1)} ; \beta, \eta, \gamma\right)=0$,$F\left(t_{(n+1)} ; \beta, \eta, \gamma\right)=1$,$m=n+1$. When Eq. (12) obtains the maximum value, it is the estimated value obtained. When$\beta$is less than 1, it is not available, but the MPS method exists. MPS applies to the estimation of complete data, not to the estimation of censored data. Quantile Maximum Product Estimation (QMPE) [13-15] is the generalization of MPS. We choose not to study QMPE here because the quartile is unknown. 

## 3. MINIMIZING ADEQUACY
The most common minimization method used for 3P WD is the least squares estimation (LSE) [33]. There are other methods such as the weighted least squares estimation (WLSE) [34], the linear regression estimation (LRE) [35-38], the bi-linear regression estimation (BLRE) [39], the minimum discrepancy estimation (MDE) [40], and the errors-in-variables (EIV) [41-42]. 

### 3.1 Least squares estimation (LSE)
Least squares estimation (LSE), as presented in reference [33], enjoys significant popularity in parameter estimation. It leverages the sum of squared errors of CDF as a performance metric. By minimizing the sum of squared errors of the CDF, the estimation results can be obtained: 

$$
\left(\hat{\beta}_{L S E}, \hat{\eta}_{L S E}, \hat{\gamma}_{L S E}\right)=\arg \min \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[F\left(t_i\right)-\hat{F}_i\right]^2, \tag{13}
$$

where$\hat{F}_i$can use the probability estimators. The median rank estimator of failure probability can be used for$F(t)$: 

$$
F\left(t_i\right)=\frac{i-0.3}{n+0.4}(i=1,2, \cdots, n). \tag{14}
$$



---


where i denotes the rank number of the product life$t_i$in the$n$sample outcomes. LSE applies to both the estimation of complete data and censored data. For small sample sizes, LSE has a low estimation accuracy. In addition, a weight can be added to target the least squares method to become a weighted least squares estimate (WLSE) [34]:

$$
(\hat{\beta}_{WLSE}, \hat{\eta}_{WLSE}, \hat{\gamma}_{WLSE}) = \arg \min_{(\beta,\eta,\gamma)} \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i [F(t_i) - \hat{F}_i]^2 , \tag{15}
$$

where$w_i$is the weight such as$w_i = \frac{(i+1)^2(n+2)}{(n-i+1)(n+1)}$.

### 3.2 Linear regression estimation (LRE)

Due to its simplicity and user-friendliness, linear regression analysis remains the predominant approach for parameter estimation. In this technique, Equation (2) can be transformed into a linear form as follows:

$$
\ln(-\ln(1-F)) = \beta \ln(t-\gamma) - \beta \ln \eta . \tag{16}
$$

$\gamma$can be obtained by LRE when the sample correlation coefficient Eq. (17) obtains the maximum value [37,38]:

$$
R(\gamma) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i - n \overline{x} \overline{y}}{\sqrt{\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2 - n \overline{x}^2 \right) \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i^2 - n \overline{y}^2 \right)}} , \tag{17}
$$

where$x_i = \ln(t_i - \gamma)$,$y_i = \ln(-\ln(1-F_i))$,$\overline{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i / n$,

$\overline{y} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i / n$.

In practical applications, it is a reasonable approach to impose a specific condition, denoted as $0 \leq \gamma < t_{(1)}$. Then$\gamma$can be estimated by

$$
\hat{\gamma}_{LRE} = \arg \max R(\gamma) . \tag{18}
$$

Eq. (19) can be used to obtain$\beta$and$\eta$as follows:

$$
\begin{cases}
\hat{\beta}_{LRE} = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i - n \overline{x} \overline{y}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2 - n \overline{x}^2} , \\
\hat{\eta}_{LRE} = \exp\left( -\dfrac{\overline{y} - \hat{\beta}_{LRE} \overline{x}}{\hat{\beta}_{LRE}} \right)
\end{cases} \tag{19}
$$

LRE is applicable to both complete and censored samples, which has the accuracy to meet engineering requirements. When the sample is relatively small, the estimation results are better than other minimization methods.

In addition, the bi-linear regression estimation method (BLRE) is a relatively high-accuracy regression method. However, it has the disadvantage that in the process of numerical iterative calculation, there may be some negative samples, in which case their logarithm is contrary to the mathematical principles of logarithmic calculation. It cannot be performed, so other estimation methods have to be used instead [39].

### 3.3 Minimum discrepancy estimation (MDE)

Xie (2022) [40] introduced a minimum discrepancy methodology, aiming to derive precise estimates of$\beta$and$\gamma$by minimizing the standard deviation of the estimated scale parameter values. Through this meticulous approach,$\eta$ultimately estimated as the arithmetic mean of the estimated scale parameter values.

By minimizing the disparities among the scale parameters, a robust estimation of the correct shape parameters and location parameters can be accomplished, leading to a comprehensive characterization of the model:

$$
(\hat{\beta}_{MDE}, \hat{\gamma}_{MDE}) = \arg \min_{(\beta,\gamma)} std\left( \dfrac{t_i - \gamma}{(-\ln(1-F_i))^{1/\beta}} \right), \tag{20}
$$

where$F_i$can use the probability estimators.

To mitigate the impact of sample values on the uncertainty associated with the estimation of the scale parameter in the Weibull distribution, employing the average value emerges as a viable strategy for scale parameter estimation. By calculating the mean value from the sample, a more stable and reliable estimate of the scale parameter can be obtained:

$$
\hat{\eta}_{MDE} = \dfrac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \dfrac{t_i - \hat{\gamma}_{MDE}}{(-\ln(1-F_i))^{1/\hat{\beta}_{MDE}}} . \tag{21}
$$

MDE is applicable to both complete and censored samples. When the sample is small, the accuracy of estimation results is better than MLE and LSE.

### 3.4 Errors-in-variables (EIV)

In recent times, the utilization of errors-in-variables methodology [41-42] has gained prominence in the estimation of parameters of 3P WD. In this context, the selection of an appropriate performance metric is to be minimized by:

$$
(\hat{\beta}_{EIV}, \hat{\eta}_{EIV}, \hat{\gamma}_{EIV}) = \arg \min_{(\beta,\eta,\gamma)} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (t_i - t_{(i)})^2 , \tag{22}
$$

---


where$t_i (i=1,2, \ldots, n)$are the failure data.
$$
t_{(i)} = \eta \left[ -\ln(1 - F_i) \right]^{1/\beta} + \gamma , \tag{23}
$$
where$F_i$can use the probability estimators.
EIV is applicable to both complete and censored samples. When the sample size is small, the accuracy of the estimation results is low.
## 4. MOMENT TECHNOLOGY
The basic idea of the method of moments is that the sample moment is equal to the population moment, so the other parameters can be accordingly estimated.
### 4.1 Method of Moment (MM)
Let$\overline{T}$,$S^2$and$S'$denote the mean, variance, and skewness of the samples. MM can be represented by the following equations Dodson (2006) [43]:
$$
S'^3 = \frac{\Gamma\left( \frac{3}{\beta} + 1 \right) - 3\Gamma\left( \frac{2}{\beta} + 1 \right)\Gamma\left( \frac{1}{\beta} + 1 \right) + 2\Gamma^3\left( \frac{1}{\beta} + 1 \right)}{\left[ \Gamma\left( \frac{2}{\beta} + 1 \right) - \Gamma^2\left( \frac{1}{\beta} + 1 \right) \right]^{3/2}}, \tag{24}
$$
$$
S^2 = \eta^2 \left[ \Gamma\left( \frac{2}{\beta} + 1 \right) - \Gamma^2\left( \frac{1}{\beta} + 1 \right) \right], \tag{25}
$$
$$
\overline{T} = \gamma + \eta \Gamma\left( \frac{1}{\beta} + 1 \right). \tag{26}
$$
The shape parameters can be determined by solving Eq (24). The value of$\eta$can be obtained by substituting the estimated$\beta$into Eq (25). Moreover, the location parameters can be found by substituting the estimated$\beta$and$\eta$into Eq (26).
However, it is essential to note that MM might lack accuracy when dealing with small sample sizes. Additionally, it is applicable only to complete samples and not suitable for censored data.
Similar to MMLE, the modified moment’s method uses the estimated parameters of the constraints, and Cohen and Whitten (1982) [9] proposed three modified moments methods (MMM) with Eqs. (6), (7), and (9).
### 4.2 L-Moment (LM)
The L-moment technique, as outlined by reference [44], operates by equating specific sample L-moments ($l_r$) to the corresponding population L-moments ($\lambda_r$), as the commonly used moment method:
$$
\lambda_r = l_r \quad \forall r = 1,2,\ldots,p , \tag{27}
$$
where$p$is the number of the estimated parameters. Within the context of 3P WD, three equations are required.
The L-moment method relies on the application of sequential statistics. Consider the quantile random variable$T$associated with the Weibull distribution function. The L-moments of$T$are:
$$
\lambda_r = \frac{1}{r} \sum_{k=0}^{r-1} (-1)^k \binom{r-1}{k} E(T_{r-k:r}) \quad \forall r = 1,2,\ldots, \tag{28}
$$
where the expectation of the order statistics can be defined as
$$
E(T_{r:s}) = \int_0^\infty \frac{rt}{(s-r)!(r-1)!} [F(t)]^{r-1} [1-F(t)]^{s-r} dF(t) . \tag{29}
$$
The unbiased estimates$b_r$are set to
$$
b_r = n^{-1} \binom{n-1}{r}^{-1} \sum_{j=r+1}^{n} \binom{j-1}{r} T_{j:n} . \tag{30}
$$
$l_r$can be defined as
$$
l_{r+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{r} p_{r,k} b_k \quad r = 0,1,\ldots,n-1 , \tag{31}
$$
where
$$
p_{r,k} = (-1)^{r+k} \binom{r+1}{k} \binom{r+k}{k} . \tag{32}
$$
LM needs fewer constants and an iterative procedure, which is usually less complex to compute. And it is only applicable to censored data.
### 4.3 TL-Moment (TLM)
The TL-moment technique, as outlined by reference [45-47], operates by equating specific sample TL-moments ($m'_r$) to the corresponding population TL-moments ($\mu'_r$).
The TL moment is given as
$$
\mu'_r = \frac{1}{r} \sum_{k=0}^{r-1} (-1)^k C_r^k E(X_{r-k:r+k+2}) . \tag{33}
$$
The sample TL-moments are defined as:
$$
m'_r = \frac{\sum_{i=r+1}^{n} (-1)^{i-r-1} C_n^{i-1} C_{i-1}^{r} C_{n-i+r}^{r} X_{(i)}}{C_{n+r}^{2r+1}} . \tag{34}
$$

---


TLM is more robust than LM. Unlike L-moment estimation, TL-moment estimation can be applied to moments of distribution at infinity. Teimouri (2020) [48] performed calculations to show that for 3P WD, TLM using the Cramer-Von Mises criterion outperforms other estimation methods in terms of performance. The TL-moment method is only applicable to the case of complete samples, not to censored data.

### 4.4 Probability-weighted moment (PWM)
The probability-weighted moment (PWM) was first proposed by Greenwood et al. in 1979. PWM of the test samples [49-50] are

$$M_{1,0,k} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \left( 1 - \frac{i-0.35}{n} \right)^k , k = 0,1,3 . \tag{35}$$

The kth-order probability-weight moments of the Weibull distribution are

$$M_{1,0,k} = \frac{\gamma}{1+k} + \frac{\eta \Gamma(1+1/\beta)}{(1+k)^{1/\beta}} . \tag{36}$$

The three parameters of WD are obtained by making the PWM of the sample equal to the PWM of the population. Han et al. (2021) [51] used a 3P WD-based curve correction to obtain S-N curve expressions of cast steel base materials and butt welds more accurate than existing methods. PWM is used to estimate parameters to obtain the corrosion fatigue S-N curve, which does not require a complicated iterative process, easy to calculate. The estimator based on a PWM has excellent statistical properties and can provide better initial values for parameter estimation. It is expressed in an explicit function, which requires iterative methods to solve the equations. In the case of small samples, the estimation results are poor. PWM is only applicable to the case of complete samples and does not apply to censored data.

## 5. GREY ESTIMATION
The grey model method is a relatively practical estimation method based on the grey system principle proposed by Deng [52,53]. The basic principle is as follows. Eq. (2) can be converted to

$$t = \gamma + \eta \exp \left[ \frac{ \ln \left( -\ln \left( 1 - F(t) \right) \right) }{\beta} \right] , \tag{37}$$

where$x_t = \ln \left( -\ln \left( 1 - F(t) \right) \right)$,$\eta = c_1^{-1} = \frac{1}{-\alpha}$;$\gamma = b$, then the above equation can be transformed into

$$t_t = c \exp(-ax_t) + b . \tag{38}$$

The gray model GM (1,1) differentiates the time series and the differential equation is obtained as:

$$\frac{d i(x_t)}{dx_t} + a i(x_t) = u . \tag{39}$$

Its time response is Eq. (38), which can estimate$\eta, \beta, \gamma$by the relevant parameters$a, u, c$using the “gray model” GM (1,1).

Zheng (2002) [54] applied an improved direct modeling method for the first-order gray differential equation model. A gray estimation method that estimates 3P WD without iteration can be derived, which is simple. Yan (2005) [55] pointed out that the gray estimation method still has high accuracy even when the sample size is very small. Ma (2017) [56] proposed to estimate 3P WD which is applied to the wind speed by the gray estimation method. The gray estimation method has the advantages of a low number of iterations, fast solution speed, and high estimation accuracy, and can adapt to different numbers of samples. The Monte Carlo simulation was used to validate the algorithm, and the validation results showed that the gray estimation method can estimate 3P WD relatively accurately. In their publication (Liu and Xie, 2022) [57], a discrete variant of the gray three-parameter Weibull model was introduced. Subsequently, a two-stage hybrid approach, incorporating linear and nonlinear least square principles, was proposed for estimating 3P WD using the gray model. This novel gray estimation method enhances the efficacy of existing gray estimation methods and finds practical application in the frequency analysis of Weibull data. The gray estimation method can have high estimation accuracy in the case of small samples, but it is more sensitive to the data and the parameter estimation, and is not stable enough. The gray estimation method should only be applicable to complete samples and does not apply to censored data. The discrete form of the gray estimation method can be estimated for small samples and is more stable and effective than the GM (1,1) gray estimation method.

## 6. METHOD OF CONSTRUCTING STATISTICS
In recent years, some statisticians have constructed statistics to estimate a parameter of 3P WD, such as γ or β. The statistics constructed by Chen (2004) and Chen (2009) [58,59] for γ independent of η and β were used to find point estimates and confidence intervals for γ. The best choices of i and k are found. Point estimates for γ of 3P WD are also discussed. Compared with MLE, this paper provides a simpler, more efficient, and more accurate method for estimating γ. In their publication (Susugiyama et al., 2019) [60], a minimum variance linear unbiased estimation technique (T_j^{MVE}) was introduced, employing order statistics (T_{(j)}) as foundation for estimating γ, which requires all order statistics. And it was verified that the estimation results were better than other methods when the sample size n = 5, and an example of the

---


fatigue life of 10 bearings for parameter estimation by McCool (1974)[61] was given. For the shape parameter, Nagatsuka et al. (2003)[62] proposed a new three-parameter Weibull inference method capable of estimating only$\beta$. Independent of$\gamma$and$\eta$, Teimouri et al. (2013)[63] proposed a new estimation of the shape parameter using the correlation coefficient between the sample and the corresponding rank with the following properties: expression closure, simplicity, and asymptotic unbiaseness. Simulations show that the new method works comparable to the maximum likelihood estimation. Nagatsuka et al. (2014)[64] introduced a notable technique, known as the location and scale parameter-free maximum likelihood (LSF) method. The primary objective of this method is to circumvent the issue of unbounded likelihood, by employing constructed statistics that are independent of$\gamma$and$\eta$, instead of the original samples. In the case of quantile estimation, all three estimates need to be performed simultaneously. To avoid the problems that arise with the LSF method, Nagatsuka et al. (2015)[65] proposed another new statistic independent of$\gamma$, which leads to a single number for degenerate variables. Ogura (2020)[66] devised an approach for estimating$\beta$by deriving an unbiased estimator of$\beta$that are uncorrelated with$\gamma$and$\eta$. The minimum variance linear unbiased estimation technique is employed subsequently. As the proposed method incorporates hyperparameters, an optimal selection of these hyperparameters is obtained through Monte Carlo simulation. The hyperparameters required to estimate$\beta$depend on the sample size. Promising outcomes were achieved when the sample shape parameter exhibited a value of 2 or higher. The utility of the method is demonstrated by the analysis of a numerical data set. Sugiyama (2022)[67] varied two ideas to improve the performance of the minimum variance linear estimation of hyperparameters (MVLE-H) method based on Ogura (2020)[66].

The constructive static method is basically for the estimation of a single parameter of 3P WD and requires a mixture of other methods to estimate all three parameters.

## 7. BAYESIAN METHOD

In terms of its theoretical foundation, the Bayesian method diverges from other parameter estimation techniques. Within the Bayesian framework, the key components include prior distributions and posterior distributions. The prior distribution is known, while the posterior distribution is formed by combining the prior distribution with the sample information, following Bayes’ rule. Subsequently, point and interval estimates for the parameters can be derived from the posterior distribution.

Let$\pi(\theta)$be the prior distribution.$\pi(\theta|t)$is denoted as the posterior distribution.$\theta=(\theta_1,\cdots,\theta_l)$is the model parameter,$t=(t_1,\cdots,t_n)$is a set of sample data, and$f(t|\theta)$is the probability density function of the sample. According to Bayes’ law, the density function expression of Bayes’ formula can be obtained as

$$
\pi(\theta|t)=\frac{f(t|\theta)\pi(\theta)}{\int f(t|\theta)\pi(\theta)d\theta}. \tag{40}
$$

Green (1994)[68] demonstrated that MLE of$\gamma$in negative values, which is usually inappropriate for young trees. A Bayesian model was then proposed and fitted to three data sets using a Gibbs sampler. The latter model is easy to implement and guarantees a positive estimation of$\gamma$. Hall and Wang (2005)[69] considered MLE to estimate$\gamma$, the endpoint of a distribution. The likelihood function is imposed on$\gamma$modified by the prior distribution. The prior is explicit and modified by a general form adapted to itself depending on the setting. Bayesian methods can be applied to parameter estimation of 3P WD and gamma distributions. PDF of 3P WD is more complicated. The calculation of the integral of the posterior distribution becomes the main obstacle. Liu (2007)[70] studied the computational problem of Bayes estimation using the Gibbs sampling algorithm. Firstly, the characteristics of the Gibbs sampling algorithm were described, the joint posterior distribution of parameters without prior information was derived in the case of a fixed number of censored life tests, and then, the Gibbs sampling scheme for conduction parametric Bayes estimation was designed in conjunction with the trade-off sampling method. Tang (2009)[71] proposed two methods for Bayes estimation of three parameters, one based on the Laplace numerical integration method and the other based on the Gibbs sampling method. Simulation examples illustrate the validity of the estimation methods. Wei et al. (2015)[72] used the Hybrid Gibbs algorithm (a mixture of Gibbs sampling and Metropolis algorithm) to give a Bayesian estimation of 3P WD for complete samples and a fixed number of censored samples. The Bayesian estimates and confidence intervals of the parameters are obtained by Monte Carlo simulation. Song et al. (2017)[73] used the Bayesian-Monte Carlo method to analyze the reliability assessment of three-parameter Weibull distribution under small samples, considering the current reality of small-sample failure data of complex products or systems with high reliability and less failures. Teimouri (2020)[74] considered the Bayesian estimation of the parameters proposed by Green (1994)[68]. The proposed sampling algorithm is faster than Green et al. (1994)[68]. The core of Bayesian theory lies in the Bayesian formula, which integrates the prior information, the population information, and the sample information for parameter fusion. 3P WD, as a formally relatively complicated failure distribution, does not have a corresponding conjugate distribution, which makes the determination of its prior distribution more difficult. The integral calculation of the posterior distribution is the main difficulty of Bayes estimation and needs to be performed using some sampling methods. Bayesian estimation can address small sample problems, both for complete samples and censored samples.

## 8. ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE METHOD

In Weibull reliability analysis, the least squares method has been used in the past in terms of fitting data points, while a

---


theory of machine learning laws specializing in the case of small samples, support vector machine theory, has been developed in recent years and is considered to be the best [75], which can be used to address the problem of data fitting. Zhang (2012) [76] presented a Weibull reliability analysis approach that utilizes the least squares support vector machine based on gray estimation method. The outcome shows that the least squares support vector machine exhibits superior fitting accuracy in Weibull reliability analysis, surpassing both the traditional least squares method and the support vector machine. This indicates that the proposed method can enhance the efficiency and accuracy of reliability analysis, establishing itself as a novel technique for Weibull reliability analysis, particularly suitable for scenarios involving small sample sizes. Petkovic (2014) [77] estimated the Weibull distribution parameters by employing polynomials and radial basis functions (RBFs) as kernel functions for support vector machines (SVMs). The findings reveal that the support vector machine outperforms other numerical methods in terms of accuracy and validity. Yin (2017) [78] pointed out that the support vector regression machine (SVR) maintains the characteristics of the support vector machine applicable to small samples. Similarly, many scholars [79-81] applied support vector machines in the parameter estimation of 2P WD. To effectively estimate the parameters of the 3P WD reliability model for mining equipment under small sample conditions, Wang (2021) [82] proposed a parameter estimation method based on GM-noise SVR. The method estimates γ and β based on the gray estimation method (GM). It estimates β and γ using an e-band support vector regression machine (e-SVR) based on the number of training samples and noise parameter search. Moreover, Abbas (2008) [83] put forward a straightforward artificial neural network model aimed at the simultaneous estimation of the three Weibull parameters. This neural network incorporates the concept of the method of moments to estimate parameters by utilizing statistics such as the mean, standard deviation, median, skewness, and kurtosis. The efficacy of the proposed method, based on artificial neural networks, is substantiated. Nonetheless, it is recommended to apply this method in scenarios with large sample sizes.

## 9. OTHER METHODS

In addition to the aforementioned approaches, alternative methods have been proposed by scholars. Markovic (2009) [84] presented a hybrid method that combines non-parametric and parametric techniques. The fundamental concept is to initiate the estimation process with optimal nonparametric density estimation and subsequently apply the nonlinear least squares method to estimate the unknown parameters. A key outcome of this work is the establishment of a theorem regarding the existence of least-squares estimates. When the initial density is of sufficient quality, the estimation results prove satisfactory. Jin (2014) [85] proposed the least-squares relative entropy parametric method along with the bootstrap method for the reliability assessment of 3P WD. Xie (2020) [86] introduced a novel approach for estimating γ, based on four right-truncated lifetime data. Leveraging the properties of the normal distribution, knowledge of the product life distribution, and Monte Carlo simulation results, a nonparametric estimation method was proposed for estimating γ. Yang (2022) [87] developed a theoretical model that describes the relationship between γ and the minimum sample value, along with proposing an iterative method to estimate η and β while fixing β. By conducting Monte Carlo simulations and comparing against MLE, Weibull plot with the sample correlation coefficient, and TL-moment, it was verified that the iterative method achieves greater accuracy and effectiveness.

## 10. COMPARISONS

A comprehensive delineation of the application scope for the aforementioned methods is provided below. The maximizing adequacy approach, excluding MPS, the minimizing adequacy technique, and the Bayesian method are applicable to both complete and censored data. Conversely, the moment technology, grey estimation, and method of constructing statistics are exclusively suitable for complete data and do not accommodate censored data. In general, MLE is the preferred choice owing to its efficiency and asymptotic properties when dealing with large sample sizes. WMLE, on the other hand, performs better in small sample estimation but necessitates knowledge of the population shape parameter. The grey estimation technique proves useful for small sample sizes; however, it exhibits greater sensitivity to small sample sizes, lacks stability. The Bayesian method, linear regression estimation (LRE), minimum discrepancy estimation (MDE), and the scenario method proposed by Yang are advantageous in scenarios involving relatively small sample sizes. Conversely, other methods of moments such as MM, LM, TLM, and PMM exhibit reduced accuracy in small samples. It is worth noting that all the aforementioned methods exhibit bias with small sample sizes, but they demonstrate consistency.

## 11. CONCLUSION

The three-parameter Weibull distribution (3P WD) is extensively employed in engineering, reliability, and survival analysis as a prominent distribution for modeling failure data. Parameter estimation assumes a pivotal role in the analysis of Weibull distribution commonly utilized methodologies for estimating the parameters of 3P WD including the maximizing adequacy (MLE, MMLE, WMLE, and MPS), the minimizing adequacy (LSE, WLSE, LRE, MDE, and ELV), the moment technology (MM, MMM, LM, TLM, and PWM), the grey estimation, the method of constructing statistics, the Bayesian estimation, the artificial intelligence method, and other methods. Each method exhibits its unique set of advantages and disadvantages, and the selection of the most appropriate approach relies on the specific application requirements and the nature of the available data.
