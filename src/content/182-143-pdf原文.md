# Bayesian likelihood methods for estimating the end point of a distribution

Peter Hall

Australian National University, Canberra, Australia

and Julian Z. Wang

Australian National University, Canberra, and University ofWestern Sydney, Australia

[Received June 2004, Revised May 2005]

Summary. We consider maximum likelihood methods for estimating the end point of a distribution. The likelihood function is modified by a prior distribution that is imposed on the location parameter. The prior is explicit and meaningful, and has a general form that adapts itself to different settings. Results on convergence rates and limiting distributions are given. In particular, it is shown that the limiting distribution is non-normal in non-regular cases. Parametric bootstrap techniques are suggested for quantifying the accuracy of the estimator. We illustrate performance by applying the method to multiparameter Weibull and gamma distributions.

Keywords: Non-regular problems; Prior; Shape parameter; Spacings

## 1. Introduction

Let a probability density be of the form

$$
f (x) \sim (x - \theta) ^ {\alpha - 1} g (x | \theta , \omega), \qquad \theta <   x,\tag{1.1}
$$

where θ is a single unknown location parameter, ω is a vector of parameters other than θ and $g ( x | \theta , \omega )$ converges to a strictly positive constant as x decreases to θ. Throughout, α will be taken to be an unknown shape parameter. Distributions of the type (1.1) include the Weibull and gamma distributions. In extreme value theory the generalized extreme value distribution and the generalized Pareto distribution are closely related to this form.

More generally, the polynomial ‘shape’ of f at expression (1.1) produces data whose logdistances from the end point are in simple proportion, i.e., if the data are $X _ { 1 } \leqslant . . . \leqslant X _ { n }$ , and n is large and r is small to moderate, then there is an approximate linear relationship between r and log $( \theta - X _ { r } )$ , for $r \geqslant 1$ . The variety of settings to which this law applies is extremely wide; see Zipf (1941, 1949). This prevalence is sometimes explained through the ‘thermodynamic property’ that the power law shape off, at expression (1.1), minimizes entropy for a given amount of information per data value.

Since the support of f depends on θ, the classical asymptotic properties of the maximum likelihood method fail to apply in this case (LeCam, 1970). There are intensive studies of the behaviour of the maximum likelihood method applied to this type of distribution. Smith (1985) and Smith and Weissman (1985) made a comprehensive study of this subject. During the last decade there has been a significant resurgence in interest in problems where non-regularity is caused by dependence of the boundary on unknowns. This occurs particularly in economics. See, for example, work on censored or truncated regression models (e.g. Breen (1996) and Long (1997)), market structure analysis (e.g. Robinson and Chiang (1996)) and auction models (e.g. Chernozhukov and Hong (2004)), and also the further references that are given in Section 4.

The problem of non-regular estimation emerges when the shape parameter α satisfies $\alpha \leqslant 1$ and the likelihood function fails to produce a solution other than ${ \widehat { \theta } } = X _ { 1 }$ , the smallest observation. Various methods have been sought to overcome the problem. Chiefly, there are two sorts of suggestion: methods that are based on the likelihood function with modifications, e.g. the corrected likelihood of Cheng and Iles (1987), and methods based on other quantities, such as groups or spacings of observations. The grouping method was discussed by Giesbrecht and Kempthorne (1976) and the spacing method was studied by Cheng and Amin (1983).

In general the likelihood approach is preferred, partly because it tends to perform better than its competitors when it is applicable. More importantly, the likelihood function and related inference procedures and methods, such as the likelihood ratio test and the Bayesian method, are well established and readily available for use in practice. See, for example, Gelman et al. (2004).

Cheng and Iles (1987) stated that the use ofthe product ofdensities for the likelihood function was a misapplication of the maximum likelihood method in non-regular cases. They suggested a corrected likelihood in which $f ( X _ { 1 } )$ / was replaced by $\int _ { X _ { 1 } } ^ { X _ { 1 } + h } f ( x )$ dx, where $h > 0$ . The difficulty in using this method is the practical choice of $h ,$ , which is yet to be addressed. When $\alpha < 1$ , the method leads to ${ \hat { \theta } } = X _ { 1 }$ , which is clearly biased. No result was given for $\alpha = 1$ . Lockhart and Stephens (1994) have suggested an iterative bias correction method for the particular case of the three-parameter Weibull distribution. It requires estimation of all three parameters.

Our view is that a piece of vital information is missed in the conventional likelihood. In other words, the information that is provided by the product is incomplete and conditional. Therefore a conditional likelihood seems to be more appropriate. The condition can be represented from a Bayesian point of view, in the form of a prior distribution.

In this paper we propose a sample spacing-based empirical prior for θ. The prior is empirical, in that it relies on empirical spacings to determine shape and scale, and incorporates only minimal information from outside the data, in that it has a simple monotone shape near the smallest order statistic $X _ { 1 }$ . Away from $X _ { 1 }$ the prior plays a negligible role in inference. Therefore, the prior might fairly be said to be informative, and empirical, only in a near neighbourhood of $X _ { 1 }$ and uninformative elsewhere. Alternative Bayesian approaches, which use priors that exert more influence over the estimator, are difficult to justify if there is not clear evidence for the assumptions that are made. Moreover, a purely Bayesian approach to solving non-regular problems in statistics, including those where unknown parameters determine boundaries, suffers from its own intrinsic difficulties. See, for example, Akahira and Takeuchi (1995) and Ghosal and Samanta (1995).

Combining the prior with the product of the densities we obtain a penalized likelihood function that inevitably attains a global maximum and thereby provides simultaneous solutions as estimates of the parameters. The prior proposed provides an approach to Bayesian data analysis in non-regular cases.

In Section 2 we discuss the penalized likelihood function and its asymptotic behaviour. The methods that we use to describe the limiting distribution of our estimator also allow us to give a representation for the limiting distribution in the classical unpenalized case. See Section 2.3.4. Hitherto the distribution has been available only via a complex mathematical formula (Woodroofe, 1974). We illustrate the performance of the proposed Bayesian likelihood method in Section 3 by simulation on the Weibull and gamma distributions. Concluding remarks are made in Section 4. There we also assess the performance of bootstrap methods.

## 2. Maximum likelihood estimation of end points

## 2.1. Methodology

## 2.1.1. Case 1: $\ O < \alpha < I$

Suppose that data are drawn by sampling randomly from the distribution with density $f ( x - \theta _ { 0 } )$ /, where $f$ is supported on $( 0 , \infty )$ and satisfies

$$
f (x) \sim \beta x ^ {\alpha - 1} \quad \text { as } x \downarrow 0,\tag{2.1}
$$

with $0 < \alpha < 1$ and $\beta > 0$ . Denote by $X _ { 1 } \leqslant . . . \leqslant X _ { n }$ the ordered values of the sample. In most models of this type, $f$ is monotone decreasing on the positive real line, and then, if $f$ is known but $\theta _ { 0 }$ is not, the maximum likelihood estimator $\hat { \theta }$ of $\theta _ { 0 }$ is identical to $X _ { 1 }$ , which is also the quantity that maximizes the ‘semiparametric likelihood’,

$$
L _ {0} (\theta) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \theta) ^ {\alpha - 1}.\tag{2.2}
$$

More generally, if $f = f ( \cdot | \omega )$ , where $\omega$ denotes a vector of parameters other than $\theta ,$ and if $0 < \alpha < 1$ , then the maximum profile likelihood estimator of $\theta$ is equal to $X _ { 1 } , \mathrm { i . e . }$ if we choose $\hat { \omega } _ { \boldsymbol { \theta } }$ to maximize

$$
L (\theta , \omega) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (X _ {i} - \theta | \omega)\tag{2.3}
$$

for given $\theta ,$ and then select $\theta = { \hat { \theta } }$ to maximize the conventional profile likelihood, $L _ { 1 } ( \theta ) =$ $L ( \theta , { \hat { \omega } } _ { \theta } )$ , then $\hat { \theta }$ will be identical to $X _ { 1 }$

An alternative approach, which is the basis for work in this paper, involves multiplying $L _ { 0 } ( \theta )$ or $L _ { 1 } ( \theta )$ by an empirical prior for $\theta ,$ specifically

$$
p (\theta) = \frac {X _ {1} - \theta}{X _ {2} - \theta}.
$$

This gives penalized forms of likelihood, i.e.

$$
L _ {2} (\theta) = p (\theta) \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \theta) ^ {\alpha - 1}\tag{2.4}
$$

in the semiparametric case and

$$
\begin{array}{l} L _ {3} (\theta) = p (\theta) L _ {1} (\theta) \\ \qquad = p (\theta) \left\{\prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (X _ {i} - \theta) ^ {\alpha - 1} \right\} \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} g (X _ {i} | \theta , \hat {\omega} _ {\theta}) \end{array}\tag{2.5}
$$

in the parametric setting, where

$$
g (x | \theta , \omega) = f (x | \theta , \omega) (x - \theta) ^ {1 - \alpha}.\tag{2.6}
$$

The factor $p ( \theta )$ shifts the estimator of θ from its value, under $L _ { 0 }$ or $L _ { 1 }$ , of $X _ { 1 }$ , which we know to be an overestimate, to a more plausible value that is strictly less than $X _ { 1 }$

As is well known, the limiting distribution of $n ^ { 1 / \alpha } ( X _ { 1 } - \theta _ { 0 } )$ is a type III extreme value distribution (Smith, 1985). However, if $\hat { \theta }$ maximizes $L _ { 2 } ( \theta )$ or $L _ { 3 } ( \theta )$ , and if condition (2.1) holds, the limiting distribution of $n ^ { 1 / \alpha } ( \hat { \theta } - \theta _ { 0 } )$ is relatively complex; it is the distribution of $Z _ { 0 }$ , which is defined in Section 2.3.1.

In the semiparametric case, where $L _ { 2 }$ is maximized, a sufficient condition for the above result is that condition (2.1) holds and $f$ has a ‘reflected J’-shape on $( 0 , \infty )$ . When $L _ { 3 }$ is maximized, the result that was stated in the previous paragraph holds true in a range of settings, including those of the three-parameter Weibull and gamma models. The limiting distribution, i.e. the distribution of $Z _ { 0 }$ , is the same in semiparametric and parametric cases.

The rate $n ^ { - 1 / \alpha }$ , at which both $X _ { 1 }$ and the penalized versions of $\hat { \theta }$ converge to $\theta ,$ cannot be bettered, even in parametric cases. In fact, this rate can be shown to be optimal in a variety of senses. See, for example, Hall (1982) and Smith (1987).

In a parametric setting the first-order properties of the estimator $\hat { \omega } _ { \hat { \theta } }$ of ω are unaffected by the presence of the penalty $p ( \theta )$ in equation (2.5). In particular, the asymptotic variance of $\hat { \omega } _ { \hat { \theta } } .$ , which has a limiting normal distribution, is the same as in the conventional case where $\hat { \theta }$ is defined by maximizing $L _ { 1 } ( \theta )$ rather than $L _ { 3 } ( \theta )$ . This can be explained intuitively by the fact that any finite number of order statistics, including for example the two smallest, have an asymptotically negligible effect on first-order limiting properties of $\hat { \omega } _ { \hat { \theta } }$ . Indeed, that is to be expected from the fact that the limit is normal.

## 2.1.2. Case $2 \colon I \leqslant \alpha < 2$

In the range $1 \leqslant \alpha < 2$ , maximizing the semiparametric likelihood $L _ { 0 }$ , defined at equation (2.2), does not give an estimator that is first-order equivalent to the one that maximizes the profile likelihood $L _ { 1 }$ . Indeed, Woodroofe (1974) showed that, in many circumstances where $\omega$ is degenerate and $f$ is known and satisfies condition (2.1), the maximum likelihood estimator of $\theta ,$ , based on data that have the density $f ( x - \theta )$ , has a limiting distribution that is quite different from that of the smallest order statistic. Nevertheless the limiting dis tribution depends only on $\alpha$ and so can be given in general cases. The same limiting distribution obtains when the maximum likelihood estimator is computed from the profile likelihood $L _ { 1 } ( \theta )$ in a multiparameter model, such as the three-parameter Weibull and gamma models.

A related result holds in the context of the penalized profile likelihood $L _ { 3 }$ , given at equation (2.5). If $\hat { \theta }$ maximizes $L _ { 3 } ( \theta )$ then, for the above-mentioned multiparameter models, $n ^ { 1 / \alpha } ( \hat { \theta } - \theta _ { 0 } )$ converges in distribution to $Z _ { 0 }$ , which is defined in Section 2.3.2. This result is also true if ω is degenerate and we compute $\hat { \theta }$ by maximizing $p ( \theta ) \Pi _ { i } f ( X _ { i } - \theta )$ . In this case the regularity conditions on $f ,$ which were given by Woodroofe (1974), should be assumed. They include cases like the one-parameter Weibull and gamma models.

Just as in the case $\alpha < 1$ , the first-order properties of $\hat { \omega } _ { \hat { \theta } }$ are not affected by the presence of the penalty $p ( \theta )$ in equation (2.5), for the same reasons as before.

## 2.1.3. Case 3: α -2

In the setting $\alpha \geqslant 2$ the factor $p ( \theta )$ has no asymptotic effect on first-order properties of ${ \hat { \theta } } ;$ the limiting distributions of the values of $\theta$ that maximize $L _ { 1 } ( \theta )$ and $L _ { 3 } ( \theta )$ are identical. This comes about because the effects of any finite number of extreme order statistics on the overall convergence rate are negligibly small. To put it another way, when $\alpha \geqslant 2$ the factor $p ( \theta )$ can be retained in equation (2.5), or dropped, without altering first-order asymptotic properties of the maximum likelihood estimator. Again, this can be anticipated from the fact that, when $\alpha \geqslant 2$ , limiting distributions of all estimators are normal. See also Woodroofe (1972).

## 2.2. Interpretation ofthe empirical prior

The empirical prior $p ( \theta )$ has a general form and holds the information about θ that is contained in the first two sample spacings. Intuitively we may see the following.

(a) No estimates of θ can equal $X _ { 1 }$ , since $p ( X _ { 1 } ) = 0$

(b) For θ distant from $X _ { 1 } , p ( \theta ) \approx 1$ , indicating that those values are equally likely. Hence the prior is non-informative.

(c) When $X _ { 1 } < < X _ { 2 } , p ( \theta )$ rises slowly to 1 from 0 as θ decreases, indicating that θ is not near $X _ { 1 }$

(d) When $X _ { 1 } \approx X _ { 2 } , p ( \theta )$ has a sharp rise to 1 as θ decreases, indicating that θ is close to $X _ { 1 }$

The prior also has indirect implications for estimating α, as follows. Imagine that we want to estimate α solely from the first two spacings, without knowing the form of $f .$ . Suppose that n is large. Then the estimator of $\alpha$ is

$$
\hat {\alpha} ^ {- 1} = - \frac {1}{2} \log \left(\frac {X _ {1} - \theta}{X _ {2} - \theta}\right).
$$

It is clear that, as $\theta \to - \infty , p ( \theta ) \to 1$ and $\hat { \alpha } \to \infty$ , and, as $\theta  X _ { 1 } , p ( \theta )  0$ and $\hat { \alpha } \to 0$

The limit laws that we seek are identical in the semiparametric and parametric settings, where $\hat { \theta }$ is defined by maximizing $L _ { 2 } ( \theta )$ or $L _ { 3 } ( \theta )$ respectively. ## 2.3.3. Discussion

The results in Sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.2 show that, in the cases that are addressed there, the convergence rates of estimators of $\theta$ are $O _ { p } ( n ^ { - 1 / \alpha } )$ . Since the distribution of the limiting random variable $Z _ { 0 }$ is not degenerate then the convergence rate is exactly $n ^ { - 1 / \alpha }$ , and in fact this rate is optimal; see Section 2.1.1.

Moreover, the fact that the limiting distribution is non-normal implies that the large sample properties of θ<sup>ˆ</sup> depend intrinsically, even to first order, on the values that are taken by individual order statistics. In particular, in both cases the definitions of $Z _ { 0 }$ depend on each $Z _ { i }$ for $i \geqslant 1$ Hence, there is no law of large numbers effect, and no limiting normal distribution.

From a practical viewpoint this has at least two consequences. First, it means that it is inappropriate to use a conventional normal approximation to the distribution of ${ \hat { \theta } } .$ In particular, confidence intervals that were constructed in that way would be inaccurate, even in the large sample limit.

The second implication, which is more subtle than the first, is that conventional nonparametric bootstrap methods for estimating the distribution of $\hat { \theta }$ will not be consistent. This follows from the form of the distribution of $Z _ { \mathrm { 0 } } , \mathrm { e . g }$ . from its intimate dependence on each value of $Z _ { 1 } , Z _ { 2 } , \ldots$ . In particular, the relationship between the $Z _ { i } \mathbf { s }$ is not preserved by the nonparametric bootstrap.

For instance, if i does not equal j then the probability that $Y _ { i } = Y _ { j }$ equals $0 ,$ and likewise the probability that $Z _ { i } = Z _ { j }$ is 0; but the probability that the nonparametric bootstrap versions of $Y _ { i }$ and $Y _ { j }$ are equal to one another converges to $1 - \exp ( - 1 )$ as n increases. This means that in the bootstrap world there are, with non-negligible probability, ties among extreme order statistics. These ties prevent the bootstrap distribution of $\hat { \theta }$ from being an accurate approximation to the true distribution of ${ \hat { \theta } } ,$ even in the asymptotic limit.

This difficulty can be overcome in either of two ways. First, we can use the parametric bootstrap, rather than its nonparametric form. In many instances this is an attractive option, since models such as multiparameter Weibull and gamma distributions are often appropriate. Section 3 will discuss the performance of the parametric bootstrap in the present setting.

Secondly, the m out ofn bootstrap may be employed. This can be shown to result in consistent estimation of the asymptotic distribution of ${ \hat { \theta } } ,$ provided that $m = m ( n )$ is chosen so that $m \to \infty$ and $m / n \to \infty \operatorname { a s } n \to \infty$ . However, the difficulty with the m out of n bootstrap is, as always, the need to choose $m ,$ , for which no algorithm is available in the present setting. The attractiveness of parametric models, and the practical difficulties that are experienced with the $m$ out of n bootstrap in other contexts, makes it difficult to motivate paying significant attention to that method here. See Bickel et al. (1997), and the references therein, for discussion of the m out of n bootstrap.

## 3. Performance of Bayesian likelihood method

In this section we examine the performance of the method proposed and of the parametric bootstrap. We begin by defining bootstrap methods in the context of this problem.

The parametric bootstrap in this setting is conventional. It operates by, first, replacing unknown parameters (including α and θ) by their estimators, and then simulating from the model. This Monte Carlo simulation step can be used to construct variance estimates, and confidence bounds, as though the estimated parameters were the true parameters. Those estimates and bounds can be reported along with the values of the parameter estimates to which they refer. If greater accuracy is required then a double-bootstrap step can be included.

$$

and

f _ {\mathrm{G}} (\alpha , \beta , \theta) = \Gamma (\alpha) ^ {- 1} \beta^ {- \alpha} (x - \theta) ^ {\alpha - 1} \exp \{- (x - \theta) / \beta \}

respectively. In the Monte Carlo study 500 random samples each of sizes $n = 2 5$ and $n = 1 0 0$ were drawn from each of the distributions. The parameters were set as $\theta = 0$ and $\beta = 1$ , with various values of α. The proposed Bayesian likelihood method (method BL) is compared with the method of product of spacings (method PS) (Cheng and Amin, 1983) and the method of corrected likelihood (method CL) (Cheng and Iles, 1987). In using method CL we must choose a value for h that is of order at least $n ^ { - \bar { 1 } / \alpha }$ . In our simulation study, $h = X _ { 2 } - X _ { 1 }$ , which was suggested by Cheng and Iles (1987) for general use.

Simulation results of non-bootstrap properties are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. It can be seen from those results that method BL has in general lower mean-squared error across a broad range. Method CL, which was proposed more recently than method PS, has higher absolute bias than method PS in 20 out of24 parameter settings considered. The absolute bias ofmethod BL is less than that of method PS in 13 out of 24 cases.

Next we examine the performance of the bootstrap for estimating the standard error of $\mathbf { \hat { \theta } }$ for the Weibull model. In this simulation study, the sample size n is 25 and the number of bootstrap simulations is 500. The number of repetitions of bootstrap estimates is 500. Let R be the value of the bootstrap estimate divided by the true value of the standard error. Monte Carlo approximations to the pair (expected value of R, standard deviation of R) were (1.24, 0.724), (1.09, 0.569), (1.03, 0.43) and (0.955, 0.287) in the cases $\alpha { = } 0 . 5 , 1 . 0 , 1 . 5 , 2 . 0$ respectively. Fig. 1 shows box plots of the values of R. It can be seen that performance of the bootstrap estimator of the standard error improves with increasing α, and that the main difficulty experienced by the bootstrap is with stochastic error, not bias.

## 4. Conclusions

We have introduced a sample spacing-based empirical prior for the end point θ of a distribution. The prior is informative in a region that is close to the true parameter value, but non-informative elsewhere. The prior leads naturally to a penalized likelihood which always attains a global maximum.

The resulting estimator ofθ enjoys optimal convergence rates. It overcomes the unsatisfactory property, which standard likelihood-based methods have in non-regular cases, of producing an end point estimator that equals the data value that is nearest to the end point. Unlike some alternative remedies, our method does not require a choice of smoothing parameters. The ready interpretability of our estimator, in terms of the Bayesian paradigm applied to frequentist methodology, as well as the estimator’s numerical performance, makes it attractive and competitive in practice.

The approach that is suggested in this paper can be adapted to other settings. For example, when explanatory variables $V _ { i \cdot }$ , say, are available in addition to the ‘responses’ $X _ { i }$ , the density $f$ at expression (1.1) might depend on $V _ { i }$ . (For problems of this general type, see, for example, Donald and Paarsch (1993), Hirano and Porter (2003), Jofre-Bonet and Pesendorfer (2003) and Chernozhukov and Hong (2004).) Indicate the dependence through the notation $f _ { v _ { i } }$ . If it is assumed that, when $V _ { i }$ is close to $V _ { j }$ , the functions $f _ { v _ { i } }$ and $f _ { v _ { j } }$ are also close, and in particular that the end points $\theta = \theta _ { i }$ and $\theta _ { j }$ are close, then a regression model for $\theta _ { i }$ may be appropriate: $\theta _ { i } = a + b V _ { i }$ , say, for constants a and b. If the $V _ { i } \mathbf { s }$ are reasonably dense in an interval then statistical smoothing methods, combined with the techniques that were discussed in Section 2, can be used to estimate the densities $f _ { v }$ and, in particular, to estimate the end points $\scriptstyle { \theta = a + b V }$

Since the contributions of $U _ { 1 }$ and $U _ { 2 }$ are multiplied by $\alpha - 1$ , which vanishes when $\alpha = 1$ , then the contributions of $W _ { 1 }$ and $W _ { 2 }$ vanish also. Therefore, when $\alpha = 1$

## A.3. Derivation of limit of $\eta ^ { - 1 / \alpha } \partial L _ { 3 } / \partial \theta$

Let $T ^ { - 1 }$ have the distribution of a generic value of $T _ { i } ^ { - 1 } \equiv X _ { i } - \theta _ { 0 }$ . The distribution of T is in the domain of attraction of a stable law with exponent α and, in fact,

$$
P (T > x) \sim \alpha^ {- 1} \beta x ^ {- \alpha}
$$

as $x \to \infty$ , where $\beta$ is as at expression (2.1). Therefore, there are constants $a _ { n }$ such that the distribution of $U _ { 2 }$ converges to a stable law with exponent α. The case $\alpha = 1$ does not concern us, since the contribution of $U _ { 2 }$ to $\partial L _ { 3 } / \partial \theta$ vanishes when $\alpha = 1$ . When $1 < \alpha < 2 , E ( T ) < \infty$ and we may take $a _ { n } = n ~ E ( T )$

Note that $n ^ { 1 / \alpha } ( Y _ { 1 } , \ldots , Y _ { r } )$ denotes the vector of the r largest values of $T _ { 1 } , \ldots , T _ { n }$ . It is known that when $T$ is in the domain of attraction of a stable law, and the scaling constants $n ^ { - 1 / \alpha }$ are appropriate for the centred sum to converge, as they are in the case of $U _ { 2 } .$ , the distribution of the rescaled extremes, and of the centred and rescaled sum, have a joint limiting distribution. See, for example, Hall (1978b), Chow and Teugels (1979), Anderson and Turkman (1991), Samorodnitsky and Taqqu (1994) and Resnick (1986, 1987).

This enables us to prove the joint convergence in distribution of $( Y _ { 1 } , \dots , Y _ { r } , U _ { 2 } )$ to $( Z _ { 1 } , \ldots , Z _ { r } , W _ { 2 } )$ From that result, and the fact that $U _ { 1 } ( z )$ and $W _ { 1 } ( z )$ may be approximated arbitrarily closely, uniformly in z in compact intervals bounded below the respective extrema $Y _ { 1 }$ and $Z _ { 1 }$ , follows the weak convergence of $( Y _ { 1 } , \dots , { \bar { Y } } _ { r } , U _ { 1 } , U _ { 2 } )$ to $( Z _ { 1 } , \dots , Z _ { r } , W _ { 1 } , W _ { 2 } )$ , uniformly on such compact intervals. The fact that $U _ { 3 } \to 0$ in probability if $\alpha > 1$ , and $U _ { 3 } \to - 1$ in probability if $\alpha = 1$ , follows by using arguments from Woodroofe (1974).
