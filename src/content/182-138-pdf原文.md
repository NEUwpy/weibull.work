# Trimmed L-moments

Elsayed A.H. Elamir<sup>a;1</sup> , Allan H. Seheult<sup>b;</sup>∗

<sup>a</sup>Department of Social Statistics, University of Southampton, Southampton S017 1BJ, UK <sup>b</sup>Science Laboratories, Department of Mathematical Sciences, University of Durham, South Road, Durham DH1 3LE, UK

## Abstract

Classical estimation methods (least squares, the method of moments and maximum likelihood) work well in regular cases such as the exponential family, but outliers can have undue in1uence on these methods. We de3ne population trimmed L-moments (TL-moments) and corresponding sample TL-moments as robust generalisations of population and sample L-moments. TL-moments assign zero weight to extreme observations, they are easy to compute, their sample variances and covariances can be obtained in closed form, and they are more robust than L-moments are to the presence of outliers. Moreover, a population TL-moment may be well de3ned where the corresponding population L-moment does not exist: for example, the 3rst population TL-moment is well de3ned for a Cauchy distribution, but the 3rst population L-moment, the population mean, does not exist. The sample TL-mean is compared with other robust estimators of location. c 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: L-moments; Order statistics; Outliers; Robust estimation; Trimmed mean

## 1. Introduction

Outliers can have undue in1uence on standard estimation methods such as least squares, the method of moments and maximum likelihood; for example, each of these three methods estimates the mean of a normal population by the sample mean X<sup>;</sup> which is the unique minimum variance unbiased estimator but is not robust to outliers or departures from normality. Thus, if there is concern about extreme observations having undue in1uence, a robust method of estimation, developed to reduce in1uence of outliers on the 3nal estimates, should be used; see, for example, Barnett and Lewis (1994), Hawkins (1980), Hampel et al. (1986) and Rousseuw and Leroy (1987).

Sillito (1969) introduced population L-moments $\lambda _ { r }$ as alternatives to the classical population central moments $\mu _ { r } = E ( Y - \mu ) ^ { r }$ determined by the population distribution of the underlying random variable Y . A population L-moment $\lambda _ { r }$ is de3ned to be a certain linear function of the expectations of the order statistics $Y _ { 1 : r } , . . . , Y _ { r : r }$ in a “conceptual random sample of size $r ^ { \ast }$ from the underlying population. For example, $\lambda _ { 1 } = E ( Y _ { 1 : 1 } )$ , which is the same as the population mean $\mu = E ( Y )$ , is de3ned in terms of a conceptual sample of size $r = 1$ , while

$$
\lambda_ {2} = \frac {1}{2} [ E (Y _ {2: 2} - Y _ {1: 2}) ],
$$

an alternative to the population standard deviation, is de3ned in terms of a conceptual sample of size $r { = } 2$ . Similarly, the L-moments $\lambda _ { 3 }$ and $\lambda _ { 4 }$ are alternatives to the unscaled measures of skewness and kurtosis $\mu _ { 3 }$ and $\mu _ { 4 } ,$ , respectively.

Sample L-moments $l _ { r }$ are unbiased estimates of population L-moments $\lambda _ { r }$ based on the order statistics $X _ { 1 : n } , \ldots , X _ { n : n }$ of a random sample $X _ { 1 } , \ldots , X _ { n }$ of size n from the population. For example, $l _ { 1 }$ is the sample mean $\bar { X }$ and

$$
l _ {2} = \frac {1}{n (n - 1)} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (n - 2 i + 1) X _ {i: n} = \frac {1}{n (n - 1)} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {[ n / 2 ]} (n - 2 i + 1) \left(X _ {n - i + 1: n} - X _ {i: n}\right),
$$

where the second form shows clearly that $l _ { 2 }$ is a measure of scale.

Thus, we need to keep in mind the distinction between a conceptual random sample used to de3ne a population quantity and an actual random sample which may be used to estimate it. To emphasise the distinction in this account, we use Y for the conceptual sample and $X$ for the random sample. Such a distinction is not necessary for population central moments $\mu _ { r }$ as all of them are de3ned in terms of a conceptual sample of size $r = 1$

Hosking (1990) uni3ed discussion of analysis and estimation of distributions using L-moments, and used particular ratios of them as measures of skewness and kurtosis analogous to those based on population central moments and their sample counterparts. Royston (1992) and Vogel and Fennessey (1993) discussed the advantages of L-skewness and L-kurtosis over their classical counterparts.

L-moments have been applied in engineering, meteorology, quality control and hydrology; see, for example, Hosking et al. (1985), Gingras and Adamowski (1994), Guttman et al. (1993), Pearson (1993), Pilon and Adamowski (1992), Elamir and Seheult (2001a), and Sankarasubramanian and Srinivasan (1999).

Mudholkar and Hutson (1998) introduced LQ-moments as a robust version of Lmoments in which the expectation of the conceptual order statistics (in the de3nition of population L-moments) is replaced by a class of robust location measures de3ned in terms of simple linear combinations of symmetric quantiles of the distributions of the order statistics, including the median and trimean; and they show how to estimate them.

When seeking to identify outliers, a common practise is to “reject” observations which are more than some multiple of the sample standard deviation away from the sample mean. However, in the presence of outliers the sample standard deviation will be seriously in1ated and the procedure will lack power.

An alternative is to censor or trim a predetermined percentage of the extreme values from the sample before estimating the mean and the standard deviation from the un-trimmed sample values. For example, to symmetrically trim a univariate sample, we remove the k smallest and k largest values for some speci3ed $k < n / 2$ . For univariate samples the trimmed mean, the mean of the $n - 2 k$ un-trimmed sample values, is by far the most widely studied trimmed statistic. Such a procedure sacri3ces some information when there are no outliers but gains power when they do occur.

We introduce an alternative robust version of L-moments called “trimmed Lmoments” (TL-moments) in which the expectations of the order statistics of a conceptual sample (in the de3nition of population L-moments) are replaced by expectations of the order statistics of a larger conceptual sample, the size increase equal to the total amount of trimming. For example, $\mathbf { \dot { \lambda } } _ { 1 } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ , the 3rst population moment symmetrically trimmed by one conceptual sample value, is de3ned by

$$
\lambda_ {1} ^ {(1)} = E (Y _ {2: 3}),
$$

the expectation of the median $Y _ { 2 : 3 }$ of a conceptual sample $Y _ { 1 } , Y _ { 2 } , Y _ { 3 }$ of size three.

We de3ne sample TL-moments, show that they are unbiased estimators of the corresponding population quantities, obtain closed form expressions for their variances and covariances in terms of second-order moments of the order statistics of the associated conceptual samples, and demonstrate that they assign zero weight to extreme observations. Thus, for example,

$$
l _ {1} ^ {(1)} = \frac {1}{\binom {n} {3}} \sum_ {i = 2} ^ {n - 1} (i - 1) (n - i) X _ {i: n}
$$

is an unbiased estimator of $\lambda _ { 1 } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ . It is worth noting here that $E ( l _ { 1 } ^ { ( 1 ) } )$ is 3nite for a Cauchy distribution because $X _ { 1 : n }$ and $X _ { n : n }$ (which both receive zero weight in the de3nition of $l _ { 1 } ^ { ( 1 ) } )$ are the only order statistics that do not have 3nite expectation.

TL-moments have certain advantages over L-moments and central moments. A population TL-moment can exist when the corresponding population L-moment or central moment does not exist, as mentioned above for the Cauchy distribution. Sample TL-moments are more resistant to outliers. The method is not intended to replace existing robust methods but rather to complement them, especially in situations where we may have outliers.

In Section 2, we de3ne population TL-moments, TL-skewness and TL-kurtosis. In Section 3, we obtain unbiased estimators of population TL-moments using certain linear combinations of sample order statistics, called sample TL-moments. In Section 4, we establish exact results for the variances and covariances of sample TL-moments for any univariate continuous distribution. We give two illustrative examples in Section 5.

## 2. Population TL-moments

Let $Y _ { 1 } , \ldots , Y _ { r }$ be a conceptual random sample of size r from a continuous distribution with quantile function $Q _ { Y } ( u ) = F _ { Y } ^ { - 1 } ( u )$ and let $Y _ { 1 : r } \leqslant Y _ { 2 : r } \leqslant \cdot \cdot \cdot \leqslant Y _ { r : r }$ denote the corresponding order statistics.

Hosking (1990) de3ned the rth L-moment $\lambda _ { r }$ as

$$
\lambda_ {r} = \frac {1}{r} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {r - 1} (- 1) ^ {k} \binom{r - 1}{k} E (Y _ {r - k: r}), \quad r = 1, 2, \dots .\tag{1}
$$

While L-moments enjoy certain robustness properties, Mudholkar and Hutson (1998) suggested a robust modi3cation in which the mean of the distribution of $Y _ { r - k : r }$ in (1) is replaced by its median or some other population location measure. In particular, they de3ned the rth LQ-moment $\zeta _ { r }$ as

$$
\zeta_ {r} = \frac {1}{r} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {r - 1} (- 1) ^ {k} \binom{r - 1}{k} \tau_ {p, \alpha} (Y _ {r - k: r}), \quad r = 1, 2, \ldots ,\tag{2}
$$

where

$$
\tau_ {p, \alpha} (Y _ {r - k: r}) = p Q _ {Y _ {r - k: r}} (\alpha) + (1 - 2 p) Q _ {Y _ {r - k: r}} (\frac {1}{2}) + p Q _ {Y _ {r - k: r}} (1 - \alpha)\tag{3}
$$

and $\textstyle 0 \leqslant \alpha \leqslant { \frac { 1 } { 7 } } , 0 \leqslant p \leqslant { \frac { 1 } { 7 } }$ . Examples of $\tau _ { p , \boldsymbol { \alpha } }$ are the median $( p = 0 , \alpha = 1 )$ , the trimean $\begin{array} { r } { ( p = \frac { 1 } { 4 } , \alpha = \frac { 1 } { 4 } ) } \end{array}$ and Gastwirth $\begin{array} { r } { ( p = 0 . 3 , \alpha = \frac { 1 } { 3 } ) } \end{array}$ .

In this paper we propose a somewhat simpler alternative robust modi3cation of (1) in which $E ( Y _ { r - k : r } )$ is replaced by $E ( Y _ { r + t _ { 1 } - k : r + t _ { 1 } + t _ { 2 } } )$ . Thus, for each $r ,$ we increase the conceptual sample size from r to $r + t _ { 1 } + t _ { 2 }$ and work only with the expectations of the r order statistics $Y _ { t _ { 1 } + 1 : r + t _ { 1 } + t _ { 2 } } , \ldots , Y _ { t _ { 1 } + r : r + t _ { 1 } + t _ { 2 } }$ by trimming the $t _ { 1 }$ smallest and the $t _ { 2 }$ largest from the conceptual sample. We call such a modi3cation the rth trimmed L-moment (TL-moment) and denote it by $\lambda _ { r } ^ { ( t _ { 1 } , t _ { 2 } ) }$ . Thus, we de3ne the rth TL-moment $\lambda _ { r } ^ { ( t _ { 1 } , t _ { 2 } ) }$ as follows

$$
\lambda_ {r} ^ {(t _ {1}, t _ {2})} = \frac {1}{r} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {r - 1} (- 1) ^ {k} \binom{r - 1}{k} E (Y _ {r + t _ {1} - k: r + t _ {1} + t _ {2}}), \quad r = 1, 2, \ldots .\tag{4}
$$

Clearly, TL-moments reduce to L-moments when $t _ { 1 } = t _ { 2 } = 0$ . While there will be applications when unequal amounts of trimming $( t _ { 1 } ~ \neq ~ t _ { 2 } )$ may be appropriate, we focus here on the symmetric case $t _ { 1 } = t _ { 2 } = t$ and write (4) as

$$
\lambda_ {r} ^ {(t)} = \frac {1}{r} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {r - 1} (- 1) ^ {k} \binom{r - 1}{k} E (Y _ {r + t - k: r + 2 t}), \quad r = 1, 2, \ldots .\tag{5}
$$

Thus, for example, $\lambda _ { 1 } ^ { ( t ) } { = } E ( Y _ { 1 + t : 1 + 2 t } )$ is the expectation of the median from a conceptual sample of size $1 + 2 t$ . Note that $\lambda _ { 1 } ^ { ( t ) }$ will be zero for distributions which are symmetric about zero.

When t = 1 the 3rst four TL-moments are

$$
\begin{array}{l} \lambda_ {1} ^ {(1)} = E (Y _ {2: 3}), \\ \lambda_ {2} ^ {(1)} = \frac {1}{2} E (Y _ {3: 4} - Y _ {2: 4}), \\ \lambda_ {3} ^ {(1)} = \frac {1}{3} E (Y _ {4: 5} - 2 Y _ {3: 5} + Y _ {2: 5}), \\ \lambda_ {4} ^ {(1)} = \frac {1}{4} E (Y _ {5: 6} - 3 Y _ {4: 6} + 3 Y _ {3: 6} - Y _ {2: 6}). \end{array}
$$

Notice that $\lambda _ { 1 } ^ { ( 1 ) } , \lambda _ { 2 } ^ { ( 1 ) } , \lambda _ { 3 } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ and $\lambda _ { 4 } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ are population measures of location, scale, skewness and kurtosis analogous to the corresponding ordinary L-moments

$$
\begin{array}{l} \lambda_ {1} = E (Y _ {1: 1}), \\ \lambda_ {2} = \frac {1}{2} E (Y _ {2: 2} - Y _ {1: 2}), \\ \lambda_ {3} = \frac {1}{3} E (Y _ {3: 3} - 2 Y _ {2: 3} + Y _ {1: 3}), \\ \lambda_ {4} = \frac {1}{4} E (Y _ {4: 4} - 3 Y _ {3: 4} + 3 Y _ {2: 4} - Y _ {1: 4}). \end{array}
$$

As the expectation of $Y _ { i : r }$ can be written as

$$
E (Y _ {i: r}) = \frac {r !}{(i - 1) ! (r - i) !} \int_ {0} ^ {1} Q (u) u ^ {i - 1} (1 - u) ^ {r - i} d u,\tag{6}
$$

we may re-express the right-hand side of (5) as

$$
\frac {1}{r} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {r - 1} (- 1) ^ {k} \binom {r - 1} {k} \frac {(r + 2 t) !}{(r + t - k - 1) ! (t + k) !} \int_ {0} ^ {1} Q (u) u ^ {r + t - k - 1} (1 - u) ^ {t + k} d u\tag{7}
$$

for $r = 1 , 2 , \ldots$ . Notice that $\lambda _ { r } ^ { ( 0 ) } = \lambda _ { r }$ , the ordinary rth L-moment, corresponding to no trimming.

The alternative expressions for the 3rst four TL-moments when t = 1 are

$$
\lambda_ {1} ^ {(1)} = 6 \int_ {0} ^ {1} Q (u) u (1 - u) d u,
$$

$$
\lambda_ {2} ^ {(1)} = 6 \int_ {0} ^ {1} Q (u) u (1 - u) (2 u - 1) \mathrm{d} u,
$$

$$
\lambda_ {3} ^ {(1)} = \frac {2 0}{3} \int_ {0} ^ {1} Q (u) u (1 - u) (5 u ^ {2} - 5 u + 1) d u,
$$

$$
\lambda_ {4} ^ {(1)} = \frac {1 5}{2} \int_ {0} ^ {1} Q (u) u (1 - u) \left(1 4 u ^ {3} - 2 1 u ^ {2} + 9 u - 1\right) \mathrm{d} u.
$$

Observe that a distribution may be speci3ed by its trimmed L-moments even if some of its L-moments and conventional moments do not exist; for example, $\lambda _ { 1 } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ (the expectation of the median of a conceptual sample of size 3) exists for the Cauchy distribution, but $\lambda _ { 1 }$ , the population mean, does not exist.

## 2.1. TL-skewness and TL-kurtosis

The coeQcient of skewness $\beta _ { 1 } = \mu _ { 3 } / \mu _ { 2 } ^ { 3 / 2 }$ and the coeQcient of kurtosis $\beta _ { 2 } = \mu _ { 4 } / \mu _ { 2 } ^ { 2 }$ where $\mu _ { 2 } , \mu _ { 3 }$ and $\mu _ { 4 }$ are central moments, have played an important role in the classi3- cation of statistical distributions, in model 3tting and in parameter estimation. Because of some de3ciencies of $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 }$ , such as their sensitivity to the behaviour of extreme tails of a distribution and their non-existence for some distributions, such as the Cauchy distribution, alternative measures of skewness and kurtosis have been proposed: for an overview, see Groeneveld (1998) and Kendall and Stuart (1987).

Hosking (1990) has de3ned the ratios $\tau _ { 3 }$ and $\tau _ { 4 } .$ , called L-skewness and L-kurtosis, as

$$
\tau_ {3} = \lambda_ {3} / \lambda_ {2} \quad \text { and } \quad \tau_ {4} = \lambda_ {4} / \lambda_ {2}\tag{8}
$$

and Mudholkar and Hutson (1998) have de3ned the ratios $\eta _ { 3 }$ and $\eta _ { 4 }$ called LQskewness and LQ-kurtosis as

$$
\eta_ {3} = \zeta_ {3} / \zeta_ {2} \quad \text { and } \quad \eta_ {4} = \zeta_ {4} / \zeta_ {2}.\tag{9}
$$

We de3ne population TL-skewness $\tau _ { 3 } ^ { ( t ) }$ and TL-kurtosis $\tau _ { 4 } ^ { ( t ) }$ analogously as

$$
\tau_ {3} ^ {(t)} = \lambda_ {3} ^ {(t)} / \lambda_ {2} ^ {(t)} \quad \text { and } \quad \tau_ {4} ^ {(t)} = \lambda_ {4} ^ {(t)} / \lambda_ {2} ^ {(t)}.\tag{10}
$$

It should be noted that TL-skewness and TL-kurtosis are location and scale invariant, and exist for the Cauchy distribution. Their estimation will be considered in Section 3.

## 3. Sample TL-moments

We now consider estimators of population TL-moments which are certain linear combinations of the order statistics $X _ { 1 : n } \leqslant X _ { 2 : n } \leqslant \cdot \cdot \cdot \leqslant X _ { n : n }$ of a random sample $X _ { 1 } , X _ { 2 } , \ldots ,$ $X _ { n }$ of size n from the population. These estimators are based on those introduced by Downton (1966), and included a particularly simple estimator of the standard deviation.

Downton’s estimators, which are highly eQcient for normal populations, are based on the linear combinations

$$
U _ {k l: n} = k! l! \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \binom{i - 1}{k} \binom{n - i}{l} X _ {i: n}.\tag{11}
$$

Moreover, he established the elegant result that

$$
E (U _ {k l: n}) = k! l! \binom{n}{k + l + 1} E (Y _ {k + 1: k + l + 1}),\tag{12}
$$

so that

$$
\hat {E} (Y _ {k + 1: k + l + 1}) = \frac {1}{\binom{n}{k + l + 1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \binom{i - 1}{k} \binom{n - i}{l} X _ {i: n}\tag{13}
$$

may be regarded as an unbiased estimator of the expectation of the $( k + 1 )$ )th order statistic $Y _ { k + 1 : k + l + 1 }$ in a conceptual sample of size $k + l + 1$

Now suppose that in the de3nition of the rth population TL-moment $\lambda _ { r } ^ { ( t ) }$ in (5) we replace $E ( Y _ { r + t - k : r + 2 t } )$ by its unbiased estimator

$$
\hat {E} (Y _ {r + t - k: r + 2 t}) = \frac {1}{\binom {n} {r + 2 t}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \binom {i - 1} {r + t - k - 1} \binom {n - i} {t + k} X _ {i: n}\tag{14}
$$

obtained by the assignments $k  r + t - k - 1$ and $l  t + k$ in (13). We now de3ne the rth sample TL-moment $l _ { r } ^ { ( t ) }$ as

$$

$$

which shows that $l _ { r } ^ { ( t ) }$ is a linear combination of $X _ { 1 : n } , \ldots , X _ { n : n }$ that is an unbiased estimator of $\lambda _ { r } ^ { ( t ) }$ . Note that since for each $k = 0 , . . . , r - 1$ the coeQcients of $X _ { i : n }$ in (14) are zero unless $r + t - k \leqslant i \leqslant n - t - k .$ , it follows that the coeQcients of $X _ { i : n }$ in $l _ { r } ^ { ( t ) }$ are zero outside $r + t \leqslant i \leqslant n - t .$ Thus, $l _ { r } ^ { ( t ) }$ is a symmetrically weighted linear combination of the $n - 2 t$ trimmed sample values $X _ { t + 1 : n } , \dotsc , X _ { n - t : n }$ . ## 4. Variance structure of TL-moments

Sample TL-moments are linear combinations of order statistics but their particular form allows us to use the methods of Downton (1966) and Elamir and Seheult (2001b) to obtain their exact variances and covariances in terms of expectations, variances and covariances of order statistics from samples of 3xed size which do not depend on the actual sample size n. Straightforward modi3cations of the method developed by Elamir and Seheult (2001b) for 3nding exact variances and covariances of sample L-moments show that

$$
\begin{array}{l} E (l _ {r} ^ {(t)} l _ {s} ^ {(t)}) = \frac {1}{r s} \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {r - 1} \sum_ {l = 0} ^ {s - 1} (- 1) ^ {r} (- 1) ^ {s} \binom{r - 1}{k} \binom{s - 1}{l} \\ \qquad \qquad \times \Omega (r + t - k - 1, k + t, s + t - l - 1, l + t), \end{array}\tag{20}
$$

where

$$

## 4.1. Distribution-free unbiased estimators of variances and covariances of sample TL-moments

Expressions for variances and covariances of sample TL-moments such as (21) are only useful when we know the exact form of the population distribution of Y . However, when we do not know the population distribution we will still want to provide (estimated) standard errors for the $l _ { r } ^ { ( t ) }$ or functions of them, such as sample TL-skewness and TL-kurtosis. Fortunately, we can use (20) in combination with the following relationships due to Downton (1966) to derive distribution-free unbiased estimators of the variances and covariances of sample TL-moments.

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (i - 1) ^ {(k)} (n - i) ^ {(l)} E (X _ {i: n}) = k!   l! \binom{n}{k + l + 1} E (Y _ {k + 1: k + l + 1}),
$$

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (i - 1) ^ {(k)} (n - i) ^ {(l)} E (X _ {i: n} ^ {2}) = k! l! \binom{n}{k + l + 1} E (Y _ {k + 1: k + l + 1} ^ {2}),
$$

$$
\sum_ {i <   j} (i - 1) ^ {(k)} (n - j) ^ {(l)} E \left(X _ {i: n} X _ {j: n}\right)
$$

$$
= k! l! \binom{n}{k + l + 2} E (Y _ {k + 1: k + l + 2} Y _ {k + 2: k + l + 2}).\tag{22}
$$

The relationships in (22) show that the following expressions are unbiased estimators of $E ( Y _ { k + 1 : k + l + 1 } ) , E ( Y _ { k + 1 : k + l + 1 } ^ { 2 } )$ and $E ( Y _ { k + 1 : k + l + 2 } Y _ { k + 2 : k + l + 2 } )$ , respectively:

$$
A (k, l) = \frac {1}{\binom{n}{k + l + 1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \binom{i - 1}{k} \binom{n - i}{l} X _ {i: n},
$$

$$
S (k, l) = \frac {1}{\binom {n} {k + l + 1}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \binom {i - 1} {k} \binom {n - i} {l} X _ {i: n} ^ {2},
$$

$$
C (k, l) = \frac {1}{\binom {n} {k + l + 2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \sum_ {j = i + 1} ^ {n} \binom {i - 1} {k} \binom {n - i} {l} X _ {i: n} X _ {j: n}.\tag{23}
$$

For example, a distribution-free unbiased estimator of $\mathrm { v a r } ( l _ { 1 } ^ { ( 1 ) } )$ in (21) is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \widehat {\mathrm{var}} (l _ {1} ^ {(1)}) = 3 \big \{1 2 (n - 2) ^ {2} [ C (2, 1) + C (1, 2) ] - 1 0 (n - 2) (2 n - 3) C (1, 1) \\ \qquad - 2 (3 n ^ {2} - 1 5 n + 2 0) C (2, 2) + 3 0 S (1, 1) \\ \qquad + 5 (n - 3) [ S (2, 1) + S (1, 2) ] \\ \qquad + 2 (n - 3) (n - 4) S (2, 2) \big \} / 5 n (n - 1) (n - 2). \end{array}
$$

## 6. Summary

We have introduced a new class of population moment measures, such as location, scale, skewness and kurtosis, termed trimmed L-moments (TL-moments) that are robust extensions of population L-moments. L-moments themselves were introduced as robust alternatives to classical population moments, such as the mean and variance. However, L-moments and their estimates lack certain robustness properties; for example, the 3rst L-moment coincides with the mean.

Sample TL-moments (such as the TL-mean, TL-scale and TL-skewness and TLkurtosis) are linear combinations of order statistics that assign zero weight to a predetermined number of extreme sample values, similar to trimmed and Winsorised means. Sample TL-moments are unbiased estimators of their corresponding population TL-moments, so it is clear what they are estimating. We have obtained their variances and covariances in closed form. We have also investigated the properties of TL-moments for some common symmetric distributions and have compared the sample TL-mean with other robust estimators of location. Their performance compares favourably with trimmed and Winsorised location estimators which are also averages of trimmed sample values but the weights attached to the un-trimmed values are not arbitrary.

Several theoretical and practical aspects of the TL-moments are under study or remain for future investigations.

In the de3nition of TL-moments, can t be fractional? The de3nition in terms of integrals of the quantile function in (7) suggests that this is possible.

A related issue is the choice of t, the amount of trimming. For a particular distribution we might investigate, for example, the variance of the TL-mean as a function of t, bearing in mind that the population quantity being estimated (the corresponding population TL-mean) will be diUerent for diUerent values of t for non-symmetric distributions.

It would be of interest to study and compare the alternative procedures (alluded to towards the end of Section 3) where ordinary sample L-moments might applied the n − 2t trimmed sample values.

Finally, as suggested by a referee, it would be interesting to investigate the possible use of the exact bootstrap variances of L-estimators given Section 3 of Hutson and Ernst (2000) to express the variance of TL-moments in matrix form.
