## Abstract

Three-parameter Weibull is one of the most popular and most widely-used distribution in many fields of science. Therefore, many studies have been conducted concerning the statistical inferences of the parameters of Weibull distribution. In general, the maximum likelihood (ML) methodology is used in the estimation process of unknown parameters. In this study, the ML estimation of the parameters of Weibull distribution is considered using particle swarm optimization (PSO). As in other heuristic optimization methods, the performance of PSO is affected by initial conditions. The novelty of this study comes from the fact that we propose a new adaptive search space based on confidence intervals in PSO. The modified maximum likelihood (MML) estimators are utilized for constructing the confidence intervals. MML based confidence intervals allow a narrower search space for the parameters of Weibull distribution than the search space used in the literature. Therefore, the performance of PSO increases, since the search space is wisely narrowed. In order to show the performance of the proposed approach, an extensive Monte-Carlo simulation study is conducted. Simulation results show that the proposed approach works well. In addition, real world data is analyzed to show implementation of the proposed method.

Keywords: Particle swarm optimization, search space, Weibull distribution, maximum likelihood, Monte-Carlo simulation, strengths of glass fibre.

## 1. Introduction

Three-parameter Weibull distribution has various applications in many areas of science, such as engineering, renewable energy, air quality determination, reliability and biomedical sciences, and so forth. [1-5]. Therefore, it has been widely-used by many researchers and scientists from different disciplines. Weibull distribution and its modifications are also of great interest in the context of reliability, see i.e. [6-9]. In the remainder of the paper, we use Weibull distribution instead of three-parameter Weibull distribution for the sake of brevity. It has the following probability density function (pdf)

$$
f (x; \mu , \sigma , p) = \frac {p}{\sigma^ {p}} (x - \mu) ^ {p - 1} \exp \left\{- \left(\frac {x - \mu}{\sigma}\right) ^ {p} \right\}, x > \mu , \sigma > 0, p > 0\tag{1}
$$

and the cumulative density function (cdf)

$$
F (x; \mu , \sigma , p) = 1 - \exp \left\{- \left(\frac {x - \mu}{\sigma}\right) ^ {p} \right\}\tag{2}
$$

where $\mu , \sigma$ and represent the location, the scale and the shape parameters, respectively. Random variable having pdf (1) is denoted in short by $X { \sim } W ( \mu , \sigma , p )$ . The parameter allows the distribution to take different shapes. For example, Weibull distribution is reversed as J-shaped when $p \leq 1$ . If $p > 1$ then it is bell-shaped [10]. It reduces to the well-known exponential distribution when $p = 1$ . Rayleigh distribution is also a special case of Weibull distribution for . Therefore, it provides flexibility for modelling positively and negatively skewed data sets. It should be mentioned that Weibull distribution is not appropriate in modelling data sets having a bathtub hazard rate since its hazard rate is monotonic.

There are many methods for estimating the unknown parameters of Weibull distribution, such as maximum likelihood (ML), method of moments (MM), modified maximum likelihood (MML), least squares (LS), percentile and so forth; see for example, Kantar and Senoglu [5] and the references therein in this context. We also refer to Teimouri et al. [11], and Akram and Hayat [12], in which a comparision of the estimators for Weibull distribution is presented. Among the estimation methods, ML is the most popular and the most widely-used because of its well-known asymptotic properties, such as a lack of bias, consistency, efficiency, normality, and suchlike. These properties are satisfied when regularity conditions are held; see Cheng and Amin [13]. It should be noted that the ML method does not provide explicit estimators of the parameters in certain cases. Therefore, we resort to numerical methods. However, numerical methods may have the following problems; (i) non-convergence of iterations, (ii) slow convergency, and (iii) convergence to the wrong root [14-18].

In this study, particle swarm optimization (PSO), which is a population based heuristic optimization method, is utilized to find the ML estimates of the parameters of Weibull distribution. There is little literature concerning the application of PSO in the estimation of Weibull parameters as far as we know. A number of studies can be given as follows. Wen et al. [19] propose the use of the correlation coefficient optimization method based on the PSO algorithm to find the estimates of the location, scale and shape parameters of Weibull distribution. Wang and Huang [20] consider ML estimation via PSO to estimate the parameters of mixtures of two Weibull distributions for both complete and multiple censored data. Orkcu et al. [21] provide an extensive Monte-Carlo simulation study on ML estimation of the location, scale and shape parameters of Weibull distribution using a PSO algorithm. A PSO algorithm is used for modelling wind resources in the Brazilian Northeast Region via Weibull distribution by Carnerio [22]. The advantage of using PSO is that it examines different points in different regions in a search space, so that it can avoid local optimum traps and can quickly converge to the global optimal solution [23-25]. Therefore, the problems mentioned above may be alleviated using PSO. The performance of the PSO method is affected by initial conditions as in other heuristic optimization methods. To be more precise, defining a good search space, determining the inertia weight, accelerating coefficients and swarm size significantly affect the performance of the PSO.

The originality of this paper comes from the fact that we strategically construct the search space for the parameters of Weibull distribution instead of determining arbitrarily as in the related literature. In the proposed approach, we construct the search space using confidence intervals based on Tiku’s [26,27] MML estimators for each unknown parameter in Weibull distribution. It should be noted that the confidence intervals are functions of the sample observations, so the proposed approach can be called an adaptive approach. Using the proposed adaptive approach provides us with a narrower search space. Therefore, the performance of PSO is improved compared to the traditional PSO. In order to show the high performance of the proposed approach, an extensive Monte-Carlo simulation study is conducted. In addition, the proposed method is applied to real life data in order to show the applicability of the method.

Later in the paper, we assume that the shape parameter of Weibull distribution is greater than two. It should be noted that maximum likelihood (ML) estimators are not minimum variance bound (MVB) estimators when the shape parameter $p \leq 2$ . The reason for this is that the regularity conditions are satisfied while $p > 2$

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In section 2, ML estimation of the parameters of Weibull distribution via PSO considered. A Monte-Carlo simulation study and its results are given in Section 3. Real life data is examined to show the implementation of the proposed method in Section 4. The paper ends with concluding remarks in the final section.

## 2. ML estimation of the parameters of Weibull distribution via PSO

In this section, we consider the ML estimation of the parameters of Weibull distribution via PSO. In subsection 2.1, the PSO method is briefly summarized. The likelihood equations for Weibull distribution are reviewed in subsection 2.2. The proposed methodology is given in subsection 2.3.

## 2.1. The PSO method

PSO, first proposed by Kennedy and Eberhart [28], is a population based heuristic algorithm. The idea underlying PSO is to mimic the collective behaviour of bird flocks. In PSO, each solution is called a particle and a set of solutions is called a population. PSO can avoid local optimum traps and attain a global optimal solution by searching at different points in different regions of the search space. Furthermore, it has easy implementation, high precision and fast convergence; Carnerio et al. [22]. For these reasons, PSO has been considered extensively in many applied studies; see [29-32].

Therefore, we chose to use PSO in our study.

## 2.2. Likelihood equations for three parameter Weibull distribution

Let $x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , \ldots , x _ { n }$ be a random sample drawn from $W ( \mu , \sigma , p )$ distribution. The ML estimates of the parameters , and is the point on which the following log-likelihood ( ) function attains its maximum:

$$
\ln L = n \ln p - n \ln \sigma + (p - 1) \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \ln z _ {i} - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} z _ {i} ^ {p}\tag{7}
$$

where $z _ { i } = ( x _ { i } - \mu ) / \sigma$ . In order to find the ML estimators of unknown parameters, one should take the partial derivatives of the log-likelihood function with respect to the parameters of interest and set them equal to 0. Likelihood equations do not have explicit solutions since they include nonlinear functions. It is obvious that solving likelihood equations for the unknown parameters is an optimization problem. However, this is a challenging process because of the difficulties encountered in finding an optimal solution. Therefore, using an effective heuristic optimization technique can be a wise strategy for solving this optimization problem; see [33,34]. PSO is a widely-used and well-known heuristic optimization technique.

In this study, we use PSO to find the ML estimates of the parameters of Weibull distribution. Unlike earlier studies, we propose an adaptive and narrower search space in PSO. The details of the proposed methodology are explained in the following subsection.

## 2.3. The proposed methodology

In this part of the study, we present the proposed approach for PSO in determining the search space for the parameters of Weibull distribution. Determining the ML estimates of the parameters of Weibull distribution is an optimization problem. In this optimization problem, the function, given in equation (7), is the objective function. The parameters of Weibull distribution , and are the response variables of the problem. In this study, this optimization problem is solved using the PSO method. In other words, the PSO method is utilized in attempting to determine the best values of the ML estimates of the parameters of Weibull distribution. Before starting the optimization process, initial conditions need to be determined. It is a well-known fact that initial conditions directly affect the performance of PSO as in other heuristic optimization methods. Determining the search space is a vital issue. It is obvious that PSO will attain a good solution, or the global optimal solution rapidly, if the solution space can be wisely-determined. In some studies, the search space is arbitrarily determined (see for example, Orkcu et al. [21]). Unlike earlier studies, a new adaptive search space based on confidence intervals is proposed in this study.

It should be noted that the dimension of the search space is directly related to the number of parameters in the distribution. In our case, we have three unknown parameters in Weibull distribution namely location ( ), scale ( ) and shape ( ) and it is, therefore, clear that the dimension of the search space is three. In addition, it is obvious that confidence intervals are based on sample observations, so their values directly depend on the data. For this reason, the proposed method can be seen as adaptive.

## 2.3.1. Adaptive search space based on MML estimators

In the proposed approach, the search space is strategically determined using the confidence intervals for each parameter in Weibull distribution. The confidence intervals are established by employing Tiku’s [26,27] modified maximum likelihood (MML) estimators.

The asymptotical $1 0 0 ( 1 - \alpha ) \%$ confidence intervals for the location and the scale parameters are given as follows:

$$
\hat {\mu} _ {M M L} - z _ {\alpha / 2} s e (\hat {\mu} _ {M M L}) <   \mu <   \hat {\mu} _ {M M L} + z _ {\alpha / 2} s e (\hat {\mu} _ {M M L})\tag{8}
$$

and

$$
\widehat {\sigma} _ {M M L} - z _ {\alpha / 2} s e (\widehat {\sigma} _ {M M L}) <   \sigma <   \widehat {\sigma} _ {M M L} + z _ {\alpha / 2} s e (\widehat {\sigma} _ {M M L}),\tag{9}
$$

respectively. Here, $z _ { \alpha / 2 }$ is the upper $\alpha / 2$ quantile of the standard normal distribution, ̂ and ${ \widehat { \sigma } } _ { M M L }$ are Tiku’s [26,27] MML estimators of the location and the scale parameters, respectively [5,35]. They are formulated as follows: K

$$
\hat {\mu} _ {M M L} = \hat {\mu} _ {n} - \frac {\Delta}{m} \widehat {\sigma} _ {M M L}\tag{10}
$$

and

$$
\widehat {\sigma} _ {M M L} = \frac {- B + \sqrt {B ^ {2} + 4 n C}}{2 \sqrt {n (n - 1)}}\tag{11}
$$

where

$$
\delta_ {i} = (p - 1) \beta_ {i 0} + p \beta_ {1 i}, m = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \delta_ {i}, \Delta_ {i} = (p - 1) \alpha_ {i 0} - p \alpha_ {1 i}, \Delta = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Delta_ {i},
$$

$$
B = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Delta_ {i} (y _ {(i)} - \hat {\mu} _ {n}), C = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \delta_ {i} (y _ {(i)} - \hat {\mu} _ {n}) ^ {2}, \hat {\mu} _ {n} = \frac {1}{m} \sum_ {i =} ^ {n} \delta_ {i} y _ {(i)},

$$
\beta_ {0 i} = t _ {i} ^ {- 2}, \alpha_ {0 i} = 2 t _ {i} ^ {- 1}, \beta_ {1 i} = (p - 1) t _ {i} ^ {p - 2}, \alpha_ {0 i} = (2 - p) t _ {i} ^ {p - 1},
$$

$$
t _ {(i)} = \left[ - \log \left(1 - \frac {i}{n + 1}\right) \right] ^ {1 / p}, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots , n.
$$

It is clear that the MML estimators given in equations (10)-(11) are explicitly formulated and that they are functions of the sample observations. Therefore, there will not be any difficulty in computing the MML estimators.

Furthermore, the MML method not only provides explicit forms of the estimators, but also preserves the beauty of the ML method since the MML estimators are asymptotically equivalent to the ML estimators under certain mild regularity conditions. The MML estimators are also as efficient as the ML estimators even for small sample sizes [16,36,37]. There are also plenty of recent studies showing the high performance of MML estimators; see for example, [5,38-41].

It can also be shown that the distribution of $\widehat { \pmb { \theta } } = ( \hat { \mu } _ { M M L } \widehat { \sigma } _ { M M L } ) ^ { \prime }$ is asymptotically normal with mean $\pmb \theta = ( \mu , \sigma ) ^ { \prime }$ and variance-covariance matrix $I ^ { - 1 }$ where

$$

$$

It should be noted that the MML estimators are obtained under an assumption of the known shape parameter. Therefore, the MML estimators are only given for the location and the scale parameters above. However, in practical studies, shape parameters should also be estimated. Therefore, we use a methodology known as profile likelihood here to estimate the shape parameter. The steps of this method are explained below:

Step 1. For a given value calculate the $\hat { \mu } _ { M M L }$ and ${ \widehat { \sigma } } _ { M M L }$ using equations (10)- (11).

Step 2. Calculate the log-likelihood value using the following equation:

$$

where $\hat { z } _ { i } = ( x _ { i } - \hat { \mu } _ { M M L } ) / \widehat { \sigma } _ { M M L }$

Next, the asymptotical ( ) confidence interval for the shape parameter is given by:

$$
\hat {p} - z _ {\alpha / 2} s e (\hat {p}) <   p <   \hat {p} + z _ {\alpha / 2} s e (\hat {p})\tag{13}

It should be mentioned that Weibull probability plot (WPP) method which is widely used in engineering can also be used to estimate the shape parameter of Weibull distribution. The idea underlying WPP method is to estimate the shape parameter using a simple linear regression model which is obtained taking logarithm of the cdf of Weibull distribution twice. The slope of resulting simple linear regression model is the shape parameter of Weibull distribution. Therefore, shape parameter is estimated using well-known LS method, see i.e. Murthy et al. [42] for further details. However, this LS estimator is biased. In this context, we refer to Zhang et al. [43] in which bias correction for the LS estimator of the shape parameter is given for both complete and censored data. It should be noted that we do not consider WPP method in this study since it is out of the scope.

As mentioned above, the confidence intervals given in equations (8), (9) and (13) are functions of the observations in the data set. In other words, these confidence intervals are obtained from the data, so they change according to the data. Therefore, the proposed approach can be described as an adaptive approach.

Here, the following question may arise. Can the confidence intervals (based on asymptotic normality or bootstrap) achieve nominal (asymptotic) coverage probability? It has been shown by Akgul and Senoglu [41] that asymptotic and bootstrap confidence intervals based on MML estimators achieve nominal (asymptotic) coverage probability in the context of system reliability estimation with Weibull distribution. We also refer to Xiong [44] for local optimization-based statistical inference related to this issue.

It should be noted that confidence intervals can also be constructed using the wellknown LS estimators of the corresponding parameters. However, LS estimators are sensitive to data anomalies; i.e. LS estimators lose their efficiencies when there are outliers in the data. This situation may cause unreliable confidence intervals. On the other hand, MML estimators are robust to outliers; see, for example, Senoglu and Tiku [35]. This is another advantage of using the MML based confidence intervals in this context.

The advantage of using the proposed adaptive search space in PSO is that the search space is wisely narrowed so that the algorithm examines a relatively small number of different points. This allows the PSO method to find a good solution, or the global optimal solution, rapidly.

It should be noted that the MML estimators here are used as a tool for constructing a narrower search space in PSO. The reasons for this are: (i) MML estimators have explicit forms; (ii) they are functions of the sample observations; (iii) they are easy to compute; (iv) they are asymptotically equivalent to ML estimators; and (v) they are robust to outliers.

It should be noted that the ML estimators for Weibull parameters do not exist when $p < 1$ since the likelihood function is not bounded. On the other hand, ML estimators exist for $1 \leq p \leq 2$ , but they do not satisfy the usual regularity conditions and this leads to inefficient estimators [45]. Therefore, for the remainder of the paper, we consider cases where $p > 2 .$

## 5. Conclusion

In this study, the ML estimation for the parameters of Weibull distribution is considered. It is well-known that obtaining ML estimates can be complicated in certain cases since there may be difficulties in finding a solution of the likelihood equations with respect to the parameters of interest. For this reason, using an effective heuristic optimization technique can be a wise alternative for obtaining ML estimates. Therefore, we use PSO to find the ML estimates of the parameters of Weibull distribution in this study. The originality of the paper is that we propose a new adaptive search space based on confidence intervals. Tiku’s [26,27] MML estimators are utilized to construct confidence intervals for the parameters of Weibull distribution. These confidence intervals are obtained using the sample observations. Therefore, proposed search space is adaptive and it is directly depending on the data. It is obvious that using arbitrary search space does not guarantee that it will cover true parameters. If the arbitrarily determined search space does not include the true values of the parameters, the global optimal solution will never be found. On the other hand, the proposed adaptive search space most probably includes the true values of the parameters, since the search space is determined by the confidence intervals which are obtained from the data. In addition, in the proposed approach, the search space is wisely-narrowed using confidence intervals for the parameters. The possibility of finding the global optimal solution increases, since the optimization process is performed in a narrower search space. At the same time, computation time is saved because of searching in a narrower search space. Therefore, the performance of PSO is improved as expected. In order to show the performance of the proposed approach, a Monte-Carlo simulation is performed. The simulation results show that the proposed approach produces considerably better results than those obtained from an arbitrary search space. At the end of the study, real life data obtained from the literature is also analysed to show the applicability of the proposed method. The superiority of the proposed method is also seen in this real life application.

Our main focus is to compare the performance of the proposed search space identified in a systematic way with the traditional search space based on arbitrary selections. For this reason, we just consider PSO and do not include other optimization methods into the current study. However, the proposed search space may be adapted to other optimization methods in order to improve their performances in future studies. In our study, in spite of the fact that we use the easiest and the simplest version of PSO, we obtain efficient results. We anticipate having more efficient results if we use new versions of PSO. In future studies, we plan to use recent versions of PSO to estimate distribution parameters.
