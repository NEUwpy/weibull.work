# Maximum likelihood estimation in a class of nonregular cases

By RICHARD L. SMITH

Department of Mathematics, Imperial College, London SW7 2BZ, U.K.

# SUMMARY

We consider maximum likelihood estimation of the parameters of a probability density which is zero for  $x < \theta$  and asymptotically  $\alpha c(x - \theta)^{\alpha - 1}$  as  $x \downarrow \theta$ . Here  $\theta$  and other parameters, which may or may not include  $\alpha$  and  $c$ , are unknown. The classical regularity conditions for the asymptotic properties of maximum likelihood estimators are not satisfied but it is shown that, when  $\alpha > 2$ , the information matrix is finite and the classical asymptotic properties continue to hold. For  $\alpha = 2$  the maximum likelihood estimators are asymptotically efficient and normally distributed, but with a different rate of convergence. For  $1 < \alpha < 2$ , the maximum likelihood estimators exist in general, but are not asymptotically normal, while the question of asymptotic efficiency is still unsolved. For  $\alpha \leqslant 1$ , the maximum likelihood estimators may not exist at all, but alternatives are proposed. All these results are already known for the case of a single unknown location parameter  $\theta$ , but are here extended to the case in which there are additional unknown parameters. The paper concludes with a discussion of the applications in extreme value theory.

Some key words: Extreme value theory; Maximum likelihood; Nonregular estimation; Stable distribution; Weibull distribution.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

It is well known that, if the support of a probability density depends on an unknown parameter, then the classical regularity conditions for maximum likelihood estimation are not satisfied. In some cases the maximum likelihood estimators exist and have the same asymptotic properties as in regular cases. In other cases they may exist but not be asymptotically efficient or normally distributed, and in still other cases they may not exist at all, at least not as solutions of the likelihood equations.

The case of a single unknown location parameter

$$
f (x; \theta) = f _ {0} (x - \theta) \quad (\theta <   x <   \infty); \quad f _ {0} (x) \sim \alpha c x ^ {\alpha - 1} \tag {1-1}
$$

as  $x \downarrow 0$  ( $\alpha > 0, c > 0$ ), subject to certain smoothness conditions, is well documented. For  $\alpha > 2$  the Fisher information is finite and the maximum likelihood estimators have the same asymptotic properties as in regular cases. This is stated by Woodroofe (1972, Proposition 1.1), based on results of Le Cam (1970); the result is also implicit in Dawid's (1970) proof of the asymptotic normality of posterior distributions in this case. For  $\alpha = 2$  the maximum likelihood estimators are asymptotically normal (Woodroofe, 1972; Akahira, 1975a) and efficient (Weiss & Wolfowitz, 1973), but the order of convergence is  $O\{(n \log n)^{\frac{1}{2}}\}$  instead of the usual  $O(n^{\frac{1}{2}})$ . For  $1 < \alpha < 2$  the maximum likelihood estimators have a nonnormal limiting distribution with order of convergence  $O(n^{1 / \alpha})$

(Woodroofo, 1974). Asymptotic efficiency is an open question, though Akahira (1975a, b) showed that no estimator has a larger order of convergence. When  $\alpha \leqslant 1$  the likelihood function has no local maximum but is globally maximized at the sample minimum. The sample minimum itself is a consistent estimator with order of convergence  $O(n^{1 / \alpha})$ , and Akahira's results show that, for  $0 < \alpha < 2$ , this order of convergence cannot be improved. When  $\alpha = 1$  a result of Weiss (1979) shows that the sample minimum has a property of asymptotic sufficiency, but I have not succeeded in applying Weiss's argument for any other value of  $\alpha$ .

In the present paper these results are extended to distributions in which there are other unknown parameters as well as  $\theta$ . Some examples which fall within our framework are as follows.

(i) Three-parameter Weibull,

$$
f (x; \theta , \alpha , \beta) = \alpha \beta (x - \theta) ^ {\alpha - 1} \exp \left\{- \beta (x - \theta) ^ {\alpha} \right\} \quad (\theta <   x <   \infty , \alpha > 0, \beta > 0). (1 \cdot 2)
$$

(ii) Three-parameter gamma,

$$
f (x; \theta , \alpha , \beta) = \beta^ {\alpha} (x - \theta) ^ {\alpha - 1} \exp \left\{- \beta (x - \theta) \right\} / \Gamma (\alpha) \quad (\theta <   x <   \infty , \alpha > 0, \beta > 0). \tag {1·3}
$$

(iii) Three-parameter beta, which is a beta distribution with an unknown scale parameter, which can be recast in our framework by defining  $X$  to be a random variable on  $\theta < x < \infty$  with  $1 - \exp(-X + \theta)$  beta distributed; thus

$$
f (x; \theta , \alpha , \beta) = B (\alpha , \beta) \left\{1 - e ^ {- (x - \theta)} \right\} ^ {\alpha - 1} e ^ {- \beta (x - \theta)} \quad (\theta <   x <   \infty , \alpha > 0, \beta > 0). \tag {1·4}
$$

(iv) Three-parameter log gamma, which arises when  $\log (X - \theta +1)$  has a gamma distribution; thus

$$
f (x; \theta , \alpha , \beta) = \beta^ {\alpha} \left\{\log (x - \theta + 1) \right\} ^ {\alpha - 1} (x - \theta + 1) ^ {- \beta - 1} / \Gamma (\alpha) \quad (\theta <   x <   \infty , \alpha > 0, \beta > 0). \tag {1·5}
$$

Our results also cover certain instances of the Box-Cox transformation family but we shall not consider these explicitly.

Of these four examples, the Weibull has been studied the most intensively. For all four, when  $\alpha < 1$  the density tends to infinity as  $x\downarrow \theta$  so that, unless the range  $\alpha < 1$  is excluded, the likelihood function always tends to infinity along some path in the likelihood space as  $\theta$  tends to the sample minimum. Therefore it is necessary to distinguish between global and local maxima of the likelihood. In this paper, by the maximum likelihood estimator we shall always mean a local maximum, thus satisfying the likelihood equations. A second point, which applies to all four examples but may not be true in general, is that for  $\alpha < 1$  the density is J-shaped, so there cannot exist a maximum likelihood estimator for which  $\alpha < 1$ . In particular, if the true value of  $\alpha$  is less than 1, maximum likelihood estimators either do not exist at all or are inconsistent.

Harter & Moore (1965) described an iterative procedure for finding maximum likelihood estimators for the Weibull and gamma distributions with possibly censored data. In cases where maximum likelihood estimators do not exist, they proposed an ad hoc modification based on treating the smallest observation as if it were censored. Rockette, Antle & Klimko (1974) showed for the Weibull distribution that, if a local maximum of the likelihood function exists, then there is a second solution of the likelihood equations which is a saddlepoint. Their result shows that, in finding maximum

likelihood estimators one must take care to ensure that a solution of the likelihood equations really is a local maximum. Other relevant references are Cohen (1965) and Lemon (1975). The Weibull distribution may be reparameterized as the generalized extreme value distribution (Jenkinson, 1955) for which modern algorithms are available (NERC, 1975; Prescott & Walden, 1980, 1983).

Thus there is a fair-sized literature on finding maximum likelihood estimators of the Weibull distribution, but their asymptotic properties are largely unexplored. It is easily checked that, when  $\alpha > 2$ , the Fisher information matrix is finite, and it is widely assumed that the classical properties hold in this case. For  $\alpha \leqslant 2$  the Fisher information for  $\theta$  is infinite, so the classical results are certainly not valid.

In this paper it is confirmed that the classical results hold when  $\alpha > 2$ , and the case  $\alpha \leqslant 2$  is studied in detail. The surprising result is that, in this case, estimation of  $\theta$  and the other parameters, denoted by  $\phi$ , in general a vector, are asymptotically independent: each of the maximum likelihood estimators of  $\theta$  and  $\phi$  has the same asymptotic distribution when the other is unknown as when the other is known, and we are also able to show that these asymptotic distributions are independent. For  $1 < \alpha < 2$  we prove the existence of a consistent sequence of maximum likelihood estimators as the sample size tends to infinity, while for  $\alpha < 1$  no consistent maximum likelihood estimators exist. We also propose efficient alternatives to maximum likelihood which, in particular, cover the cases where maximum likelihood estimators do not exist.

Cheng & Amin (1983) have also studied the asymptotic properties of maximum likelihood estimators for the Weibull and gamma cases, though their Theorem 2 is less extensive than our results in §§3 and 4 and their proofs are unpublished. On the other hand they introduce a new estimator, the 'maximum product of spacings' estimator, which deserves to be studied further. Johnson & Haskell (1983) prove consistency of the maximum likelihood estimator for the three-parameter Weibull with  $\alpha > 1$ , and present Monte Carlo results which indicate that, even in the 'regular' case  $\alpha > 2$ , the asymptotic normality of the estimators is approached only slowly.

Our main results require a long list of assumptions, which are stated in §2. The remainder of the paper is organized so that statements of the main results appear at the beginning of each section, and may be understood without reference to the proofs. Results of a purely technical nature are stated as lemmas and contained within the proofs of the theorems, though Lemmas 6 and 7 may be of independent interest.

# 2. ASSUMPTIONS

We consider probability densities of the form

$$
f (x; \theta , \phi) = (x - \theta) ^ {\alpha - 1} g (x - \theta ; \phi) \quad (\theta <   x <   \infty), \tag {2·1}
$$

where  $\theta$  and  $\phi$ , the latter a vector, are unknown parameters and  $g$  tends to a constant  $\alpha c$  as  $x \downarrow \theta$ . We assume  $\alpha \equiv \alpha(\phi)$  is a twice continuously differentiable function of  $\phi$  with  $0 < \alpha < \infty$ . This formulation allows both the case when  $\alpha$  is a component of  $\phi$  and where it is a known constant. Similarly  $c \equiv c(\phi)$ . For our four examples the function  $g$  is given by:

(i) Weibull,  $g(x;\alpha ,\beta) = \alpha \beta \exp (-\beta x^{\alpha});$  
(ii) gamma,  $g(x;\alpha ,\beta) = \beta^{\alpha}\exp (-\beta x) / \Gamma (\alpha);$  
(iii) beta,  $g(x;\alpha ,\beta) = B(\alpha ,\beta)\left\{(1 - e^{-x}) / x\right\}^{\alpha -1}e^{-\beta x};$  
(iv) log gamma,  $g(x;\alpha ,\beta) = \beta^{\alpha}\{(\log (1 + x)) / x\}^{\alpha -1}(1 + x)^{-\beta -1} / \Gamma (\alpha).$

In general, we assume  $\theta$  is real-valued and  $\phi$  takes values in a set  $\Phi \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p$  for some  $p \geqslant 1$ . We adopt the convention that subscripts,  $\phi_1, \phi_2$ , etc., are used to denote elements of  $\Phi$ , whilst superscripts,  $\phi^1, \phi^2$ , etc., denote components of a particular  $\phi$ . Thus  $\phi_j^i (i = 1, \dots, p)$  is the  $i$ th component of  $\phi_j$ . The symbol  $|\phi|$  denotes the  $L^2$  norm of  $\phi$ .

The detailed assumptions are as follows.

Assumption 1. All second-order partial derivatives of  $g(x; \phi)$  exist and are continuous in  $0 < x < \infty$ ,  $\phi \in \Phi$ . Moreover  $c(\phi) = \alpha^{-1} \lim g(x; \phi)$  as  $x \downarrow 0$  exists, is positive and finite for each  $\phi$ , and is twice continuously differentiable as a function of  $\phi$ .

Assumption 2. If  $\phi_{1}$  and  $\phi_{2}$  are distinct elements of  $\Phi$  then the set

$$
\{x \colon f (x; 0, \phi_ {1}) \neq f (x; 0, \phi_ {2}) \}
$$

has positive Lebesgue measure.

Assumption 3. For some fixed  $\delta^{*}\geqslant 0$  and for each  $\eta >0,\phi_1\in \Phi$  , we have

$$
\log g (x - \varepsilon ; \phi) - \log g (x - \varepsilon_ {1}; \phi_ {1}) <   H _ {\eta} (x; \phi_ {1}),
$$

whenever  $|\varepsilon| < \eta$ ,  $|\varepsilon_1| < \eta$ ,  $|\phi - \phi_1| < \eta$ ,  $x - \varepsilon > \delta^*$ ,  $x - \varepsilon_1 > \delta^*$  and the function  $H_\eta$  satisfies

$$
\lim  _ {\eta \rightarrow 0} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} H _ {\eta} (x + y; \phi_ {1}) f (x; 0, \phi_ {0}) d x = 0
$$

for each  $y\geqslant 0,\phi_0\in \Phi$

Assumption 4. There exists a fixed increasing sequence of compact subsets  $\{K_m, m \geqslant 1\}$  of  $\mathbb{R}^{p+1}$  and a fixed constant  $\delta'$  such that  $\bigcup_{m} K_m = \mathbb{R} \times \Phi$ , and, for  $\theta, \theta_0, \phi, \phi_0$  satisfying  $\theta < \theta_0 - \delta', \phi, \phi_0 \in \Phi, (\theta, \phi) \notin K_m$ , we have

$$
\log f (x; \theta , \phi) - \log f (x; \theta_ {0}, \phi_ {0}) <   H _ {m} ^ {0} (x; \theta_ {0}, \phi_ {0}) (x > \theta_ {0}),
$$

where

$$
- \infty \leqslant \lim  _ {m \rightarrow \infty} \sup  _ {\theta_ {0}} \int_ {\theta_ {0}} ^ {\infty} H _ {m} ^ {0} (x; \theta_ {0}, \phi_ {0}) f (x; \theta_ {0}, \phi_ {0}) d x <   0.
$$

Assumption 5. There exists a fixed increasing sequence of compact subsets  $\{K_m', m \geqslant 1\}$  of  $\Phi$  with  $\bigcup_{m} K_m' = \Phi$ , and  $\delta' > 0$  a fixed constant, such that for  $\phi \in \Phi - K_m'$ ,  $\phi_0 \in \Phi$ ,  $|\varepsilon| < \delta'$ ,  $\max(0, \varepsilon) < x < \infty$  and some  $\eta' > 0$ , we have

$$
\eta^ {\prime} \alpha (\phi) + \left\{\alpha (\phi) - \alpha \left(\phi_ {0}\right) \right\} \log x + \log g (x - \varepsilon ; \phi) - \log g (x; \phi_ {0}) <   H _ {m} ^ {\prime} (x; \phi_ {0}),
$$

where

$$
- \infty \leqslant \lim  _ {m \rightarrow \infty} \sup  _ {0} ^ {\infty} H _ {m} ^ {\prime} (x; \phi_ {0}) f (x; 0, \phi_ {0}) d x <   0.
$$

Assumption 6. If  $E_{\phi}$  denotes expectation with respect to  $f(.,0,\phi)$  then for each  $\phi \in \Phi (i,j = 1,\dots,p)$ :

(a)

$$
E _ {\phi} \left\{\left(\frac {\partial}{\partial \phi^ {i}}\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \right\} = 0,
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} E _ {\phi} \left\{\left(\frac {\partial}{\partial \phi^ {i}}\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \left(\frac {\partial}{\partial \phi^ {j}}\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \right\} = - E _ {\phi} \left\{\left(\frac {\partial^ {2}}{\partial \phi^ {i} \partial \phi^ {j}}\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \right\} \\ = m _ {i j} (\phi); \\ \end{array}
$$

(b) if  $\alpha > 1$ , then

$$
\begin{array}{l} E _ {\phi} \Bigl \{\Bigl (\frac {\partial}{\partial x} \Bigr) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \Bigr \} = 0, \\ - E _ {\phi} \Bigg \{\left(\frac {\partial}{\partial x}\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \left(\frac {\partial}{\partial \phi^ {i}}\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \Bigg \} = E _ {\phi} \Bigg \{\left(\frac {\partial^ {2}}{\partial x \partial \phi^ {i}}\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \Bigg \} \\ = m _ {i 0} (\phi) = m _ {0 i} (\phi); \\ \end{array}
$$

(c) if  $\alpha > 2$ , then

$$
\begin{array}{l} E _ {\phi} \left[ \left\{\left(\partial / \partial x\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \right\} ^ {2} \right] = - E _ {\phi} \left\{\left(\partial^ {2} / \partial x ^ {2}\right) \log f (X; 0, \phi) \right\} \\ = m _ {0 0} (\phi) > 0. \\ \end{array}
$$

It is part of the assumption that all these expectations are finite.

Assumption 7. If  $h(x; \phi)$  is any of  $(\partial^2 / \partial x \partial \phi^i) \log g(x; \phi)$  or  $(\partial^2 / \partial \phi^i \partial \phi^j) \log g(x; \phi)$ , then, as  $\theta \to \theta_0, \phi \to \phi_0$ ,

$$
E _ {0} \left| h \left(X - \theta ; \phi\right) - h \left(X - \theta_ {0}; \phi_ {0}\right)\right|\rightarrow 0,
$$

where  $E_0$  is with respect to  $f(.;\theta_0,\phi_0)$ . If  $\alpha (\phi_0) > 2$ , we require the same of  $h(x;\phi) = (\partial^2 /\partial x^2)\log g(x;\phi)$ .

Assumption 8. For each  $\varepsilon >0,\delta >0$  , there exists a function  $h_{\varepsilon ,\delta}$  such that

$$
\left| \left(\partial^ {2} / \partial x ^ {2}\right) \log g (x; \phi) \right| <   \varepsilon / x ^ {2} + h _ {\varepsilon , \delta} (y; \phi_ {0})
$$

whenever  $|\phi - \phi_0| < \delta$ ,  $|x - y| < \delta$ , and  $h_{\varepsilon, \delta}$  satisfies

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {\infty} h _ {\varepsilon , \delta} (x; \phi_ {0}) f (x; 0, \phi_ {0}) <   \infty .
$$

Assumption 9. For each  $\delta >0,\phi \in \Phi$

$$
\int_ {\delta} ^ {\infty} \left\{(\partial / \partial x) \log g (x; \phi) \right\} ^ {2} f (x; 0, \phi) d x <   \infty .
$$

We now make some remarks on the assumptions. The key assumptions are Assumptions 1 and 6; the rest are there for technical reasons. Assumptions 2-5 are similar to, but necessarily more complicated than, the classical assumptions of Wald (1949). In Assumption 3 we may have  $\delta^{*} = 0$  or  $\delta^{*} > 0$ ; in the Weibull case, Assumption 3 is true with  $\delta^{*} > 0$  for all  $\alpha$  but with  $\delta^{*} = 0$  only for  $\alpha > 1$ . The distinction is reflected in the statement of Theorem 2 below. Assumptions 7 and 8 are needed for Lemma 4 in §3, and Assumption 9 is taken from Woodrofe (1972, 1974). Assumption 1 could be weakened to  $g(x;\phi)$  slowly varying at  $x = 0$  for each  $\phi$ , but at the cost of an increase in technical detail.

For all our examples, Assumptions 1-6 and 9 are straightforward, if somewhat tedious, to check. For examples (ii)-(iv),  $\log g$  and its partial derivatives are bounded in  $x$  for each  $\phi$ , so Assumptions 7 and 8 are easy to check as well. For the Weibull distribution we have

$$
\log g (x; \alpha , \beta) = \log \alpha + \log \beta - \beta x ^ {\alpha},
$$

$$
\frac {\partial}{\partial x} \log g (x; \alpha , \beta) = - \alpha \beta x ^ {\alpha - 1}, \quad \frac {\partial^ {2}}{\partial x ^ {2}} \log g (x; \alpha , \beta) = - \alpha (\alpha - 1) \beta x ^ {\alpha - 2},
$$

which are bounded as  $x \downarrow 0$  respectively for  $\alpha > 0, \alpha > 1, \alpha > 2$ ; Assumption 7 follows easily. To satisfy Assumption 8, given  $0 < \alpha_{1} < \alpha_{0} < \alpha_{2}$  and  $\delta > 0$ , there exists  $K$  such that

$$
\left| \frac {\partial^ {2}}{\partial x ^ {2}} \log g (x; \alpha , \beta) \right| <   \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} K x ^ {\alpha_ {1} - 2} & (x \leqslant 1), \\ K x ^ {\alpha_ {2} - 2} & (x \geqslant 1), \end{array} \right.
$$

and all  $(\alpha, \beta)$  satisfying  $\alpha_{1} < \alpha < \alpha_{2}, |\beta - \beta_{0}| < \delta$ . Let  $x_{\varepsilon} = (\varepsilon / K)^{1 / \alpha}$  which we assume to be  $\leqslant 1$ . Then

$$
\left| \left(\partial^ {2} / \partial x ^ {2}\right) \log g (x; \alpha , \beta) \right| <   \varepsilon / x ^ {2} + h _ {\varepsilon , \delta} ^ {\prime} (x; \alpha_ {0}, \beta_ {0}),
$$

where

$$
h _ {\varepsilon , \delta} ^ {\prime} (x; \alpha_ {0}, \beta_ {0}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & (x \leqslant x _ {\varepsilon}), \\ K x ^ {\alpha_ {1} - 2} & (x _ {\varepsilon} \leqslant x \leqslant 1), \\ K x ^ {\alpha_ {2} - 2} & (x \geqslant 1). \end{array} \right.
$$

This function is bounded as  $x \to 0$ . If we define  $h_{\varepsilon, \delta}(y; \alpha_0, \beta_0)$  to be

$$
\sup \left\{h _ {\varepsilon , \delta} ^ {\prime} (x; \alpha_ {0}, \beta_ {0}) \colon | x - y | <   \delta \right\},
$$

Assumption 8 is satisfied.

# 3. EXISTENCE AND UNIQUENESS OF CONSISTENT ESTIMATORS

From now on we shall let  $\theta_0, \phi_0$  denote the true values of  $\theta$  and  $\phi$ . The letters  $\alpha$  and  $c$  unless indicated otherwise, will always denote  $\alpha(\phi_0)$  and  $c(\phi_0)$ . Let  $X_1, \ldots, X_n$  denote a sample of independent observations from the common density  $f(.; \theta_0, \phi_0)$ . Let  $L_n$  denote the log likelihood divided by  $n$ , that is

$$
L _ {n} (\theta , \phi) = n ^ {- 1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \log f (X _ {i}; \theta , \phi).
$$

The order statistics will be denoted  $X_{n,1} < \ldots < X_{n,n}$ ; note that  $L_{n}(\theta, \phi)$  is defined only for  $\theta < X_{n,1}$ .

The maximum likelihood estimator, when it exists, will be denoted by  $(\hat{\theta}_n,\hat{\phi}_n)$  and thus satisfies

$$
\partial L _ {n} (\widehat {\theta} _ {n}, \widehat {\phi} _ {n}) / \partial \theta = 0, \quad \partial L _ {n} (\widehat {\theta} _ {n}, \widehat {\phi} _ {n}) / \partial \phi^ {i} = 0 \quad (i = 1, \dots , p).
$$

For the special case when  $\theta = \theta_0$  is known, let  $\bar{\phi}_n \equiv \bar{\phi}_n(\theta_0)$  denote the maximum likelihood estimator for  $\phi$ , satisfying  $(\partial L_n / \partial \phi^i)(\theta_0, \bar{\phi}_n) = 0$ . The existence and consistency of  $\bar{\phi}_n$  follows from the classical results for regular estimation problems. Similarly, let  $\bar{\theta}_n \equiv \bar{\theta}_n(\phi_0)$  denote the maximum likelihood estimator for  $\theta$  when  $\phi = \phi_0$  is known. The asymptotic properties of  $\bar{\theta}_n$  are given by the results in §1. In particular,  $\bar{\theta}_n$  exists and is consistent when  $\alpha > 1$ .

Define  $\xi_{n,\beta}$  ( $n\geqslant 1$ ) to be 1 if  $\beta >1$ ,  $\log n$  if  $\beta = 1$  and  $n^{1 / \beta -1}$  if  $0 < \beta < 1$ , and write  $Y_{n} < _{\mathrm{p}}r_{n}$ , for random variables  $\{Y_n\}$  and positive constants  $\{r_n\}$ , if

$$
\lim  _ {a \rightarrow \infty} \lim  _ {n \rightarrow \infty} \sup  _ {n \rightarrow \infty} \Pr \left(\left| Y _ {n} \right| > a r _ {n}\right) = 0. \tag {3-1}
$$

Our main result in this section is the following.

THEOREM 1. Assume Assumptions 1 and 6-8 are satisfied, and that  $\alpha > 1$ . Suppose that

$M$  is strictly positive-definite, where:

(i) for  $\alpha > 2$ ,  $M$  is the  $(p + 1) \times (p + 1)$  matrix with entries  $m_{ij}(\phi_0)$ $(i, j = 0, \dots, p)$ ;  
(ii) for  $1 < \alpha \leqslant 2$ ,  $M$  is the  $p \times p$  matrix with entries  $m_{ij}(\phi_0)$  ( $i, j = 1, \ldots, p$ ), the functions  $m_{ij}$  being defined in Assumption 6. Then there exists a sequence  $(\hat{\theta}_n, \hat{\phi}_n)$  of solutions to the likelihood equations such that

$$
\hat {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0} <   _ {\mathrm {p}} (n \xi_ {n, \alpha}) ^ {- \frac {1}{2}}, \quad \hat {\phi} _ {n} - \phi_ {0} <   _ {\mathrm {p}} n ^ {- \frac {1}{2}}.
$$

Moreover, if  $\alpha = 2$  we have

$$
\hat {\theta} _ {n} - \bar {\theta} _ {n} <   _ {\mathrm {p}} n ^ {- \frac {1}{2}} (\log n) ^ {- 1}, \quad \hat {\phi} _ {n} - \bar {\phi} _ {n} <   _ {\mathrm {p}} (n \log n) ^ {- \frac {1}{2}},
$$

while if  $1 < \alpha < 2$  we have

$$
\hat {\theta} _ {n} - \bar {\theta} _ {n} <   _ {\mathrm {p}} n ^ {- 2 / \alpha + \frac {1}{2}}, \quad \hat {\phi} _ {n} - \bar {\phi} _ {n} <   _ {\mathrm {p}} n ^ {- 1 / \alpha}.
$$

Theorem 1 is a result about the local behaviour of the likelihood function near the true parameter values. There is no guarantee that the local maximum, whose existence is guaranteed by Theorem 1 when  $\alpha > 1$ , is unique, and we know already that the local maximum is not a global maximum. The following result goes part of the way to settling the uniqueness of the estimator. It is an analogue for this problem of Wald's (1949) result on the consistency of the maximum likelihood estimator in regular estimation problems.

THEOREM 2. Suppose that, for each  $n$ , we have a sample of  $n$  independent observations from the density  $f(\cdot; \theta_0, \phi_0)$ , ordered  $X_{n,1} < \ldots < X_{n,n}$ . For fixed  $\delta > 0$ ,  $\varepsilon > 0$ ,  $\gamma < \infty$ , define the following regions of the parameter space  $\mathbb{R} \times \Phi$ :  $U$  is the set of  $(\theta, \phi)$  for which  $\theta < \theta_0 - \delta$ ,  $\phi \in \Phi$ , and  $V_n$  is the set of  $(\theta, \phi)$  for which  $|\theta - \theta_0| < \delta$ ,  $|\phi - \phi_0| > \delta$  and either  $\alpha(\phi) > 1 + \varepsilon$  or  $\theta < X_{n,1} - n^{-\gamma}$ .

(i) Suppose Assumptions 1-6 hold with  $\delta^{*} = \delta$  in Assumption 3,  $\delta^1 = \delta$  in Assumptions 4 and 5. Then

$$

$$

(ii) Suppose Assumptions 1-6 hold with  $\delta^{*} = 0$  in Assumption 3,  $\delta' = \delta$  in Assumptions 4 and 5, and  $\alpha > 1$ . Then

$$

We remark that the main interest in this result lies in the case  $\alpha > 1$ , when (i) and (ii) both hold. The theorem then shows that the region on which  $X_{n,1} - \theta$  is exponentially small and  $\alpha(\phi) \leqslant 1$  is asymptotically the only region where the likelihood function is badly behaved. When  $\alpha < 1$ , for all our four examples the log likelihood is J-shaped, which shows at once that there can be no consistent maximum likelihood estimator.

Note that we have not settled the question of whether there is a unique local maximum of the likelihood function. The proof of Theorem 1 makes it clear that the Hessian of the log likelihood is negative-definite on a small neighbourhood depending on  $n$  around  $(\theta_0, \phi_0)$ , and results of Mäkeläinen, Schmidt & Styan (1981) then show that there is a unique local maximum on this neighbourhood. The question of global uniqueness is much harder to resolve.

\operatorname * {p r} \left\{Z _ {n t} \geqslant 0, n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \Sigma_ {j} a ^ {j} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \leqslant y \right\},
$$

where  $Z_{nt}$  ( $n \geqslant 1$ ) is a sequence of renormalized row sums of a triangular array, converging to an infinitely divisible limit. The limit is given in Woodroofe's Theorem 2-2, and does not contain a Gaussian component. Therefore the limiting probability in (4-3) is the same as that of

$$
\operatorname * {p r} \left\{(c n) ^ {1 / \alpha} (\bar {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0}) \leqslant - t \right\} \operatorname * {p r} \left\{n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \Sigma_ {j} a ^ {j} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \leqslant y \right\}.
$$

Similar arguments may be applied to the limits of

$$
\operatorname * {p r} \left\{(c n) ^ {1 / \alpha} (\bar {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0}) \leqslant 0, n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \Sigma_ {j} a ^ {j} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \leqslant y \right\},
$$

$$
\operatorname * {p r} \left\lbrace (c n) ^ {1 / \alpha} (\bar {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0}) > t, n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \Sigma_ {j} a ^ {j} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \leqslant y | X _ {n, 1} > \theta_ {0} + t (c n) ^ {- 1 / \alpha} \right\rbrace
$$

for  $t > 0$ , using Theorems 2-1 and 2-3 of Woodroofoe's paper. The last equation implies that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname * {l i m p r} \left\{(c n) ^ {1 / \alpha} (\bar {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0}) > t, n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \Sigma_ {j} a ^ {j} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \leqslant y \right\} \\ = \operatorname * {l i m p r} \left\{(c n) ^ {1 / \alpha} (\bar {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0}) > t, n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \Sigma_ {j} a ^ {j} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \leqslant y \mid X _ {n, 1} > \theta_ {0} + t (c n) ^ {- 1 / \alpha} \right\} \\ \times \operatorname {p r} \left\{X _ {n, 1} > \theta_ {0} + t (c n) ^ {- 1 / \alpha} \right\} \\ = \lim  \Pr \left\{\left(c n\right) ^ {1 / \alpha} \left(\bar {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0}\right) > t \mid X _ {n, 1} > \theta_ {0} + t \left(c n\right) ^ {- 1 / \alpha} \right\} \\ \times \operatorname * {p r} \left\{n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \Sigma_ {j} a ^ {j} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \leqslant y | X _ {n, 1} > \theta_ {0} + t (c n) ^ {- 1 / \alpha} \right\} \operatorname * {p r} \left\{X _ {n, 1} > \theta_ {0} + t (c n) ^ {- 1 / \alpha} \right\}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Lemma 7 implies the asymptotic independence of  $(cn)^{-1 / \alpha}(X_{n,1} - \theta_0)$  and the score statistic evaluated at  $\phi_0$  for the parameter  $\Sigma a^{j}\phi^{j}$ . It easily follows that the second factor in this expression is independent of  $t$ , and hence that the whole expression equals

$$
\operatorname * {l i m p r} \left\{(c n) ^ {1 / \alpha} (\bar {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0}) > t \right\} \operatorname * {p r} \left\{n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \Sigma_ {j} a ^ {j} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \leqslant y \right\}.
$$

This proves the independence of the asymptotic distributions of  $\bar{\theta}_n$  and  $\Sigma a^j\bar{\phi}_n^j$  for any  $a^1,\ldots ,a^p$ , and hence gives the required result.

Proof of Corollary. Rewrite (4-1) as

$$
(n c \log n) ^ {\frac {1}{2}} (\bar {\theta} _ {n} - \theta_ {0}) \left\{- \frac {1}{c \log n} \frac {\partial^ {2} L _ {n}}{\partial \theta^ {2}} (\bar {\theta} _ {n}, \phi_ {0}) \right\} ^ {\frac {1}{2}},
$$

the product of two factors, the first converging to standard normal and the second to 1. This gives (4·1), and (4·2) is similar. Note that (4·1) also holds if  $\alpha > 2$ .

# 5. AN ALTERNATIVE TO MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD

The complicated nature of the preceding results when  $1 < \alpha \leqslant 2$ , and the nonexistence of a consistent estimator when  $\alpha < 1$ , make it desirable to seek some alternative estimator. An obvious candidate for a point estimator of  $\theta$  is  $X_{n,1}$ , the sample minimum. The asymptotic distribution of  $n^{1 / \alpha}(X_{n,1} - \theta_0)$  is Weibull, and Akahira's results show that no point estimator of  $\theta$  converges at a faster rate when  $\alpha < 2$ . Thus it seems reasonable to use  $X_{n,1}$  as an estimator of  $\theta$  when  $\alpha < 2$ . The difficulties are, first, that it is generally not known a priori whether  $\alpha < 2$  and, secondly, that we still need an estimator of  $\phi$ .

In this section we propose a new estimator  $\tilde{\phi}_n$  of  $\phi$ . It is consistent, so that it may be used to discriminate between the cases  $\alpha > 2, \alpha < 2$ , and when  $\alpha < 2$  it is asymptotically efficient, and may therefore be used in place of the maximum likelihood estimator  $\hat{\phi}_n$ . The new estimator is defined as the local maximum of the function

$$
\sum_ {k = 2} ^ {n} \log f (X _ {n, k}; X _ {n, 1}, \phi).
$$

It is therefore equivalent to estimating  $\theta$  by the smallest observation, and dropping that observation for the estimation of  $\phi$  by maximum likelihood.

Define a modified likelihood function

$$
\tilde {L} _ {n} (\theta , \phi) = n ^ {- 1} \sum_ {k = 2} ^ {n} \log f (X _ {n, k}; \theta , \phi).
$$

Thus our new estimator satisfies

$$
\partial \tilde {L} _ {n} (X _ {n, 1}, \tilde {\phi}) / \partial \phi^ {i} = 0 (i = 1, \dots , p). \qquad \qquad (5 \cdot 1)
$$

THEOREM 4. Under the conditions of Theorem 1, with probability tending to 1 as  $n \to \infty$ , there exists an estimator  $\tilde{\phi}_n$  satisfying (5-1), and we have

$$
\tilde {\phi} _ {n} - \bar {\phi} _ {n} <   _ {\mathrm {p}} \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} n ^ {- 1 / \alpha} & (\alpha > 1), \\ n ^ {- 1} \log n & (\alpha \leqslant 1). \end{array} \right.
$$

COROLLARY. For all  $\alpha, \widetilde{\phi}_n$  is a consistent estimator of  $\phi_0$ . If  $\alpha < 2$ , then  $\widetilde{\phi}_n$  is also asymptotically efficient, and  $n^{\frac{1}{2}} (\widetilde{\phi}_n - \phi_0)$  converges to a normal distribution with mean

zero and covariance matrix  $M^{-1}$ , where  $M$  is the  $p \times p$  matrix with entries

$$
m _ {i j} (\phi_ {0}) \quad (i, j = 1, \dots , p).
$$

Remark. The results of this section continue to hold if  $X_{n,1}$  is replaced by any  $n^{1 / \alpha}$ -consistent estimator  $\widetilde{\theta}_n$  in (5·1), provided  $\widetilde{\theta}_n \leqslant X_{n,1}$ .

Proofs. We start with some elementary observations about  $\tilde{L}_n$ . Clearly,

$$
\begin{array}{l} L _ {n} (\theta , \phi) - \tilde {L} _ {n} (\theta , \phi) = n ^ {- 1} \log f (X _ {n, 1}; \theta , \phi) \\ = n ^ {- 1} \{\alpha (\phi) - 1 \} \log (X _ {n, 1} - \theta) + n ^ {- 1} \log g (X _ {n, 1} - \theta ; \phi). \\ \end{array}
$$

When  $\theta = \theta_0$  we have

$$
\log \left(X _ {n, 1} - \theta\right) = O _ {p} (\log n), \quad \log g (X _ {n, 1} - \theta ; \phi) \to_ {\mathfrak {p}} \log c (\phi),
$$

so that the whole expression is  $O_{p}(n^{-1}\log n)$ . The same applies to

$$
\left(\partial / \partial \phi^ {i}\right) \left\{L _ {n} (\theta , \phi) - \tilde {L} _ {n} (\theta , \phi) \right\},
$$

since

$$
\left(\partial / \partial \phi^ {i}\right) \log g \left(X _ {n, 1} - \theta_ {0}; \phi\right)\rightarrow_ {\mathfrak {p}} \left(\partial / \partial \phi^ {i}\right) \log c (\phi)
$$

by Assumption 1. We also note that parts II and III of Lemma 4 apply with  $\tilde{L}_n$  in place of  $L_{n}$ , and without the lower bound restriction on  $X_{n,1} - \theta_0$ .

Let

$$
\xi_ {n} ^ {i} = (\partial / \partial \phi^ {i}) \tilde {L} _ {n} (\theta_ {0}, \bar {\phi} _ {n}) = (\partial / \partial \phi^ {i}) \left\{\tilde {L} _ {n} (\theta_ {0}, \bar {\phi} _ {n}) - L _ {n} (\theta_ {0}, \bar {\phi} _ {n}) \right\}
$$

which is  $<_{\mathfrak{p}} n^{-1} \log n$ , by the preceding remarks. Therefore

$$
\frac {\partial \tilde {L} _ {n}}{\partial \phi^ {i}} (X _ {n, 1}, \phi) = \xi_ {n} ^ {i} + (X _ {n, 1} - \theta_ {0}) \frac {\partial^ {2} \tilde {L} _ {n}}{\partial \theta \partial \phi^ {i}} (\theta^ {*}, \phi^ {*}) + \Sigma_ {j} (\phi^ {j} - \bar {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j}) \frac {\partial^ {2} \tilde {L} _ {n}}{\partial \phi^ {i} \partial \phi^ {j}} (\theta^ {*}, \phi^ {*}),
$$

where  $(\theta^{*},\phi^{*}) = \lambda (X_{n,1},\phi) + (1 - \lambda)(\theta_0,\bar{\phi}_n)$  for some  $\lambda$ $(0 <   \lambda <  1)$

Suppose  $\alpha \leqslant 1$ . Let  $\delta_n, n \geqslant 1$ , be any positive sequence such that  $\delta_n \to 0$ ,  $n\delta_n / \log n \to \infty$ . Define, for  $y \in \mathbb{R}^p$  such that  $|y| = 1$ ,

$$
f _ {n} (y) = \delta_ {n} ^ {- 2} \tilde {L} _ {n} \left(X _ {n, 1}, \bar {\phi} _ {n} + \delta_ {n} y\right). \tag {5·2}
$$

Then

$$
\frac {\partial f _ {n}}{\partial y ^ {i}} = \delta_ {n} ^ {- 1} \left[ \xi_ {n} ^ {i} + (X _ {n, 1} - \theta_ {0}) \frac {\partial^ {2} L _ {n}}{\partial \theta \partial \phi^ {i}} \left\{\theta_ {n} ^ {*} (y), \phi_ {n} ^ {*} (y) \right\} \right] + \Sigma_ {j} y ^ {j} \frac {\partial^ {2} L _ {n}}{\partial \phi^ {i} \partial \phi^ {j}} \left\{\theta_ {n} ^ {*} (y), \phi_ {n} ^ {*} (y) \right\},
$$

where the dependence of  $(\theta^{*},\phi^{*})$  on both  $n$  and  $y$  is indicated. Now, for all  $y$  with  $|y| = 1$ , we have

$$
\left| \theta_ {n} ^ {*} (y) - \theta_ {0} \right| <   \left| X _ {n, 1} - \theta_ {0} \right| <   _ {\mathbf {p}} n ^ {- 1 / \alpha}, \quad \left| \phi_ {n} ^ {*} (y) - \phi_ {0} \right| \leqslant \delta_ {n} \rightarrow 0,
$$

so by Lemma 4, modified to apply to  $\tilde{L}_n$  as indicated above,

$$
\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{L}_{n}}{\partial\theta\partial\phi^{i}}\bigl\{\theta_{n}^{*}(y),  \phi_{n}^{*}(y)\bigr \} <  \mathrm{p}\left\{ \begin{array}{ll}\log n & (\alpha = 1),\\ n^{1 / \alpha -1} & (\alpha <  1), \end{array} \right.
$$

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} \tilde {L} _ {n}}{\partial \phi^ {i} \partial \phi^ {j}} \left\{\theta_ {n} ^ {*} (y), \phi_ {n} ^ {*} (y) \right\} + m _ {i j} \rightarrow_ {\mathrm {p}} 0
$$

uniformly over  $y$ . Therefore

$$
\partial f _ {n} / \partial y ^ {i} = - \Sigma_ {j} y ^ {j} m _ {i j} + e _ {n} ^ {i} (y), \tag {5·3}
$$

where  $e_n^i(y) \to_{\mathfrak{p}} 0$  uniformly over  $y$  such that  $|y| = 1$ . It now follows using Lemma 5, as previously, that with probability tending to 1 there exists  $\tilde{\phi}_n$  satisfying  $|\tilde{\phi}_n - \bar{\phi}_n| < \delta_n$ ,  $\partial \tilde{L}_n(X_{n,1}, \tilde{\phi}_n) / \partial \phi^i = 0$  for  $i = 1, \dots, p$ . Since  $\delta_n$  may be chosen so that  $n\delta_n / \log n$  increases arbitrarily slowly, the result of the theorem follows in the case  $\alpha \leqslant 1$ .

The argument for  $\alpha > 1$  is almost identical. Now choose  $\delta_{n} (n \geqslant 1)$  so that  $\delta_{n} \to 0$ ,  $n^{1 / \alpha} \delta_{n} \to \infty$ , and define  $f_{n}$  by (5·2). We have  $\partial^2 \tilde{L}_n / \partial \theta \partial \phi^i \to_{\mathfrak{p}} - m_{0i}$  uniformly on a region, so  $\delta_{n}^{-1}(X_{n,1} - \theta_0) <_{\mathfrak{p}} \delta_n^{-1} n^{-1 / \alpha} \to 0$  and so (5·3) again holds. The rest of the proof is the same.

Proof of Corollary. Consistency follows from the relations  $\bar{\phi}_n - \phi_0 \to_{\mathfrak{p}} 0, \tilde{\phi}_n - \bar{\phi}_n \to_{\mathfrak{p}} 0$ . The results for  $\alpha < 2$  follow by noting that

$$
n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} (\widetilde {\phi} _ {n} - \phi_ {0}) = n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} (\bar {\phi} _ {n} - \phi_ {0}) + n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} (\widetilde {\phi} _ {n} - \bar {\phi} _ {n})
$$

and the last term tends in probability to zero. Therefore the asymptotic distribution of  $n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tilde{\phi}_n - \phi_0)$  is the same as that of  $n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bar{\phi}_n - \phi_0)$ , which is as claimed.

# 6. HYPOTHESIS TESTING

In this section we consider testing hypotheses about  $\theta$ , making first a few remarks about the case  $\phi$  known before turning to the case  $\phi$  unknown. The results given here are of course also relevant to the construction of confidence intervals.

Consider first a simple versus simple test of  $H_0$ :  $\theta = \theta_0$ ,  $\phi = \phi_0$  against  $H_1$ :  $\theta = \theta_n$ ,  $\phi = \phi_0$ , based on sample size  $n$ . The Neyman-Pearson test is to reject  $H_0$  if

$$
n \left\{L _ {n} \left(\theta_ {n}, \phi_ {0}\right) - L _ {n} \left(\theta_ {0}, \phi_ {0}\right) \right\} > c ^ {*} \tag {6·1}
$$

for some critical value  $c^*$ . If  $\alpha < 2$ , the natural choice of  $\theta_n$  is of the form

$$
\theta_ {n} = \theta_ {0} + n ^ {- 1 / \alpha} t, \tag {6·2}
$$

for fixed real  $t$ , since in this case a test of fixed size will have limiting power strictly between 0 and 1.

Now suppose  $\phi_0$  is unknown but estimated by  $\tilde{\phi}_n$ , as defined in §5. The obvious analogue of (6·1) is the test: reject  $H_0$  if

$$
n \left\{L _ {n} \left(\theta_ {n}, \tilde {\phi} _ {n}\right) - L _ {n} \left(\theta_ {0}, \tilde {\phi} _ {n}\right) \right\} > c ^ {*}. \tag {6·3}
$$

To compare (6·1) and (6·3), note that

$$
\begin{array}{l} n \left\{L _ {n} \left(\theta_ {n}, \tilde {\phi} _ {n}\right) - L _ {n} \left(\theta_ {0}, \tilde {\phi} _ {n}\right) - L _ {n} \left(\theta_ {n}, \phi_ {0}\right) + L _ {n} \left(\theta_ {0}, \phi_ {0}\right) \right\} \\ = n (\theta_ {n} - \theta_ {0}) \Sigma_ {j} (\widetilde {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) (\partial^ {2} / \partial \theta \partial \phi^ {j}) L _ {n} (\theta_ {n} ^ {*}, \phi_ {n} ^ {*}) \\ \end{array}
$$

for some  $\theta_n^*$ ,  $\phi_n^*$ . If  $1 < \alpha < 2$  we have

$$
\theta_ {n} - \theta_ {0} = O (n ^ {- 1 / \alpha}), \quad \tilde {\phi} _ {n} - \phi_ {0} <   _ {\mathfrak {p}} n ^ {- \frac {1}{2}}, \quad \partial^ {2} L _ {n} / \partial \theta \partial \phi^ {\jmath} <   _ {\mathfrak {p}} 1,
$$

so the whole expression is  $O_{p}(n^{\frac{1}{2} - 1 / \alpha})$  and thus tends to zero in probability. For  $\alpha = 1$ ,  $\alpha < 1$  we have  $\partial^{2}L_{n} / \partial \theta \partial \phi^{j} < _{\mathfrak{p}}\log n, n^{1 / \alpha - 1}$  respectively, leading to the same conclusion. Therefore the two tests (6-1) and (6-3) are asymptotically equivalent, in the sense that they make the same decision in large samples, provided  $\alpha < 2$ .

Note that this conclusion is false if  $\alpha > 2$ , even if  $\hat{\phi}_n$  is used in place of  $\tilde{\phi}_n$ . In that case, we have  $\theta_n - \theta_0 = O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})$  and hence that  $n(\theta_n - \theta_0)\Sigma_j(\hat{\phi}_n^j -\phi_0^j)\partial^2 L_n / \partial \theta \partial \phi^j$  is  $O_p(1)$  rather than  $o_{p}(1)$ , so that the equivalence of (6·1) and (6·3) fails. When  $\alpha = 2$ , we have

$\tilde{\phi}_n - \phi_0 < _{\mathfrak{p}} n^{-\frac{1}{2}}$  by Theorem 4, but in this case  $\theta_{n} - \theta_{0}$  may be taken to be  $O\{(n\log n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}$ , so that (6-1) and (6-3) are again asymptotically equivalent.

Our conclusion is that for testing a simple hypothesis about  $\theta$  against a simple alternative, ignorance about  $\phi$  makes no difference to the power of the test when  $\alpha \leqslant 2$ , but it decreases the power if  $\alpha > 2$ .

Now consider a test of  $H_0$ :  $\theta = \theta_0$  against the composite alternative  $H_1$ :  $\theta \neq \theta_0$ . For the construction of two-sided confidence intervals, in particular, we wish to consider this case. In regular cases, there are three widely used tests, all of which are to first order asymptotically efficient, namely the Wald test which is based on the asymptotic distribution of the maximum likelihood estimator, the score test which is based on the first derivative of the log likelihood and the likelihood ratio test; see, for example, Cox & Hinkley (1974, Ch. 9). In the nonregular case  $\alpha < 2$ , even when  $\phi$  is known, these tests are not first-order equivalent and it is not known whether any of them has any optimality properties. The following discussion is therefore concerned purely with distributional properties and not with asymptotic optimality.

For the Wald test, the test statistic is the maximum likelihood estimator, assuming it exists. Woodroofoe's (1974) results determine the asymptotic distribution of maximum likelihood estimator in the case  $1 < \alpha < 2$  but this is not easy to work with. For the likelihood ratio test, nothing appears to be known about the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic. For the score test, however, things are rather simpler, since the asymptotic distribution of the score statistic is known and the test does not require computation or even existence of the maximum likelihood estimator. Therefore we concentrate on the score test, believing that this provides at least a viable method of testing hypotheses about  $\theta$  even though it may not have asymptotic optimality properties.

The score static for testing  $H_0$ :  $\theta = \theta_0$  when  $\phi = \phi_0$  is known is

$$
\partial L _ {n} (\theta_ {0}, \phi_ {0}) / \partial \theta = (\alpha - 1) \Sigma (X _ {n, k} - \theta_ {0}) ^ {- 1} n ^ {- 1} + \Sigma \left\{\partial \log g (X _ {n, k} - \theta_ {0}; \phi_ {0}) / \partial \theta_ {0} \right\} n ^ {- 1}.
$$

When  $\alpha \leqslant 2$  the second term is  $O_{p}(1)$  and the first tends to  $\infty$ , but  $E(\partial L_{n} / \partial \theta) = 0$  when  $\alpha > 1$ , by Assumption 6. Hence  $\partial L_{n} / \partial \theta$  has the same asymptotic distribution as  $n^{-1}\{S_{n,1} - E(S_{n,1})\}$  when  $\alpha > 1$ ,  $n^{-1}S_{n,1}$  when  $\alpha \leqslant 1$ , and this asymptotic distribution is stable, by Lemma 1. In particular, when  $1 < \alpha < 2$  or  $\alpha < 1$ ,  $n^{1 - 1 / \alpha}\partial L_{n} / \partial \theta$  has a nondegenerate stable limit law.

A two-sided test is therefore defined by the acceptance region

$$
a <   n \left\{a _ {n} ^ {- 1} \partial L _ {n} \left(\theta , \phi_ {0}\right) / \partial \theta - b _ {n} \right\} <   b, \tag {6·4}
$$

where  $a_{n}, b_{n}$  are as in Lemma 1 and the percentage points  $a, b$  are determined by the limiting stable law. For  $\alpha = 2$ , the acceptance region is

$$
a <   n ^ {\frac {1}{2}} \left(c \log n\right) ^ {- \frac {1}{2}} \partial L _ {n} \left(\theta , \phi_ {0}\right) / \partial \theta <   b \tag {6·5}
$$

with  $a, b$  the appropriate percentage points of the standard normal distribution.

Now let us consider the effect of  $\phi$  being unknown, and estimated by  $\tilde{\phi}_n$ . When  $1 < \alpha < 2$  we have

$$
n ^ {1 - 1 / \alpha} \{\partial L _ {n} (\theta , \widetilde {\phi} _ {n}) / \partial \theta - \partial L _ {n} (\theta , \phi_ {0}) / \partial \theta \} = n ^ {1 - 1 / \alpha} \Sigma_ {j} (\widetilde {\phi} _ {n} ^ {j} - \phi_ {0} ^ {j}) \partial^ {2} L _ {n} (\theta , \phi_ {n} ^ {*}) / \partial \theta \partial \phi^ {j},
$$

with  $\phi_{n}^{*}$  between  $\tilde{\phi}_{n}$  and  $\phi_0$ . But  $\partial^2 L_n / \partial \theta \partial \phi^i < _\mathrm{p}1$  uniformly on a region of the form  $|\phi -\phi_0| = o_p(1)$ ,  $|\theta -\theta_0| = o_p(n^{-2 / \alpha +1})$ , so the whole expression  $\rightarrow_{\mathfrak{p}}0$  uniformly on a region of form  $|\theta -\theta_0| = O(n^{-1 / \alpha})$ . Similar arguments show that the same result holds for

$0 < \alpha < 1, \alpha = 1$  and  $\alpha = 2$ . We therefore conclude that the tests (6.4)-(6.5) remain valid when  $\phi_0$  is unknown, provided that  $\phi_0$  is replaced by its estimated value  $\tilde{\phi}_n$  or by some other  $\sqrt{n}$ -consistent estimator.

As previously remarked, no claim of optimality is made for these procedures. In one-parameter problems,  $\theta$  unknown,  $\phi$  known, the problem of constructing asymptotically optimal procedures, when  $\alpha < 2$ , is considered in detail in Chapters 5 and 6 of Ibragimov & Has'minskii (1981). These results are complicated and depend on the loss function.

# 7. APPLICATIONS IN EXTREME VALUE THEORY

The results of this paper may be applied to two particular distributions which are of importance in the analysis of extreme values. These are the generalized extreme value distribution, which includes the three-parameter Weibull as a special case, and the generalized Pareto distribution introduced by Pickands (1975).

The density function of the generalized extreme value distribution is

$$
g (y; k, \mu , \sigma) = \sigma^ {- 1} \{1 - k \sigma^ {- 1} (y - \mu) \} ^ {1 / k - 1} \exp \left[ - \{1 - k \sigma^ {- 1} (y - \mu) \} ^ {1 / k} \right] \qquad (7 \cdot 1)
$$

defined on the set  $\{y: k(y - \mu) < \sigma\}$ . In the case  $k > 0$ , this is a reparameterization of the Weibull distribution, while the cases  $k < 0$ ,  $k = 0$ , defined by taking the limit as  $k \to 0$  in (7.1), correspond to the type II and type I extreme value laws, in Gumbel's (1958) characterization. For computational details, see Prescott & Walden (1980, 1983). Recent reviews on the fitting of extreme value distributions and discrimination among types have been given by Mann (1984) and Tiago de Oliveira (1984).

For  $k > 0$ , the results of this paper are directly applicable, and show in particular that the classical properties of maximum likelihood estimators hold for  $0 < k < \frac{1}{2}$ , but not for  $k \geqslant \frac{1}{2}$ . The information matrix is finite over the whole range  $-\infty < k < \frac{1}{2}$ . For  $k < 0$ , the range of the distribution again depends on the unknown parameters, the density being positive when  $y > \mu + \sigma / k$ . Writing  $\beta = -1 / k$ ,  $\theta = \mu + \sigma / k$  gives the reparameterization

$$
g (y; \beta , \theta , \sigma) = \sigma^ {- 1} \{(y - \theta) / (\beta \sigma) \} ^ {- \beta - 1} \exp \left[ - \{(y - \theta) / (\beta \sigma) \} ^ {- \beta} \right] (y > \theta),
$$

which converges to zero faster than any power of  $y - \theta$  as  $y \downarrow \theta$ . Although this does not fall within the scope of our Theorem 1, the arguments that were applied there to the case  $\alpha > 2$  are applicable here also, and show the existence of a maximum likelihood estimator which is asymptotically normal and efficient. We conclude that the classical asymptotic properties of maximum likelihood estimators hold throughout the range  $-\infty < k < \frac{1}{2}$ , while for  $k \geqslant \frac{1}{2}$  the results for the three-parameter Weibull are directly applicable.

We remark that this argument applies also to the three-parameter log normal distribution. In this case also, the likelihood function is unbounded but there is a local maximum which is asymptotically normal and efficient. This observation provides a theoretical justification for the procedures advocated by Griffiths (1980). Another distribution for which similar arguments hold is the inverse Gaussian distribution with unknown location (Cheng & Amin, 1981).

The generalized Pareto distribution has density

$$
g (y; k, \sigma) = \sigma^ {- 1} (1 - k y / \sigma) ^ {1 / k - 1}, \tag {7·2}
$$

defined on the set  $y > 0, ky < \sigma$ . Its importance in extreme value theory arises from the fact that it characterizes the limiting distributions of the excesses over a threshold, as the threshold tends to infinity (Pickands, 1975; Davison, 1984; Smith, 1984). When

$k < 0$ , it is the same as the Pareto distribution, and the limit  $k \to 0$  gives the exponential distribution with mean  $\sigma$ . For  $k > 0$ , the density is a transformation of the three-parameter beta distribution of §1, with  $\beta = 1$ . Thus we get analogous results to those for the generalized extreme value distribution: for  $k < \frac{1}{2}$  the information matrix is finite and the classical asymptotic theory of maximum likelihood estimators is applicable, while for  $k \geq \frac{1}{2}$  the problem is nonregular and special procedures are needed.

An alternative approach was taken by Hall (1982), who considered the estimation of  $\theta$  when the density is of the form  $(1\cdot 1)$ , but without assuming a specific parametric form for  $f_0$ . Hall proposed a procedure based on a specified number of order statistics; his procedure is almost the same as restricting attention to observations beneath a threshold, and assuming the generalized Pareto distribution for the differences between these observations and the threshold. Hall proves asymptotic normality of his estimators in the case  $\alpha \geqslant 2$ . The strength of his approach is that it also takes into account the error in  $(1\cdot 1)$ , but he essentially assumes that this error is known, which is not a realistic practical assumption.

In conclusion, there are many open questions. Even when  $\alpha > 2$ , not all the higher-order moments of the score statistic are finite and it remains an open question whether arguments based on higher-order asymptotics, e.g. Efron & Hinkley (1978), Barndorff-Nielsen (1983), are applicable. Mann (1984) has reviewed a number of papers in which authors have reported practical difficulties in estimating the parameters of the three-parameter Weibull. This raises questions of the speed of convergence, and small-sample properties of the estimators, which we have not considered at all. The problem of determining asymptotically efficient tests and estimators of  $\theta$  when  $\alpha < 2$  is also unresolved. Finally, there is the question of the asymptotic performance of Bayes estimators; in the case  $\alpha < 2$  we would not expect asymptotic normality (Dawid, 1970) to hold, and it would be of interest to establish under what conditions the asymptotic posterior distribution does not depend on the prior distribution.
