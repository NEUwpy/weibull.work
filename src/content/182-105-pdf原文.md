# Maximum Likelihood Estimation in the 3-parameter Weibull Distribution A Look through the Generalized Extreme-value Distribution

Hideo Hirose

Faculty of Information Sciences

Hiroshima City University, Asaminami, Hiroshima

Japan

## ABSTRACT

The main contribution of this paper is to provide reasonable confidence intervals for maximum likelihood estimates of percentile points in dielectric breakdown voltage probability distributions. The Weibull distributions which include threshold values are often used in breakdown voltage distributions. In some breakdown voltage samples, there exist cases in which the maximum likelihood estimates of all the three Weibull parameters diverge; these cases, in limiting forms, correspond to Gumbel distributions. However, computing confidence intervals of the percentile point estimates using the observed information matrix might be impossible when the distribution models are assumed to be the Weibull type. For such cases, adoption of the generalized extreme-value distribution as an extension of the Weibull distribution allows us to assess this troublesome problem. A Monte Carlo simulation using the newly developed generalized extreme-value distribution parameter estimation code reveals the property of the percentile point estimates for extreme-value type distributions, when the Weibull shape parameter is large. Biases and root mean squared errors of percentile point estimates are investigated both for the maximum likelihood estimates and for some closed form estimates; maximum likelihood estimates provide reasonable confidence intervals. For convenience, a demonstrative example with an estimation algorithm is provided.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

FOR short-term dielectric breakdown voltage assessment, the Weibull probability distributions often have been used [2-4, 13, 18, 29], and recently the inclusion of the non-negative thresholds to the Weibull distributions have been discussed vigorously [2-4, 18, 29]. The term 'threshold' means the location parameter in the 3-parameter Weibull distribution (W3P), and it is not directly relevant to the (long term) life threshold below which breakdown will not occur in this paper. Thus, dealing with the W3P has been becoming more and more important.

It is well known that maximum likelihood parameter estimations in the W3P are tedious and many researchers have long been pursuing algorithms which find the estimates $[11, 23, 32]$ . The probability distribution function of the Weibull distribution is expressed by

$$
\begin{array}{c} F ^ {1} (x; \eta , \beta , \gamma) = 1 - \exp \left\{- \left(x - \frac {\gamma}{\eta}\right) ^ {\beta} \right\} \\ x \geq \gamma , \quad \eta > 0, \quad \beta > 0 \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $\eta$ , $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are called scale, shape and location parameters, respectively. In estimating the maximum likelihood estimates (MLE) of the three parameters, two difficulties arise in the W3P: one is the 'non-regular problem' and the other is the 'parameter diverging problem'.

Non-regular cases occur when $\beta \leqslant 2$ . There are no MLE when $0 < \beta < 1$ ; when $1 \leqslant \beta \leqslant 2$ , MLE exist but are not asymptotically normal [8, 27]. Thus, under such a non-regular condition, maximum likelihood estimation is treated anomalously in the distribution. In order to avoid the difficulty, a variety of methods have been proposed. Among them are the modified moment estimation [6, 7]; modified maximum likelihood estimation [6]; and closed form methods [16, 21, 22, 30–32, 34].

Another annoying property of the maximum likelihood estimation in the W3P is the parameter diverging problem $[5, 11]$ . In some dielectric breakdown voltage tests, the MLE of the Weibull three parameters diverge in the Newton-Raphson process, while the log-likelihood tends to converge. This phenomenon is called the 'parameter diverging problem' in this paper. The probability distributions in such cases are seen to be negatively skewed. This paper focuses on this problem. Hirose $[11]$ proposes the use of the 'limiting percentile point estimate' $\widehat{x}_p(\eta^{(j)},\beta^{(j)}\gamma^{(j)})_{(j\to \infty)}$ , in the Newton-Raphson process, and not the parameter estimate itself for such cases; $x_{p}$ is expressed in (2), and $\eta^{(j)},\beta^{(j)}$ and $\gamma^{(j)}$ denote the $j$ th values in the Newton-Raphson iterations, while $p$ is the failure probability.

This limiting value corresponds to the percentile point estimate in the 2-parameter Gumbel distribution (G2P). Cheng and Iles [5] also recommend the use of the G2P which are called the 'embedded models' associated with the 3-parameter distribution models; in their example, however, the estimated location parameter is negative and not diverging to $-\infty$ . They go on to discuss which model between the original 3-parameter model (in their case, $\widehat{\gamma} < 0$ ) and the embedded model should be used by using various hypothesis tests. However, there are highly negatively skewed samples in some experimental cases, for which other distribution models might be more appropriately fitted than the embedded model. The data case 4 in Table 1 and Figure 1 (histograms for Table 1) shows such a case. The coefficient of skewness $\sqrt{\beta_1} = -1.501$ for that case (see Table 1); in the Gumbel distribution, $\sqrt{\beta_1} = -1.14$ [14]. This leads us to introduce an extension of the Weibull distribution to the extreme-value distribution family, i.e. the generalized extreme-value distribution (GEV).

The adoption of the GEV has other benefits. One is that the maximum likelihood parameter estimation in the GEV is easier than in the W3P, which will be discussed in Section 5. References [28] and [5] are relevant to the idea of the parameterization of the GEV model from the W3P model; however, they do not show that using the GEV is effective in estimating the Weibull parameters.

The proposed algorithm is an extension of his algorithm. For all cases in the Monte Carlo simulation, this algorithm obtained the MLE of Weibull parameters under the condition that $\beta \geq 1$ . Secondly, the root mean squared errors (RMSE) can reasonably be simulated when the Weibull shape parameter is large, i.e. the GEV shape parameter is close to zero. The confidence interval of the percentile point estimate cannot be computed by using the observed information matrix (Appendix 2) in the W3P, when Weibull's three parameters diverge. The Monte Carlo simulation supports the use of the percentile point estimate obtained from the GEV model.

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL DATA IN DIELECTRIC BREAKDOWN VOLTAGE TESTS

Table 1 shows the dielectric breakdown voltages of one hundred epoxy resin test pieces which were tested in the Takaoka Electric Laboratory [11]; but it should be noted that some experiments (e.g. for a generator) are very expensive, so such a large number of test pieces is unusual. Actually, twenty test pieces were tested by applying an ascending voltage to each in turn, and this was repeated five times. Thus, we have five sets of data for the same underlying distribution. Since failure data often fit the Weibull distribution including a certain threshold, it was attempted to obtain the MLE of the 3-parameter Weibull distribution for the five data sets. The computing results using the method described in [11] are shown in Table 2. In the Table, the MLE for 100 data are also provided in the right-hand column. We see that the first and fourth data sets have infinite MLE of parameters but have finite (limiting) log-likelihood and MLE of the percentile point $x_{p}$ . Figure 2 shows a trace of the Newton-Raphson process for data case 4. In connection with the diverging phenomena in case 1 and 4, it can be seen that highly negatively skewed cases correspond to parameter diverging patterns.

How should we obtain the confidence intervals for the estimates of the percentile point for such cases?

## 3. REPARAMETERIZATION TO THE GEV MODEL

As mentioned in Sections 1 and 2, there exist cases that the MLE of the parameters in the W3P diverge. In such cases, it seems that $\eta^{(j)}/\beta^{(j)}$ and $\eta^{(j)} + \gamma^{(j)}$ are both converging [11]. These phenomena correspond to data cases 1 and 4 in this paper. The convergence of transformed parameters reminds us of the generalized extreme-value distribution (GEV) which was derived by von Mises [19]. Using the following reparameterization

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sigma = \frac {\eta}{\beta} \\ \mu = \eta + \gamma \\ k = \frac {1}{\beta} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

the probability distribution function of the GEV is expressed by

$$
\begin{array}{c} F (x; \sigma , \mu , k) = 1 - \exp \left[ - \left\{1 + k \left(x - \frac {\mu}{\sigma}\right) \right\} ^ {1 / k} \right] \\ \sigma > 0 \\ 1 + k \left(x - \frac {\mu}{\sigma}\right) > 0 \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

when $k \neq 0$ . The Weibull distribution in (1) corresponds to the case k > 0. The cases $k \rightarrow 0$ and k < 0 correspond to the Gumbel (5), and Fréchet in (6) distributions, respectively.

$$
\begin{array}{c} F ^ {2} (x; \sigma , \mu) = 1 - \exp \left\{- \exp \left(x - \frac {\mu}{\sigma}\right) \right\} \\ \sigma > 0 \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} F ^ {3} (x; \eta , \beta , \gamma) = 1 - \exp \left\{- \left(\gamma - \frac {x}{\eta}\right) ^ {- \beta} \right\} \\ x \leq \gamma \qquad \eta > 0 \qquad \beta > 0 \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

Distributions in (1), (5) and (6) are called extreme-value distributions for the minimum value which were first discovered by Fisher and Tippett [9], and the extreme types theorem was later completely proved by Gnedenko [1, 10]. Historically, the GEV has been mainly treated as a distribution representing the extreme values for maxima [15]; here the extreme-value distributions for minima are discussed.

If we fit the GEV to the fourth data case in Table 1, we can obtain the finite MLE as shown in Table 3. It should be noted that the corresponding value of the maximum log-likelihood ( $\log L_{GEV} = -27.444$ ) is larger than that obtained by the limiting form of the Weibull (i.e. the Gumbel) distribution $\log L_{\mathbf{W3P}}(\beta \rightarrow \infty) = \log L_{\mathbf{G2P}} = -27.613$ . The estimates of the GEV parameters are $\widehat{\sigma} = 0.7783$ , $\widehat{\mu} = 28.032$ , $\widehat{k} = -0.0811$ . Although the standard deviation of the percentile point estimate could not be computed in the Weibull model, the GEV model obtains it because the regularity condition holds when k < 1/2 [27]. ## 4. MONTE CARLO SIMULATION

The conditions of the Monte Carlo simulation are as follows. The numbers of sample size n = 100, 50 and 20. The Weibull shape parameter $\beta = 2, 3, 5, 8$ and 100 with the corresponding GEV shape parameter k = 0.5, 0.333, 0.2, 0.125 and 0.01. The Weibull scale and location parameters are $\beta$ and $-\beta$ , e.g. $\sigma = 1$ and $\mu = 0$ , respectively. In each case, the number of trials is 1000. The percentile points investigated are $x_{0.01}, x_{0.05}, x_{0.1}, x_{0.5}, x_{0.9}, x_{0.95}$ , and $x_{0.99}$ .

The cases that the log-likelihood has the maximum value at the corner, k = 1 or $\beta = 1$ , under the condition, $k \leq 1$ or $\beta \geq 1$ , are not considered for assessing the biases and RMSE in this simulation study, but they would not affect the results much because the occurrence ratio of such cases is small except for the case of n = 20 and $\beta = 2$ , as shown in Table 4.

Comparisons of the estimates of parameters and percentile points are made between the MLE and the WBE, because Hirose [11] mentions that Wyckoff, Bain and Engelhardt's method [31] provides better estimates with respect to bias and RMSE. The WBE of the Weibull parameters are transformed to the estimates in the GEV model for the sake of comparison.

## 5.2. DERIVATION OF THE EXTREME-VALUE TYPE DISTRIBUTIONS

It is well known that the Weibull model is connected to the weak link-chain breakage problem. In addition, the Weibull distribution is strongly related to the other two types of extreme-value distributions, the Gumbel and the Fréchet distributions.

A brief description of the derivation of the extreme-value distribution is explained as follows [17]. If $x_{(1)} \leqslant x_{(2)} \leqslant \cdots \leqslant x_{(n)}$ are n samples from a cumulative distribution function $(cdf)F(x)$ , then $G_{n}(x) = F^{n}(x)$ becomes a idf associated with $x_{(n)}$ which is the maximum value in n samples. Assume that n = km and $m, n \to \infty$ with k fixed. If $x_{(n)}$ has the limiting form when $n \to \infty$ under such a condition, the function $G(x) = G_{n}(x)$ should follow (7).

$$
G ^ {k} (x) = G \left(a _ {k} x + b _ {k}\right)\tag{7}
$$

From this functional equation, three types of asymptotic cdf are derived as follows.

Gumbel type

$$
G (x) = \exp [ - \exp \{- n F ^ {\prime} (x _ {o n}) (x - x _ {o n}) \}\tag{8}
$$

Fréchet type

$$
G (x) = \exp [ - (x / x _ {o n}) ^ {- k} ]\tag{9}
$$

Weibull type

$$
G (x) = \exp [ - \left\{\left(- n F ^ {(k)} (x _ {u}) / k!\right) ^ {1 / k} (x - x _ {u}) \right\} ^ {k} ]\tag{10}
$$

## 6. CONCLUDING REMARKS

For some experimental samples in electrical engineering, there exist cases in which the MLE of Weibull's three parameters diverge. This paper considered the treatment of confidence intervals of the percentile point estimates for such cases. Adoption of the generalized extreme-value distribution when the Weibull shape parameter is large proved to provide reasonable RMSE for the MLE of percentile points from the Monte Carlo simulation study. In addition, the paper showed the newly developed effective maximum likelihood parameter estimation scheme in the generalized extreme-value distribution model, and the maximum likelihood estimation of parameters are successfully obtained under conditions in which the Weibull shape parameter is $\geqslant$ 1.0.

## 7. APPENDIX 1

## 7.1. MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD PARAMETER ESTIMATION IN THE GEV MODEL

The main idea of the proposed algorithm lies on a assumption that $\widehat{\sigma}(k)$ and $\widehat{\mu}(k)$ do not vary as much as k does; $\widehat{\sigma}(k)$ and $\widehat{\mu}(k)$ denote the MLE for the GEV distribution when k is fixed. Moreover, when $k \to 0$ , which corresponds to the Gumbel distribution, we can easily estimate the MLE of parameters in the Gumbel distribution by using the Newton-Raphson method. Since the MLE of parameters when using the Newton-Raphson method are very sensitive in general with respect to the initial guesses, the following method is one of the recommended methods for a stable and successful searching scheme of the MLE.

Step 1: Obtain the sample's mean and standard deviation as follows

$$
m = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} x _ {i}\tag{11}
$$

$$
s ^ {2} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (x _ {i} - m) ^ {2}\tag{12}
$$

where n denotes the number of samples. Set initial guesses, $\sigma_{0}^{(0)}$ and $\mu_{0}^{(0)}$ , for the MLE of $\widehat{\mu}(G2P)$ and $\widehat{\sigma}(G2P)$ as

$$
\sigma_ {0} ^ {(0)} = \sqrt {\frac {6}{\pi}} s = 0. 7 7 9 6 9 7 s\tag{13}
$$

$$
\mu_ {0} ^ {(0)} = m + 0. 5 7 7 2 1 6 \sigma\tag{14}
$$

Obtain the MLE of G2P using $\sigma_0^{(0)}$ and $\mu_0^{(0)}$ . Set $\sigma_0 = \widehat{\sigma} (\mathrm{G2P})$ , $\mu_0 = \widehat{\mu} (\mathrm{G2P})$ , and $k_{0} = 0$ .

Step 2: Define a small number $\Delta k > 0$ , say $\Delta k = 0.01$ . Set $k_{1} = \Delta k$ , and $k_{-1} = -\Delta k$ . Obtain the MLE of the 2-parameter GEV distribution under the constraint of $k = k_{1}$ , by using $\sigma_0$ and $\mu_0$ as initial guesses. Set these MLE as $\sigma_1$ and $\mu_1$ . Obtain $\sigma_{-1}$ and $\mu_{-1}$ corresponding to $k_{-1}$ in a very similar manner.

Step 3: By using the three points, $(\sigma_{-1}, k_{-1})$ , $(\sigma_0, k_0)$ , and $(\sigma_1, k_1)$ , obtain an extrapolated point $(\sigma_2^{(0)}, k_2)$ by solving a quadratic equation, where $k_2 = k_1 + \Delta k$ (Figure 7). Obtain $(\mu_2^{(0)}, k_2)$ in a similar manner. By using $(\sigma_2^{(0)}, \mu_2^{(0)})$ as initial guesses, obtain the MLE, $\sigma_2$ and $\mu_2$ of the 2-parameter GEV distribution under the constraint of $k = k_2$ . Assuming that $(\sigma_{\nu-2}, \mu_{\nu-2})$ , $(\sigma_{\nu-1}, \mu_{\nu-1})$ and $(\sigma_{\nu}, \mu_{\nu})$ have already been obtained by the previous steps, then $\sigma_{\nu+1}$ and $\mu_{\nu+1}$ for $k_{\nu+1} = k_{\nu} + \Delta k$ are obtained by the Newton-Raphson method using $\sigma_{\nu+1}^{(0)}$ and $\mu_{\nu+1}^{(0)}$ as the initial values (Figure 7). $\sigma_{\nu+1}^{(0)}$ is an extrapolated value from $(\sigma_{\nu-2}, k_{\nu-2})$ , $(\sigma_{\nu-1}, k_{\nu-1})$ and $(\sigma_{\nu}, k_{\nu})$ ; $\mu_{\nu+1}^{(0)}$ is similarly obtained. If the constraint, $\sigma_{\nu+1}^{(j)} > 0$ or $1 + k_{\nu+1}^{(j)} \{(x_i - \mu_{\nu+1}^{(j)}) / \sigma_{\nu+1}^{(j)})\} > 0$ , is violated in the Newton-Raphson iteration, we use an alternative extrapolated point which is closer to the point $(\sigma_{\nu}, \mu_{\nu}, k_{\nu})$ ; $j$ denotes the Newton-Raphson iteration count for the point $(k_{\nu+1}, \sigma_{\nu+1}, \mu_{\nu+1})$ . If $\Delta k$ is small, then convergence would be confirmed. This continues until $k = 1 - \Delta k$ . Special treatment is needed at the corner, $k = 1$ , as Rockette et al. have pointed out [26].

Step 4: A similar process to Step 3 is taken in the opposite direction from $k = k_{-2}$ to $k = k^*$ , where usually $k^* = -1$ .

Step 5: Search for a maximum point of $\log L(\sigma(k_{\nu}), \mu(k_{\nu}), k_{\nu})$ from all the knots which we are investigating. If $k_{\nu} \neq 1$ and $k_{\nu} \neq k^{*}$ , then do the 3-parameter Newton-Raphson iteration using the initial values $\sigma_{\nu}, \mu_{\nu}$ and $k_{\nu}$ (if $\nu = 0$ , use the next point $(\sigma_1, \mu_1, k_1)$ ). The converged variables may be the (local) MLE. We must check if this point is not a saddle point by using the determinants of the submatrices formed by the observed information matrix (Appendix 2); the log-likelihood at this point should also be checked if it is larger than the log-likelihood at the corner points, $k = 1$ and $k = k^{*}$ . If $\log L(\sigma(k = 1), \mu(k = 1), 1)$ is still maximum, the MLE of the Weibull parameters are $\widehat{\gamma} = x_{\min}$ , $\widehat{\beta} = 1$ and $\widehat{\eta} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - x_{\min})$ under the constraint that $k \leqslant 1$ ; $x_{\min}$ is the minimum observation in the experiment. If $\log L(\sigma(k^*)$ , $\mu(k^*)$ , $k^*)$ is maximum, $k^*$ should be set much smaller.

In these steps, the first and second derivatives of $\log L$ and Fisher's information matrix are obtained in a similar manner in [24, 25], but the specific formulation is given in Appendix 2 for convenience in programming. As mentioned in the first paragraph of this Appendix, the curve which is constructed by connecting $(\sigma(k_{\nu}), \mu(k_{\nu}), k_{\nu})$

points is shown in Figure 8, for the data in case 4. We can confirm that the location parameter $\mu$ does not change value much with variations in the shape parameter k in the estimation process, which suggests a more stable search for the solution in the GEV space than in the Weibull space.

## 8. APPENDIX 2

## 8.1. NEWTON RAPHSON ALGORITHM

## 8.1.1. GENERAL

Denote the unknown parameters by

$\theta = (\theta_{1}, \theta_{2}, \theta_{3}, \ldots, \theta_{l})^{T}$ , and the log-likelihood function by $\log L(\theta_{1}, \theta_{2}, \theta_{3}, \ldots, \theta_{l})$ . The Hessian matrix of the log-likelihood function is

$$
H (\theta) = \left(\partial^ {2} \log \frac {L}{\partial \theta_ {a}} \partial \theta_ {b}\right)\tag{15}
$$

The matrix $-\left(\partial^{2}\log L / \partial \theta_{a}\partial \theta_{b}\right)$ is called the 'observed information matrix'. Then, the solution of the likelihood equations

$$
\begin{array}{c} \partial \log \frac {L}{\partial \theta_ {a}} = 0 \\ a = 1, 2, \ldots , l \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

can be obtained successively by

$$
\theta^ {(j + 1)} = \theta^ {(j)} - H (\theta^ {(j)}) ^ {- 1} F (\theta^ {(j)})\tag{17}
$$

until $\max |\theta_a^{(j + 1)} - \theta_a^{(j)}| < \varepsilon$ , where

$$
F (\theta) = \left(\partial \log \frac {L}{\partial \theta_ {1}}, \partial \log \frac {L}{\partial \theta_ {2}}, \dots , \partial \log L \partial \theta_ {l}\right) ^ {T}\tag{18}
$$

When using 2-parameter estimation such as the Gumbel distribution and the 2-parameter GEV distribution under the constraint of fixed k, use $\theta_{1}$ and $\theta_{2}$ ; in the case of 3-parameter estimation, use $\theta_{3}$ in addition.

## 8.1.2. HESSIAN MATRIX FORM FOR THE GUMBEL DISTRIBUTION

The log-likelihood function for the Gumbel distribution is

$$
\log L = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\{- \log \mu + z _ {i} - \exp (z _ {i}) \right\}\tag{19}
$$

where $z_{i} = (x_{i} - \mu) / \sigma$ . The function $F(\theta)$ is expressed as

$$
\partial \log \frac {L}{\partial \mu} = - \frac {1}{\sigma} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \{1 - \exp (z _ {i}) \}\tag{20}
$$

$$
\partial \log \frac {L}{\partial \sigma} = - \frac {1}{\sigma} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} [ 1 + z _ {i} \{1 - \exp (z _ {i}) \} ]\tag{21}
$$

Thus, the components of the Hessian matrix are

$$
\partial^ {2} \log L \partial \mu^ {2} = - \frac {1}{\sigma^ {2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \exp (z _ {i})\tag{22}
$$

$$
\partial^ {2} \log \frac {L}{\partial \mu \partial \sigma} = \frac {1}{\sigma^ {2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\{1 - (1 + z _ {i}) \exp (z _ {i}) \right\}\tag{23}
$$

$$
\partial^ {2} \log \frac {L}{\partial \sigma^ {2}} = \frac {1}{\sigma^ {2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \{1 + [ 2 z _ {i} \{1 - \exp (z _ {i}) \} - z _ {i} ^ {2} \exp (z _ {i}) ] \}\tag{24}
$$

## 8.1.3. HESSIAN MATRIX FORM FOR THE GEV DISTRIBUTION

The log-likelihood function for the GEV distribution is

$$
\log L = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (- \log \sigma - \log z _ {i} + \log \phi_ {i} - \phi_ {i})\tag{25}
$$

where $z_{i} = 1 + k\{(x_{i} - \mu) / \sigma\} = 1 + ky_{i}$ , and $\phi_{i} = z_{i}^{1 / k}$ . The function $F(\theta)$ is expressed as

$$
\partial \log \frac {L}{\partial \mu} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{\sigma z _ {i}} \psi_ {i}\tag{26}
$$

$$
\partial \log \frac {L}{\partial \sigma} = - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{\sigma} \left(1 - \frac {y _ {i} \psi_ {i}}{z _ {i}}\right)\tag{27}
$$

$$
\partial \log \frac {L}{\partial k} = - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \frac {1}{k} \left\{\frac {y _ {i} \psi_ {i}}{z _ {i}} + (1 - \phi_ {i}) \log \phi_ {i} \right\}\tag{28}
$$

where $\psi_{i}=k-1+\phi_{i}$

The second derivatives of the likelihood function are complicated to write out. So, only the relevant equations for the chain rule are shown as follows

$$
\partial y _ {i} / \partial \mu = - 1 / \sigma
$$

$$
\partial y _ {i} / \partial \sigma = - y _ {i} / \sigma\tag{29}
$$

$$
\partial y _ {i} / \partial k = 0\tag{30}
$$

$$
\partial z _ {i} / \partial \mu = - k / \sigma\tag{31}
$$

$$
\partial z _ {i} / \partial \sigma = - k y _ {i} / \sigma\tag{32}
$$

(33)

$$
\partial z _ {i} / \partial k = y _ {i}.\tag{34}
$$

$$
\partial \phi_ {i} / \partial \mu = - \phi_ {i} / (\sigma z _ {i})\tag{35}
$$

$$
\partial \phi_ {i} / \partial \sigma = - y _ {i} \phi_ {i} / (\sigma z _ {i})\tag{36}
$$

$$
\partial \phi_ {i} / \partial k = \phi_ {i} (- \log \phi_ {i} + y _ {i} / z _ {i}) / k\tag{37}
$$

$$
\partial \psi_ {i} / \partial \mu = \partial \phi_ {i} / \partial \mu\tag{38}
$$

$$
\partial \psi_ {i} / \partial \sigma = \partial \phi_ {i} / \partial \sigma\tag{39}
$$

$$
\partial \psi_ {i} / \partial k = 1 + \partial \phi_ {i} / \partial k\tag{40}
$$

When using the 2-parameter estimation under the constraint that k is fixed, discard the terms and equations relevant to k.

## 8.1.4. DELTA METHOD

For obtaining the approximate confidence intervals of certain functions of $\widehat{\theta}$ , the delta method is first used because of its simple form. The approximate standard error $e(g(\widehat{\theta}))$ of $g(\widehat{\theta})$ is computed as

$$
e (g (\widehat {\theta})) \approx \sum_ {a} \sum_ {b} \frac {\partial g (\widehat {\theta})}{\partial \theta_ {a}} \frac {\partial g (\widehat {\theta})}{\partial \theta_ {b}} \mathrm{Cov} (\widehat {\theta} _ {a}, \widehat {\theta} _ {b})\tag{41}
$$

where $\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat{\theta}_{a},\widehat{\theta}_{b})$ is the covariance computed from the observed information matrix.

## 9.1. NUMERICAL EXAMPLE IN THE ESTIMATION

A numerical example is demonstrated here for data case 2 in Table 1.

For the Gumbel distribution, m = 27.793 and s = 1.42484; so, $\sigma_{0}^{(0)} = 1.11094$ and $\mu_{0}^{(0)} = 28.4343$ are the first guesses. Then, $\sigma_{0} = \widehat{\sigma}(\mathrm{G2P}) = 1.36428$ , $\mu_{0} = \widehat{\mu}(\mathrm{G2P}) = 28.4911$ are obtained by the Newton-Raphson method.

We set $\Delta k = 0.01$ . Then, $\sigma_{1} = 1.35949$ , $\mu_{1} = 28.4854$ , $\sigma_{-1} = 1.36943$ and $\mu_{-1} = 28.4966$ are estimated corresponding to k = 0.01 and k = -0.01 respectively.

By using three points, $(\sigma_{-1}, k_{-1})$ , $(\sigma_0, k_0)$ and $(\sigma_1, k_1)$ , an extrapolated point $(\sigma_2^{(0)}, k_2) = (1.35507, 0.02)$ is obtained; similarly a point $(\mu_2^{(0)}, k_2) = (28.4797, 0.02)$ is obtained. Then, $(\sigma_2, \mu_2) = (1.35507, 28.4797)$ is estimated. In a similar manner, an opposite point $(\sigma_{-2}, \mu_{-2}) = (1.37493, 28.5020)$ is also estimated by the initial guesses $(\mu_{-2}^{(0)}, \mu_{-2}^{(0)}) = (1.37496, 28.5020)$ .

After obtaining the log-likelihood at the points $(\sigma_{\nu}, \mu_{\nu}, k_{\nu}) (-1 \leqslant k_{\nu} \leqslant 0.99)$ , an optimum grid point $(\sigma_{22}, \mu_{22}, k_{22}) = (1.37688, 28.3321, 0.22)$ is obtained with $\log L = -35.3748$ .

By converting GEV parameters to the Weibull ones, $(\widehat{\eta}, \widehat{\beta}, \widehat{\gamma}) = (6.23894, 4.52906, 22.0924)$ is obtained.
